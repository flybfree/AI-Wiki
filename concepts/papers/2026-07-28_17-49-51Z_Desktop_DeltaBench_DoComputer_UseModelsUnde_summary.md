## Summary  
The paper introduces Desktop‑Delta Bench (DDB), an offline step‑level benchmark that evaluates whether computer‑use agents can correctly interpret the causal desktop GUI transitions generated by their own actions. By focusing on the intermediate “delta” between frames rather than only end‑task success, DDB isolates a critical diagnostic layer that is currently missing from most UI‑grounding benchmarks. The authors demonstrate systematic gaps in model performance across ordering and single‑action settings, revealing that many systems misinterpret transient or decoy transitions as progress. This work therefore provides a targeted set of challenges to improve verification, reliability, and recovery capabilities of desktop CUA systems.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] DDB quantifies the failure modes in state verification, source tracking, and context‑aware control by labeling 2,013 human‑verified GUI transitions across 50 task domains.  
- [Finding 2] The benchmark shows that ordering remains largely unsaturated: exact‑match rates for non‑decoy and decoy instances are only 65.1 % and 65.7 %, respectively, indicating models still copy superficial sequences (e.g., A‑B‑C) rather than understanding causal intent.  
- [Finding 3] Single‑action inference is harder than localization: click detection achieves F1 = 0.96 versus drag at 0.76, while recognized drags are generally well localized, highlighting a mismatch between action family recognition and spatial grounding.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed DDB by extracting step‑level trajectories from novel Linux desktop sessions spanning ~15 applications. Each trajectory is split into two complementary tasks: (i) 463 three‑frame temporal‑ordering instances, including 105 decoy cases that swap a non‑task action between frames; and (ii) 1,550 before‑after pairs derived from five distinct actions plus their payloads. Models are evaluated in 32 ordering configurations and 16 single‑action regimes, with performance measured on exact matches, F1 scores, and error analyses.

## Results  
Across the full suite, the best non‑decoy models achieve a 65.1 % exact match, while decoy detection improves by only 0.6 percentage points, suggesting limited ability to reject irrelevant transitions. When task context is provided (e.g., a known action), decoy identification gains 6.9 pp but non‑decoy exact matches drop 2.2 pp, indicating trade‑offs in model reliance on superficial cues. Single‑action experiments reveal that click detection outperforms drag at F1 = 0.96 vs. 0.76, yet drag localization remains strong, underscoring a gap between family recognition and precise spatial grounding.

## Significance  
DDB fills the diagnostic gap between GUI grounding and final task success, enabling researchers to pinpoint where desktop CUA systems break down. By exposing systematic copying of order patterns and poor action‑family inference, it guides targeted improvements in verification logic, reliability, and recovery strategies for long‑horizon computer‑use agents.
