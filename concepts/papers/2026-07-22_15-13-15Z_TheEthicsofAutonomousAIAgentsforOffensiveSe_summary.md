## Summary  
The paper investigates how autonomous AI agents, powered by large language models (LLMs), are transforming offensive security practices. It argues that these tools introduce three distinct dimensions of indeterminacy—non‑deterministic actions, open‑ended impact, and an indeterminate user population—that collectively enable the industrialization of offensive capability while obscuring moral responsibility. The authors aim to map how ethical attribution diffuses among users, developers, and third parties, and to propose a stratified set of recommendations for governing this dual‑use technology.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Autonomous AI agents exhibit indeterminacy across three independent dimensions: non‑deterministic policy outputs, open‑ended impact, and an uncertain user base.  
- [Finding 2] These dimensions are thematically linked but not logically derivable from one another, creating a complex ethical landscape that is not captured by existing frameworks.  
- [Finding 3] The combination of these properties accelerates the industrialization of offensive security tools, giving attackers short‑term advantages while potentially democratizing defensive practice in the long run.

## Methodology  
The authors employ a conceptual analysis framework that dissects moral attribution into three stakeholder layers—users, tool‑makers, and third parties. They examine how each layer’s control over agency, predictability, and skill influences ethical outcomes. The analysis is grounded in existing cybersecurity ethics literature but extends it to the specific hybrid of offensive AI tools and LLM autonomy.

## Results  
Theoretical modeling shows that the three dimensions of indeterminacy enable rapid scaling of offensive capabilities, producing a cost asymmetry where offense becomes cheaper than defense. Empirical case studies illustrate how incident attribution fails due to non‑deterministic actions, and how skill barriers have lowered dramatically, allowing both novice operators and sophisticated actors to exploit the tools.

## Significance  
This work highlights a critical gap in current AI‑ethics and cybersecurity governance models that were not designed for autonomous offensive agents. By clarifying where moral responsibility is diffused, it underscores the urgency of developing new regulatory and ethical standards to prevent misuse while preserving legitimate security research.
