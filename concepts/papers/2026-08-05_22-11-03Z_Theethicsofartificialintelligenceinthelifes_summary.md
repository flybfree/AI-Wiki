## Summary  
The paper argues that ethical concerns about artificial intelligence in the life sciences are not unique to AI but stem from universal human brain architecture and cultural diversity. It proposes an “architecture of care” institutional framework that shifts governance from mere restraint to ongoing upbringing of AI systems. By linking the global neuronal workspace, reward cycles (wanting‑liking‑satiety), and epigenetic personalization, it seeks a normative basis for ethical AI. The authors outline required institutions and highlight open questions that remain.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Ethical concerns are not special to artificial intelligence; they arise from the human brain’s structure and socialisation.  
- [Finding 2] Although the computational architecture of the brain is universal, its content is culturally modulated through epigenetic processes.  
- [Finding 3] Governance should be an “architecture of care” that upraises AI rather than merely restrains it.

## Methodology  
The authors employ a theoretical synthesis that draws on neuroscience (global neuronal workspace), affective theory (the wanting‑liking‑satiety reward cycle), and cultural anthropology to construct an interdisciplinary model. They map these biological principles onto AI design, identifying institutional roles that embody the proposed architecture of care.

## Results  
Their architecture of care proposes three institutional pillars: Neuroethical Oversight Boards, Culturally Responsive Development Labs, and Upbringing Protocols for AI training. The framework links reward cycles to continuous learning rather than maximisation and uses epigenetic personalization to tailor AI outputs to cultural contexts.

## Significance  
This work reframes AI ethics as a continuation of human neuroethics, offering a scalable, culturally sensitive governance model that could prevent harmful applications while fostering beneficial ones in life sciences. It bridges biology, culture, and policy for responsible innovation.
