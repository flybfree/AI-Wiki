## Summary  
The paper argues that regulating autonomous and agentic AI requires new governance models because existing assumptions about knowledge control are obsolete. It investigates four regulatory regimes—UK platforms, data protection, UK financial services, and the EU AI Act’s cross‑sectoral regime—to understand the challenges posed by AI autonomy and propose solutions. The authors aim to shift regulation from a reactive process of supervisory oversight to an active, adaptive framework that can anticipate systemic risks. Their contribution is a comparative analysis that identifies key gaps in current law and suggests actionable reforms for future governance.

## Key Contributions  
- Autonomous AI’s opacity undermines traditional supervisory oversight mechanisms.  
- Cross‑sectoral risk pathways necessitate integrated regulatory design rather than siloed rules.  
- Proactive, forward‑looking governance can mitigate emergent systemic risks that reactive oversight cannot address.

## Methodology  
The authors conduct a doctrinal and policy analysis of existing legal frameworks, mapping their applicability to autonomous AI systems. They employ a comparative case‑study approach across the four regulatory regimes identified in the abstract, identifying gaps and proposing concrete reform proposals. This method combines textual review with systematic gap analysis to generate actionable insights.

## Results  
The comparative analysis reveals that UK platform regulation lacks mechanisms for algorithmic autonomy, leaving oversight reactive; data protection focuses on personal data but not AI decision logic; financial services oversight is largely retrospective; the EU AI Act’s cross‑sectoral regime offers the most robust structure with mandatory impact assessments and shared governance bodies. The study suggests integrating AI risk registers, proactive impact assessments, and collaborative regulatory bodies as practical solutions.

## Significance  
This work highlights that regulatory inertia will exacerbate risks as AI agents act autonomously, underscoring the need for a paradigm shift toward proactive, system‑wide oversight to protect public interest and ensure accountability. By demonstrating how current regimes fall short, the paper provides a roadmap for regulators to adapt and evolve with autonomous AI.
