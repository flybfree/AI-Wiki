## Summary  
The paper investigates trust as a subjunctive epistemic state and examines three representational strategies—lexicographic assumption (ordinal closeness in a Lewis–Stalnaker sphere), strong belief in conditional probability, and an analysis of common‑trust fixed points—to understand how betrayal manifests differently under each. It shows that while finite‑depth common trust is realizable, the completed common‑trust fixed point poses difficulties; these difficulties are captured by three distinct findings about how betrayal affects each representation.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: The lexicographic (assumption) representation yields a level ordering of agents that is refuted by a concrete betrayal.  
- Finding 2: The ordinal closeness representation contaminates the closeness ordering proportionally to the betrayer's deliberateness.  
- Finding 3: Under the strong‑belief representation, betrayal merely shifts the operative conditioning hypothesis while leaving her belief structure coherent.

## Methodology  
The authors model trust as a subjunctive epistemic state and develop three representations: (1) a lexicographic assumption within a Lewis–Stalnaker sphere system that treats trust as ordinal closeness; (2) a conditional‑probability framework that treats trust as strong belief; and (3) an analysis of the common‑trust fixed point. They examine how betrayal interacts with each representation, testing whether the Brandenburger–Keisler impossibility for common belief survives when rationality is replaced by an assumption of trustworthiness.

## Results  
Finite‑depth common trust is realizable across all three models; the completed fixed point is problematic. The lexicographic model shows loss of level ordering after betrayal, the ordinal model shows closeness distortion that depends on how deliberated the betrayer is, and the strong‑belief model only reconfigures conditioning hypotheses without breaking belief coherence.

## Significance  
The study clarifies how different epistemic representations capture trust and betrayal, offering insights into AI alignment where trust is a key coordination mechanism. It also bridges modal logic, common‑belief theory, and decision theory by showing that the nature of difficulty differs across these frameworks.
