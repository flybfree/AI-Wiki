## Summary
This paper introduces the Authorship-Rewriting Benchmark (ARB), a novel dataset designed to evaluate the robustness of AI-text detectors against sophisticated rewriting attacks that preserve human authorship characteristics. The authors address a critical gap in current evaluation methodologies by demonstrating that standard benchmarks, which compare direct human text against direct LLM generation, fail to predict detector performance when human content is modified by an LLM. By constructing matched pairs of texts across different generation and rewriting conditions, the study reveals significant vulnerabilities in existing detection systems. The findings indicate that while detectors are generally robust to LLM-to-LLM rewriting, they suffer catastrophic performance drops when detecting human-authored text that has been rewritten by an AI, challenging the reliability of current security measures against advanced adversarial attacks.

## Key Contributions
- **Introduction of ARB Dataset**: The authors present a new benchmark comprising 1,800 source texts from three diverse datasets (XSum, WritingPrompts, OpenWebText) processed through four open-weight LLMs to create four distinct text variants: human-written, direct LLM generation, human-to-LLM rewritten, and LLM-to-LLM rewritten.
- **Performance Disparity Revelation**: The study uncovers a massive performance gap where detectors maintain high recall for direct LLM text but fail dramatically (dropping 60-78 percentage points) when detecting human text that has been rewritten by an LLM, proving that conventional benchmarks do not generalize to this specific threat model.
- **Robustness of LLM-to-LLM Rewriting**: The research demonstrates that detectors remain largely stable when LLM-generated text is further rewritten by the same or different models, highlighting that the primary vulnerability lies in the transition from human to AI authorship rather than within pure AI generation pipelines.

## Methodology
The authors constructed the ARB dataset by selecting 600 texts each from XSum, WritingPrompts, and OpenWebText as source materials. They utilized four open-weight generators: Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, and Gemma-2-9B. For each source item, they generated four matched variants: the original human-written text (HUMAN), text directly generated by an LLM (Free-LLM), human text rewritten by an LLM (H2L), and LLM-generated text rewritten by an LLM (LLM2L). They then evaluated five prominent detectors—FastDetectGPT, Binoculars-falcon-7b, RADAR, BERT-Defense, and RoBERTa-Defense—at a strict operating point of 1% false positive rate to measure true positive recall across all four regimes.

## Results
The experimental results show that FastDetectGPT and Binoculars-falcon-7b achieved high detection rates (91.2% and 93.5%) for direct LLM text but plummeted to 30.8% and 15.1% respectively when detecting H2L texts, a decline of 60-78 percentage points. In contrast, these detectors retained high recall (78.3% and 83.0%) on LLM2L texts, indicating stability within AI-generated domains. RADAR showed a similar pattern, dropping from 66.8% to 12.2% for H2L. Notably, BERT-Defense and RoBERTa-Defense performed poorly across all categories, maintaining less than 3% recall, suggesting they are ineffective against both direct generation and rewriting attacks.

## Significance
This research is significant because it exposes a critical blind spot in AI-text detection: the assumption that detectors validated on standard human-vs-LLM benchmarks will perform well against adversarial rewriting of human content is fundamentally flawed. This implies that current security tools may offer false confidence against attackers who use LLMs to disguise human writing as AI-generated or vice versa, necessitating new evaluation standards and more robust detection algorithms for real-world applications.
