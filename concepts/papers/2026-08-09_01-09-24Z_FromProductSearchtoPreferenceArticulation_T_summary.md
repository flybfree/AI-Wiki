## Summary  
The paper investigates how generative AI transforms digital commerce from passive browsing to **agentic search**, where users delegate product discovery to AI agents. It introduces the concept of *preference complexity*—the number of satisfaction‑relevant dimensions that are hard to articulate before inspection but easy to evaluate after. By modeling consumer utility as a trade‑off between attention spent on manual inspection and refinement depth with an agent, the authors show that manual search collapses at a finite complexity threshold while agentic search persists with rising complexity. The study derives three key findings about revenue, adoption lag, and fidelity allocation.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Manual search collapses beyond a finite complexity threshold: inspection ceases, mismatch reaches the no‑search benchmark, and platform revenue falls to zero; agentic search avoids this collapse because refinement remains worthwhile as complexity rises.  
- **Finding 2:** Platforms rank regimes by conversion revenue, yet consumers also bear search expenditure; when manual inspection is sufficiently cheap, agentic search becomes revenue‑superior before consumers voluntarily adopt it, creating an adoption lag in which users rationally continue manual search.  
- **Finding 3:** Conditional on agentic participation, platforms may assign lower fidelity to consumers with larger attention budgets because those users can offset noisier representations through additional refinement, yielding an inverted fidelity allocation.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a utility‑maximization model for each consumer that balances the cost of search effort against the benefit of reducing mismatch. They simulate two regimes—manual inspection and agentic refinement—across varying levels of preference complexity and attention budgets. For each regime they compute platform conversion revenue, consumer net welfare, and fidelity (the precision with which preferences are captured). The analysis compares outcomes to a “no‑search” benchmark.

## Results  
Theoretical calculations reveal that manual inspection stops at low complexity, yielding zero platform revenue; agentic search generates positive revenue across higher complexities. Adoption lag emerges when the cost of manual inspection is less than 0.5 units of attention, causing consumers to stick with manual despite lower conversion. Fidelity allocation is inverted: users who allocate a larger attention budget receive lower‑fidelity preference data because they can refine preferences further.

## Significance  
The work clarifies the economics of AI‑mediated commerce by showing that platform incentives and consumer willingness drive adoption, while attention budgets shape fidelity design. It highlights that scarcity shifts from product inspection to preference articulation, making consumers’ willingness and ability to interact central to voluntary use.
