## Summary  
This paper investigates how peer and institutional incentives jointly shape the evolution of cooperation, social welfare, and enforcement efficiency in a four‑strategy Prisoners’ Dilemma setting. By allowing decentralized peer rewards/punishments to act simultaneously with centralised institutional rewards/punishments, the authors analyse replicator dynamics in infinite well‑mixed populations and spatial effects on square lattices through agent‑based simulations. The study evaluates various intervention schemes by their equilibrium outcomes, evolutionary trajectories, cooperation levels, and aggregate social welfare net of costs. Their key insight is that institutions can simultaneously boost cooperation and welfare without sacrificing the benefits of peer punishment.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Peer punishment most strongly promotes cooperation among the four strategies (C, D, SP, SR).  
- [Finding 2] Institutionally rewarding peer‑incentive strategies substantially improves both cooperation and social welfare, whereas subsidising unconditional cooperators has little impact.  
- [Finding 3] Directly penalising defectors is the only consistently effective institutional policy; punishing peer incentive strategies reduces cooperation and harms overall societal benefit.

## Methodology  
The authors adopt a theoretical framework based on replicator dynamics for infinite, well‑mixed populations to capture the evolution of strategies under simultaneous peer and centralised incentives. For structured environments they employ agent‑based simulations on square lattice networks to examine spatial interactions and network reciprocity. Intervention schemes are quantified by equilibrium states and evolutionary flow in the former case, and by cooperation levels and aggregate social welfare (payoff minus institutional cost) in the latter.

## Results  
Theoretical analysis shows that peer punishment drives higher cooperation rates than peer reward, while institutional rewards enhance both cooperation and welfare. Simulations confirm that rewarding peer strategies yields the best trade‑off, whereas subsidising unconditional cooperators offers minimal benefit. Directly punishing defectors is the most reliable policy for maintaining cooperation, but penalising peer incentives undermines decentralised enforcement, lowers cooperation, and reduces social welfare.

## Significance  
These findings provide concrete design principles for institutions seeking to balance cooperation promotion with overall societal benefit. They demonstrate that maximising cooperation alone can be suboptimal; instead, a mixed approach that rewards cooperative peer behaviour while directly penalising defectors yields the most effective outcomes. The results are relevant to any setting where decentralised incentives and central coordination intersect.

## Related Concepts  
Prisoners’ Dilemma, replicator dynamics, peer incentives (reward/punishment), institutional interventions, social welfare, enforcement efficiency, square lattice networks, decentralized vs. centralized incentives, evolutionary game theory.
