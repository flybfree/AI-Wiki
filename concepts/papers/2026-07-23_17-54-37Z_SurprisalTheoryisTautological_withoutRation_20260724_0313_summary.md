## Summary  
The paper argues that surprisal theory—linking the perceived difficulty of a linguistic unit to its surprisal under some language model—is logically tautological: for any non‑negative difficulty function there exists a language model that makes the two variables linearly related, so the theory cannot make falsifiable predictions without additional constraints. It critiques the implicit assumption in psycholinguistics that this language model is simply the distribution of the training corpus, which recent empirical work has shown to be empirically false. The main contribution is demonstrating this tautology and proposing a rationalist intervention that replaces empirical fitting with a non‑empirical model grounded in cognitive constraints.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Surprisal theory is logically tautological given any non‑negative difficulty measure, because an affine function can always be matched by some language model.  
- [Finding 2] Empirical studies demonstrate that improving corpus fit does not consistently reduce processing difficulty; in fact, better models can increase difficulty, undermining the assumption of monotonic correspondence.  
- [Finding 3] A rationalist intervention is required: the language model must be derived from non‑empirical constraints (e.g., memory limits or processing goals) rather than from the data itself.

## Methodology  
The authors combine a theoretical construction with a review of recent empirical experiments. Theoretically, they formalize surprisal theory by assuming an affine relationship between difficulty \(d\) and surprisal \(\sigma\): \(d = a\sigma + b\). Under mild technical conditions (e.g., positivity of coefficients), for any chosen difficulty function one can construct a language model that satisfies this equation. Empirically, they examine studies where corpus‑fitting models are compared to human processing times using statistical tests such as linear regression and permutation tests to assess whether higher fit correlates with lower difficulty.

## Results  
The theoretical derivation shows the existence of a language model satisfying the affine relation for any difficulty function, confirming the tautology. Empirically, the results reveal no monotonic link: as corpus fit improves, processing difficulty sometimes rises, indicating that the assumed correspondence is not grounded in data. The rationalist intervention is presented as a conceptual framework rather than an empirical test; it posits models based on cognitive limits (e.g., working‑memory capacity) that would produce a fixed relationship between surprisal and difficulty independent of corpus statistics.

## Significance  
This work clears a longstanding misconception, preventing researchers from treating improved model fit as evidence for genuine explanatory power in surprisal theory. By exposing the tautology, it encourages a shift toward biologically or cognitively motivated models that can generate testable predictions about processing difficulty.
