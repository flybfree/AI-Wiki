## Summary  
This paper proposes **digital statecraft** as a new organising concept for governing the data‑driven substrate of modern states, arguing that “digital” reshapes rather than merely extends the classic question of statecraft. It identifies two inter‑related dimensions—statecraft over digital systems and statecraft with digital systems—and highlights an inherent tension between **technical coherence** (the ability to manage complex algorithms) and **legitimate authority** (public trust). From this foundation the authors derive ten principles that expose structural governance failures when any condition is missing. The work also suggests that governing well in the algorithmic age may require a fundamental re‑examination of statehood’s boundaries, scale, and affiliative basis.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Introduces **digital statecraft** as an analytical framework with two dimensions (statecraft over/with digital systems) and grounds it on the tension between technical coherence and legitimate authority.  
- [Finding 2] Derives ten explicit principles of digital statecraft, each naming a condition whose absence produces identifiable governance failures such as loss of public interest first or non‑delegable core.  
- [Finding 3] Argues that algorithmic governance challenges the traditional notion of the state and may necessitate rethinking its institutional boundaries, scale, and affiliative basis.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem through **theoretical analysis** rather than empirical experimentation. They began by mapping existing concepts of statecraft onto the digital substrate, then identified the core tensions between technical coherence (systemic stability) and legitimate authority (public legitimacy). Using this conceptual mapping they generated ten principles that serve as diagnostic tools for governance failure.

## Results  
The principal results are **theoretical**: a set of ten principles outlining necessary conditions for effective algorithmic governance, and a conceptual model showing how the absence of any condition leads to specific structural failures. The paper also demonstrates that these principles highlight a gap between current state capabilities (technical coherence) and public legitimacy (authority), which is not merely a reform issue but a re‑configuration problem.

## Significance  
This work matters because it **provides a normative blueprint** for policymakers confronting algorithmic governance, exposing where existing systems break down. By foregrounding the tension between technical coherence and legitimate authority, it urges a shift from incremental reforms to a deeper rethinking of statehood, which could inform legislation on data sovereignty, hybrid institutions, and adaptive governance.
