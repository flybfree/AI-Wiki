## Summary  
The paper introduces an information‑theoretic model that interprets the stereotypical “insistence on sameness” observed in autism as a strategy to minimize surprise and uncertainty. By treating sensory input as random sequences (R) and memory storage as a retrieval system (M), the authors derive a conditional entropy metric whose reduction corresponds either to learning about the environment or to restricting it to known information. The framework links this minimization directly to autistic rigidity, offering a quantitative basis for related symptoms such as anxiety and sensory overload. Its novelty lies in translating these psychological phenomena into an optimization problem that can be implemented algorithmically.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] An entropic definition of autism as an impairment in discrimination, memorization, and prediction of tangible environmental properties.  
- [Finding 2] Insistence on sameness is a manifestation of minimizing the entropy metric \(D_H(R,M)=H(R|M)+H(M|R)\), where reduction of surprise/uncertainty drives behavior.  
- [Finding 3] The model quantifies concepts like sensory overload, anxiety, and rigidness and can be operationalized as learning or optimization algorithms for caregivers.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a theoretical information‑theoretic system: \(R\) denotes sequences of random stimuli, \(M\) stores retrieved patterns, and conditional entropies \(H(R|M)\) and \(H(M|R)\) represent surprise (uncertainty) in input and memory retrieval. By assuming that an autistic individual seeks to lower the combined metric, they explore two strategies—learning about \(R\) or restricting \(R\) to known \(M\). The derivation links these strategies to observable behaviors such as pedantry and disorientation.

## Results  
The analysis shows that minimizing \(D_H(R,M)\) can be achieved either by expanding knowledge (increasing \(H(M|R)\)) or by limiting exposure to unknown stimuli (decreasing \(H(R|M)\)). This theoretical mapping explains why autistic individuals may become distressed when faced with novel sensory patterns, as it directly raises the entropy metric. The framework also proposes that therapeutic interventions can be framed as algorithms that gradually lower this metric without overwhelming the individual.

## Significance  
Providing a formal, quantifiable explanation of autism‑related rigidity opens new avenues for designing evidence‑based therapies and robotic live‑in caregivers that act as optimization agents. By avoiding costly experimental studies on autistic participants, the model offers a Turing‑test‑like validation pathway, potentially accelerating practical implementation in daily life.
