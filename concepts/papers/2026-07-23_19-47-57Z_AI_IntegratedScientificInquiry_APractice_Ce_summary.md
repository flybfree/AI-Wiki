## Summary  
Artificial intelligence is increasingly embedded in scientific inquiry, and students need to experience it as a genuine part of the scientific process rather than as an isolated topic. This paper proposes a practice‑centered vision that treats AI tools as simplified versions of real instruments used within the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) practices of observing, analyzing, and modeling. The goal is to engage learners in authentic inquiry while fostering disciplinary AI literacy (DAIL). By integrating reflection points around each tool’s limitations, the approach prepares students for later use of agentic AI.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] AI should be presented as genuine scientific tools with simplified controls that preserve their core functions.  
- [Finding 2] Each instrument must include a distinct reflection point prompting critical evaluation of its potential biases or errors.  
- [Finding 3] Agentic AI is only appropriate after students have built a foundational understanding of inquiry and the specific AI instruments.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by mapping the three core scientific practices (observing, analyzing, modeling) to existing AI capabilities: computer vision for observation, clustering algorithms for analysis, and generative models for modeling. They designed pedagogically bounded versions of these tools—simplified interfaces that retain essential functions—and embedded reflective prompts at each stage. The framework is organized as a staged curriculum that moves from individual instrument use to the eventual deployment of agentic AI across the inquiry.

## Results  
The theoretical model yields three distinct AI instruments, each aligned with one investigative phase and equipped with its own reflection point. This structure demonstrates how AI can be integrated without compromising scientific rigor or student learning objectives, thereby supporting a coherent DAIL curriculum.

## Significance  
This vision bridges authentic science education with emerging AI technologies, equipping students to understand both the power and pitfalls of AI in research. By grounding AI instruction within real scientific practices, it prepares learners for responsible participation in data‑driven inquiry and reduces misconceptions about AI’s role in science.
