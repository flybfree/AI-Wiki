## Summary  
This paper proposes a normative framework that defines *epistemic trustworthiness* as the condition under which users are epistemically warranted to rely on generative‑AI outputs in high‑stakes professional workflows. By moving beyond conventional metrics such as accuracy, fairness, and usability, the authors argue that justified reliance requires three jointly necessary conditions: epistemic humility, epistemic access, and resistance to epistemic injustice. The study demonstrates how violations of these conditions can cause real‑world harms that standard AI evaluations fail to capture.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1]: Epistemic humility requires systems to represent and communicate the limits of their competence.  
- [Finding 2]: Epistemic access requires systems to enable users to inspect, question, and contest outputs in context.  
- [Finding 3]: Resistance to epistemic injustice requires systems to recognise users as legitimate epistemic agents and avoid marginalising their knowledge and experience.  

## Methodology  
The authors draw on philosophical accounts of trustworthiness that treat competence and audience‑orientation as core components of trustworthy interaction. They develop a constitutive normative framework comprising the three conditions above, then apply it through case analyses in legal reasoning, medical reasoning, and hiring processes to illustrate how each condition functions or fails.

## Results  
The case studies reveal concrete harms when any one condition is violated: (1) epistemic humility failures lead users to over‑trust AI without acknowledging uncertainty; (2) epistemic access deficits prevent users from verifying or contesting outputs, reinforcing blind reliance; (3) resistance to epistemic injustice silences marginalised voices and reproduces systemic bias. These outcomes are distinct from mere accuracy or fairness errors, showing that standard AI metrics do not capture the full epistemic risk.

## Significance  
By foregrounding epistemic trustworthiness as a design target, this framework offers a more holistic evaluation of generative‑AI systems, encouraging developers to build models that acknowledge limits, support user scrutiny, and respect diverse knowledge. It moves the discourse from “is it correct?” to “should I rely on it?”—a shift crucial for high‑stakes domains where misplaced confidence can have severe consequences.
