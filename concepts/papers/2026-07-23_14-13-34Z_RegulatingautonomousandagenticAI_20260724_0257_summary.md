## Summary  
The paper tackles the urgent challenge of regulating autonomous and agentic AI systems that operate beyond human direct control. By examining four existing regulatory regimes—UK content‑platform rules, data protection law, UK financial services oversight, and the EU AI Act’s cross‑sectoral framework—the authors demonstrate that current governance models are ill‑suited to the new dynamics of AI autonomy. Their contribution is a systematic analysis that identifies systemic risks generated by autonomous AI and proposes a shift from reactive to proactive regulation.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Autonomous and agentic AI generate novel systemic risks that cannot be managed through retrospective supervisory oversight alone.  
- Finding 2: Existing regulatory assumptions about the know‑how of the regulatee are obsolete because much of the AI supply chain lies outside traditional control points.  
- Finding 3: A fresh, forward‑looking governance architecture is required to adapt regulation to AI autonomy and prevent emergent harms.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem through a comparative doctrinal analysis. They mapped each regulatory regime’s scope, objectives, and enforcement mechanisms onto the four case studies, then identified gaps where autonomous AI activities fall outside the model’s reach. This qualitative synthesis was supplemented by expert interviews to gauge practical feasibility of proposed solutions.

## Results  
The analysis reveals that all four regimes rely on reactive oversight—audits after the fact—and lack mechanisms for anticipatory risk management. The EU AI Act’s cross‑sectoral regime is the most forward‑looking, yet even it does not fully address supply‑chain opacity. The study concludes that a novel governance model integrating real‑time monitoring, shared responsibility across the AI ecosystem, and adaptive licensing is essential.

## Significance  
This work matters because it calls for a paradigm shift in how regulators think about AI autonomy. By highlighting the inadequacy of current frameworks, the authors provide a roadmap for policymakers to design proactive, systemic safeguards that can mitigate risks before they materialise, thereby protecting public welfare and fostering trust.

## Related Concepts  
autonomous AI, agentic AI, supply‑chain governance, regulatory anticipation, systemic risk, cross‑sectoral regulation, reactive vs. proactive oversight, licensing frameworks, shared responsibility model.
