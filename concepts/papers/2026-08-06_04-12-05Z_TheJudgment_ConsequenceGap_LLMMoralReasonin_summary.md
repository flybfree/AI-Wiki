## Summary  
The paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) reason about moral responsibility in healthcare decisions, focusing on the gap between their judgment of patient culpability and their allocation of scarce resources. It compares LLMs to human judgments across a series of clinical vignettes where patients exhibit behaviors that could be judged as responsible or not for their illness. The study reveals systematic differences in moral reasoning that increase with model capability. This work highlights a novel gap between judgment and consequence in AI ethics.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: LLMs agree with humans that patients bear responsibility for health‑harming behaviors but then ignore this judgment when allocating scarce resources, defaulting to random allocation.  
- Finding 2: When access to health‑risk information is unavailable, LLMs place greater emphasis on information rather than responsibility, reducing the impact of culpability judgments.  
- Finding 3: As reasoning capability increases, LLMs' moral framework diverges more from human norms, amplifying disagreement.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed a set of clinical vignettes drawn from prior studies where patients exhibit behaviors that could be judged as responsible or not for their illness. Participants include both human clinicians and multiple LLM instances (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude 3) evaluated on the same prompts. The experiments measure agreement on responsibility judgments and subsequent resource allocation decisions. The methodology includes controlled random assignment of patient actions to ensure variability.

## Results  
Experimental results show that humans consistently favor allocating scarce resources to the less‑culpable patient, whereas LLMs allocate randomly regardless of culpability. Moreover, when health‑risk knowledge is withheld, LLMs prioritize information access and assign equal chances, indicating a shift away from responsibility. Notably, high‑capability models exhibit larger gaps between their allocation patterns and human preferences.

## Significance  
This study uncovers a systematic moral reasoning gap that could affect AI deployment in healthcare, where decisions about limited resources are ethically charged. By quantifying the divergence between judgment and consequence, it informs developers to align LLM behavior with human ethical standards. The findings also suggest that information availability can mediate responsibility judgments, offering a lever for improving AI fairness.
