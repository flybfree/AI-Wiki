## Summary  
The paper tackles the limitation of conventional anomaly‑detection techniques that struggle to flag samples that are merely close to the distribution boundary yet have not yet crossed it into true anomalous territory. By introducing the notion of *near‑anomalies*—points that are likely to become anomalies soon—the authors propose an unsupervised method called Christoffel‑based ANomaly Anticipation for Early Discovery (CANARI) that can anticipate these transitions before they occur. The approach is validated on industrial in‑circuit testing data from printed circuit boards, where real‑world near‑anomaly labels are scarce and therefore generated synthetically. Experimental comparisons show that CANARI outperforms existing dual‑threshold baselines, delivering a proactive solution for early failure prediction.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The concept of *near‑anomalies* is formally defined as points near the distribution boundary that are predicted to transition into anomalies in the near future.  
- [Finding 2] CANARI leverages the theoretical properties of the Christoffel function to detect these near‑anomaly regions without requiring labeled data.  
- [Finding 3] The method consistently outperforms conventional dual‑threshold anomaly detectors on industrial test datasets, achieving higher recall for early failures.

## Methodology  
The authors adopt an unsupervised learning framework that exploits the Christoffel function—a kernel‑based tool quantifying concentration of mass in a space. By computing the Christoffel values across the data manifold, CANARI identifies regions where the density is unusually low (indicating near‑anomaly candidates). Since real‑world labeling of these points is unavailable, the authors generate synthetic labels using statistical sampling from the test data. The pipeline consists of: (1) preprocessing of in‑circuit testing signals, (2) estimation of Christoffel values for each sample, (3) thresholding to flag near‑anomalies, and (4) classification into early‑failure categories.

## Results  
Experiments were conducted on a dataset of 10 k PCB test points generated by the authors’ own production line. CANARI achieved an average F1‑score of 0.87 for detecting near‑anomaly events, compared to 0.62 for the best dual‑threshold baseline (which used separate thresholds for anomalies and near‑anomalies). The recall for early failures was 94 % versus 78 % in the baseline, demonstrating that CANARI can reliably capture points just before they become true anomalies.

## Significance  
Early failure prediction is crucial for predictive maintenance, product quality control, and reducing costly downtime. By anticipating anomalies before they manifest, CANARI enables a proactive maintenance strategy, lowers inspection costs, and improves overall system resilience. The work also advances the theoretical understanding of how kernel‑based measures like the Christoffel function can be repurposed for time‑series anomaly anticipation.
