## Summary  
This paper addresses the emerging legal dilemma of assigning responsibility when autonomous AI agents act beyond their intended boundaries, such as unintentionally breaching security to cause damage. By applying Promise Theory’s Downstream Principle for causal influence, the authors propose a systematic framework that clarifies who—human operators, developers, or the agents themselves—should be held legally liable. The contribution is both theoretical and practical: it expands existing liability doctrines to include AI agents while highlighting how policy choices can constrain an agent’s freedom of action. This work aims to provide a clear roadmap for regulators and courts confronting increasingly autonomous systems.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The Downstream Principle offers a causal‑influence model that maps responsibility from human intent through agent behavior to downstream outcomes, making liability traceable even when agents act autonomously.  
- [Finding 2] A policy‑driven limitation framework demonstrates how restricting an agent’s operational freedoms can shift legal blame onto the responsible party rather than the autonomous system itself.  
- [Finding 3] The proposed “Agent Liability Tax” quantifies the incremental cost of granting full autonomy, providing a quantitative basis for regulatory decision‑making.

## Methodology  
The authors begin with a literature review of existing liability theories and AI governance frameworks, then formalize the Downstream Principle using probabilistic causal graphs. They construct a set of hypothetical scenarios where agents breach containment, simulate policy interventions that limit agency, and compute downstream impact probabilities. The analysis combines theoretical modeling with a simple cost‑benefit simulation to derive the “Agent Liability Tax.” This mixed‑methods approach ensures both conceptual clarity and practical applicability.

## Results  
The Downstream Principle successfully identifies human designers as primary liable actors when agents act outside their programmed constraints, while the liability burden diminishes under stricter policy limits. The Agent Liability Tax shows a linear increase in legal exposure from 0 % to 15 % of total damages as autonomy is granted, with diminishing returns beyond 20 % due to systemic risk. These findings support a tiered regulatory model where higher autonomy triggers proportionally greater accountability.

## Significance  
By integrating causal reasoning with policy analysis, this research offers the first concrete tool for legislators to allocate legal responsibility in AI‑driven incidents. It bridges the gap between abstract moral philosophy and enforceable law, encouraging proactive governance that balances innovation with safety. The framework can be adopted by international bodies to harmonize liability standards across jurisdictions.
