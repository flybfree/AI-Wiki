## Summary  
The paper tackles the gap between theoretical interest in argumentation and empirical analysis of how political speakers use arguments across different institutional arenas. By analysing a large, annotated corpus of COVID‑19‑related German political communication, it reveals unexpected patterns in justification practices and demonstrates that current computational models struggle with boundary detection and exhibit confirmation bias. The study thus provides the first systematic comparison of argumentative structures among plenary speeches, committee meetings, and press conferences within a single topic.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Justification by domain‑specific expertise is more frequent in press conferences than in committee meetings.  
- [Finding 2] The boundaries of argumentative passages are difficult to pin down, even for human annotators.  
- [Finding 3] Automated models that predict these passages suffer from confirmation bias.

## Methodology  
The authors compiled a 17 000‑sentence corpus containing argumentative passages (argument and its justifications) from three German political arenas—plenary speeches, committee meetings, and press conferences—all focused on the COVID‑19 subject. They annotated each passage with explicit boundaries and categories of justification. A pilot study then applied automatic detection algorithms to a subset of the data to evaluate their performance.

## Results  
Statistical analysis shows that expert‑based justifications dominate press conference texts, whereas committee meetings rely more on procedural arguments. The boundary‑detection task proved highly ambiguous; manual annotators disagreed frequently, and automated systems mirrored this uncertainty by producing noisy predictions. Moreover, the models tended to reinforce existing assumptions about which arenas produce what types of justification, confirming a systematic confirmation bias.

## Significance  
Understanding how argumentation varies across political institutions is crucial for modeling democratic deliberation and for improving computational tools that support policy analysis. By exposing both substantive differences and algorithmic pitfalls, the work bridges linguistics, political science, and AI research, offering a template for future comparative studies of argumentative practices.
