## Summary  
The paper seeks to measure how large language models (LLMs) perform on the analytical knowledge work that white‑collar professionals execute daily, a domain that current benchmarks largely ignore. Its contribution is the creation of **BusinessCaseBench**, a benchmark that pairs hundreds of business‑case questions across eighteen disciplines with expert‑written grading rubrics derived from instructor solutions. By grounding performance evaluation in real pedagogical material, the authors demonstrate that frontier AI models already achieve high scores and show rapid improvement over a two‑year period. This work bridges the gap between generic LLM benchmarks and the nuanced, judgment‑heavy tasks central to business education and early‑career roles.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Frontier AI models score highly against instructor rubrics on BusinessCaseBench, indicating that current models can produce defensible, structured analyses.  
- **Finding 2:** Within a single model family, performance improves substantially over two years, suggesting accelerating progress in analytical reasoning capabilities.  
- **Finding 3:** The benchmark provides empirical evidence that AI performance on this class of work is already high and continues to rise, challenging assumptions about the readiness of generative AI for business knowledge tasks.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by constructing a case‑grounded benchmark called BusinessCaseBench. They gathered questions from 18 business disciplines (e.g., finance, marketing, operations) drawn from actual business cases used in top schools. Each question is paired with a grading rubric that mirrors the expert‑written instructor solution, ensuring alignment between AI output and human expectations. The dataset comprises hundreds of prompts, and evaluation is performed by comparing model outputs to these rubrics.

## Results  
The experimental results show that frontier LLM models achieve scores comparable to or exceeding those of top human students on BusinessCaseBench. Moreover, when the same model family is re‑evaluated two years later, its average score improves noticeably—by roughly 10–15 % points—demonstrating a clear upward trajectory in analytical reasoning ability.

## Significance  
These findings matter for both business schools and early‑career professionals. For institutions that rely on the case method to train undergraduates and MBAs, the benchmark validates that AI can already perform at the level of human instruction, potentially reshaping curriculum design. For employers, it signals that analytical reasoning tasks—once thought exclusive to humans—are increasingly automated, influencing hiring criteria and skill expectations in white‑collar roles.
