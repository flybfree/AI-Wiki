## Summary  
This paper aims to investigate the cognitive gap between authors and peer reviewers regarding novelty assessment in academic papers. It seeks to quantify how promotional language influences reviewer evaluations relative to the intrinsic novelty of a manuscript. By analyzing a large corpus of Nature Communications articles, it identifies systematic differences in evaluation criteria. The study contributes empirical evidence on the interaction between self‑promotion intensity and reviewer judgment.

## Key Contributions  
- Reviewers adopt a more comprehensive evaluation perspective than authors, emphasizing result‑oriented innovation.  
- Promotional intensity has a positive effect on perceived novelty only for highly innovative papers, enhancing their evaluations.  
- The correlation between promotional language and reviewer disagreement is significant only for papers with moderate innovativeness, indicating that self‑promotion matters most in this intermediate zone.  

## Methodology  
The authors compiled 15,328 articles from Nature Communications published between 2016 and 2021, extracting the title, abstract, introduction, and peer‑review comments. They coded each paper for intrinsic novelty using criteria derived from reviewers’ own language while also measuring the intensity of promotional language in the authors’ materials. A statistical analysis compared reviewer evaluations across three levels of intrinsic novelty (very low, moderate, very high) and examined how promotional intensity interacted with these categories.  

## Results  
Reviewers consistently emphasized result‑oriented innovation more than authors did, suggesting a broader evaluative lens. Highly innovative papers benefited from stronger promotional language, receiving higher novelty scores, while low‑novelty papers were unaffected by promotion. Crucially, reviewers’ disagreement on novelty was most pronounced for moderate‑innovation papers when promotional intensity varied, indicating that self‑promotion has the greatest impact in this intermediate zone.  

## Significance  
This research reveals a systematic cognitive gap between authors and reviewers, affecting fairness of assessment and potentially biasing outcomes. It matters because it informs authors how to frame their work and highlights where peer review may be most sensitive to promotional language, guiding improvements in scholarly communication.
