## Summary  
The paper argues that surprisal theory is a tautological claim: any pattern of linguistic processing difficulty can be matched by an affine function of surprisal under some language model, so without additional constraints the theory makes no falsifiable predictions. It critiques the implicit assumption in psycholinguistics that the relevant language model is simply the distribution that generated the training corpus and points out recent empirical work showing that improving corpus fit does not improve human‑behavior predictions. The core contribution is to expose this tautology and to propose a rationalist intervention—using non‑empirical, cognitively motivated models of the comprehender—to break the circularity.  

## Key Contributions  
- Surprisal theory is a tautology: for any non‑negative difficulty measure there exists an affine surprisal function under mild technical conditions.  
- Empirical studies demonstrate that better corpus‑fit language models can actually worsen predictions of processing difficulty, undermining the assumption that corpus improvement improves human behavior.  
- A rationalist intervention is required—language models must be derived from cognitive constraints (e.g., memory limits or processing goals) rather than purely from empirical data.  

## Methodology  
The authors provide a theoretical proof that any difficulty function can be represented as an affine surprisal under some language model, and they review recent experimental results showing the breakdown of the corpus‑fit hypothesis. They then construct rationalist models based on hypothesized cognitive constraints to illustrate how such alternatives could avoid tautologicality.  

## Results  
Theoretical analysis shows that for any non‑negative difficulty pattern an affine surprisal mapping exists, confirming the tautology claim. Experimental results reveal that improving corpus fit does not correlate with improved human processing predictions, thereby falsifying the naive link between model performance and behavioral outcomes.  

## Significance  
This work highlights a fundamental flaw in data‑driven psycholinguistic models: they are unfalsifiable because any observed pattern can be explained by some language model. By demanding rationalist grounding—cognitive constraints rather than empirical fit—the paper opens the door to more meaningful, testable theories of comprehension that respect the limits of human processing.
