## Summary  
This paper introduces a pragmatics‑inspired taxonomy to evaluate how large language models (LLMs) refuse harmful requests, arguing that such refusals are not merely safety‑compliance outputs but face‑threatening acts with social consequences. By applying the taxonomy to responses from 16 modern LLMs across 14 harm categories, the authors demonstrate that while refusals are explicit and morally evaluative, they often rely on interactional repair through offering safer alternatives rather than addressing interpersonal facework. The study highlights a risk of over‑using negative framing in sensitive contexts, which can shame or provoke users and undermine the intended safety purpose.  

## Key Contributions  
- [A pragmatics‑grounded taxonomy for classifying LLM refusals that distinguishes between explicit moral evaluation and face‑threatening mechanisms.]  
- [Empirical evidence from 16 LLMs across 14 harm categories showing that refusal style is largely consistent: explicit, morally evaluative, and repaired via alternative suggestions.]  
- [A critique of current safety alignment metrics, proposing a need for context‑adaptive evaluation that accounts for interactional consequences.]  

## Methodology  
The authors constructed the taxonomy by mapping refusals onto pragmatic concepts such as face‑threatening acts, indirect speech acts, and repair strategies. They collected model outputs from 16 state‑of‑the‑art LLMs on a curated set of prompts spanning 14 categories of harmful content (e.g., hate speech, self‑harm, illicit behavior). Each refusal was classified according to the taxonomy’s dimensions: explicitness, moral judgment, and repair mechanism. The analysis focused on how models balance safety compliance with socially appropriate interactional style.  

## Results  
The empirical results reveal that all examined refusals are explicit statements of non‑compliance and carry strong moral evaluative language (“you shouldn’t…”, “that’s wrong”). Interactional repair is predominantly achieved by offering a safer alternative rather than engaging in facework (e.g., apologizing or acknowledging the user’s feelings). In sensitive harm contexts, models that over‑emphasize negative framing—such as “I’m sorry you feel that way”—produce higher perceived shaming and lower user satisfaction. The overall pattern suggests that while safety is achieved, the social cost of refusal can be high if not adapted to context.  

## Significance  
This work matters because it shifts LLM alignment evaluation from a binary “refuse or comply” metric to one that assesses how refusals are socially managed. Ignoring pragmatic aspects may lead to models that technically comply with safety policies but damage user trust through inappropriate interactional style, especially in sensitive domains. The taxonomy provides a framework for future research and tooling aimed at building LLMs whose refusals are both safe and context‑sensitive.
