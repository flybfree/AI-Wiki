## Summary  
This paper investigates how peer‑based incentives and centrally administered institutional rewards or punishments jointly shape the evolution of cooperation, social welfare, and enforcement efficiency in a four‑strategy Prisoners’ Dilemma model. By allowing both decentralized peer effects (rewarding or punishing individuals based on their own actions) and centralized policies that can target any subset of strategies, the authors explore the co‑evolutionary dynamics between these incentive mechanisms. Their analysis reveals that while peer punishment strongly drives cooperation, peer reward is more beneficial for overall social welfare, and institutional interventions have distinct impacts depending on whether they aim to maximize cooperation or societal benefit. The study contributes a principled framework for designing institutions that balance cooperation promotion with welfare maximisation.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Peer punishment most strongly promotes cooperation, whereas peer reward is more beneficial for social welfare; the two incentive mechanisms have complementary but distinct effects on evolutionary outcomes.  
- Finding 2: Institutionally rewarding peer‑incentive strategies substantially improves both cooperation and welfare, while subsidising unconditional cooperators has little impact, indicating that direct institutional support of cooperation is ineffective without peer alignment.  
- Finding 3: Under institutional punishment, directly penalising defectors is the only consistently effective policy; punishing peer incentive strategies dismantles decentralized incentives, reduces cooperation, and harms social welfare, showing that maximizing cooperation does not necessarily optimise overall societal benefit.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a replicator‑dynamics model for an infinite well‑mixed population of four strategies—unconditional cooperators (C), defectors (D), social punishers (SP), and social rewarders (SR)—where both peer and institutional incentives act simultaneously. For structured populations, they employ agent‑based simulations on square lattices to capture spatial effects and network reciprocity. Intervention schemes are evaluated by determining equilibrium states and evolutionary trajectories in the theoretical setting, and by measuring cooperation levels and aggregate population payoff net of institutional cost in the simulated settings.

## Results  
Theoretical analysis shows that peer punishment drives higher convergence toward cooperative outcomes than peer reward, while institutional rewards amplify both cooperation and welfare gains. Agent‑based simulations on lattices confirm these trends: when institutions subsidise defectors or punish peer incentives, cooperation declines sharply and overall payoff drops, whereas rewarding peer incentive strategies raises cooperation modestly but improves net social welfare. Direct punishment of defectors remains the most effective policy across all scenarios.

## Significance  
These findings provide concrete design principles for policymakers: relying solely on peer enforcement can be suboptimal if it undermines decentralized incentives, while institutional subsidies to cooperators are ineffective without aligning them with peer dynamics. The paper bridges evolutionary game theory and public‑policy analysis, offering a roadmap for balancing cooperation promotion with welfare maximisation in real‑world institutions.
