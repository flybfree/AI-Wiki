## Summary  
This paper introduces a trio‑ethnography that follows two computing educators with contrasting teaching philosophies and one undergraduate computer science student as they engage in three dialogue sessions about the use of generative AI in programming education. The study aims to move beyond surface‑level classroom observations toward a deeper, reflective understanding of how students’ AI‑supported learning unfolds. By juxtaposing educators’ assumptions with the student’s lived narratives, the authors reveal hidden learning processes that were previously invisible to teachers. Their contribution is a methodological framework and empirical evidence showing that such trio‑ethnography can reshape instructional practice in an era dominated by generative AI.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The narrative accounts of the student expose learning pathways—such as self‑directed debugging, iterative experimentation, and tacit knowledge transfer—that are not captured by observable classroom behaviors.  
- [Finding 2] Educators’ assumptions about AI use, assessment transparency, and pedagogical relevance are systematically challenged, prompting a re‑evaluation of their instructional strategies.  
- [Finding 3] The trio‑ethnography methodology demonstrates that reflective dialogue among multiple stakeholders can surface insights that single‑observer ethnographies miss.

## Methodology  
The authors selected two computing educators—one emphasizing constructivist inquiry and the other advocating a more traditional, standards‑driven approach—and paired them with an undergraduate student who regularly uses generative AI tools. Over three structured conversations, participants exchanged reflections on AI support, discussed evolving pedagogy, and revisited their initial assumptions after hearing the student’s narratives. This iterative dialogue constitutes a trio‑ethnography: a mixed‑methods approach that blends observation, interview, and reflective writing to capture evolving interpretations.

## Results  
The emergent themes from the student’s stories include frequent reliance on AI for code generation, yet also moments of independent problem solving; teachers’ initial skepticism gave way to curiosity about how AI can be integrated transparently into assessments; and both educators recognized gaps between their expectations and the students’ actual learning trajectories. These findings confirm that AI‑supported learning involves complex, often non‑linear processes that are invisible without direct narrative access.

## Significance  
By highlighting the invisibility of certain learning dynamics in conventional classroom observation, this work underscores the need for reflective, multi‑perspective methodologies in AI‑enhanced education. It offers educators a concrete pathway to adapt curricula responsively, ensuring that assessment and instruction remain aligned with students’ authentic use of generative tools.
