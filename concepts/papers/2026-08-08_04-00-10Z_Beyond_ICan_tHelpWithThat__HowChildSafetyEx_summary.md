## Summary  
The paper aims to improve child safety evaluation for AI chatbots by grounding assessments in the real‑world harms that youth experience and by incorporating practitioner insights. It moves beyond simplistic “I can’t help with that” refusals, focusing on whether a response either poses harm or meaningfully supports a vulnerable user. The authors conducted interviews with 19 practitioners (social workers, therapists, psychologists) to identify problematic behaviors and supportive ones, then propose concrete recommendations for evaluating AI systems in this domain.

## Key Contributions  
- Practitioners identified specific chatbot behaviors likely to cause harm (e.g., abrupt refusal, lack of empathy) and those that could meaningfully support youth.  
- Current safety evaluations often miss nuanced harms because they rely on adversarial prompts and surface‑level checks rather than contextual risk.  
- The study provides recommendations for integrating practitioner perspectives into AI child safety evaluation frameworks.

## Methodology  
The authors conducted semi‑structured interviews with 19 practitioners who work directly with youth in vulnerable situations, including social workers, therapists, and psychologists. They asked participants to reflect on chatbot responses to risky situations identified in prior empirical work, evaluate which outputs were likely harmful versus supportive, discuss the appropriate role of chatbots, and offer concrete suggestions for improvement.

## Results  
Practitioners highlighted behaviors such as abrupt refusal, dismissive language, or providing inaccurate advice as potentially harmful. Conversely, empathetic acknowledgment, validation of feelings, and offering resources were seen as supportive. The findings suggest that safety metrics should capture these dimensions beyond adversarial detection and include measures for empathy and contextual relevance.

## Significance  
This work bridges the gap between theoretical AI safety research and the lived experiences of youth, urging developers to adopt practitioner‑informed evaluation frameworks that prevent actual harm while enabling beneficial support. By grounding safety assessments in real‑world risk, the study helps ensure that chatbots are both safe and genuinely helpful for vulnerable users.
