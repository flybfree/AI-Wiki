## Summary  
This paper seeks to map how artificial intelligence is treated in U.S. federal court opinions by systematically analysing 559 cases where AI is a point of contention. It constructs a taxonomy that captures the most common dispute topics, the six principal AI technologies involved, and four recurring types of litigants, while also comparing this legal landscape to the AI Incident Database to expose coverage gaps. The study shows that courts largely rely on pre‑existing statutes rather than creating new AI‑specific rules, producing a “piecemeal” form of governance.

## Key Contributions  
- **Seven recurring dispute areas** are identified as the primary domains where AI is contested in federal court.  
- **Six distinct categories of AI technologies** dominate the litigation record, ranging from machine‑learning models to data‑analytics platforms.  
- **Four common litigant typologies** (e.g., plaintiffs suing for negligence versus defendants invoking regulatory compliance) are documented.

## Methodology  
The authors performed a comprehensive literature review of federal court opinions that mention AI as a substantive issue, extracting and coding each opinion into four dimensions: dispute topics, implicated technologies, party types, and legal doctrines applied. They then cross‑referenced their taxonomy with the AI Incident Database to assess how well the database captures the same phenomena, noting discrepancies in definitions, prevalence, and documented harms.

## Results  
The analysis revealed that while the court taxonomy aligns with seven dispute areas and six technology categories, the AI Incident Database underrepresents many of these issues, suggesting that courts capture only a fraction of actual AI‑related harms. Moreover, the majority of decisions invoke existing statutes (e.g., the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act) rather than novel AI‑specific legal frameworks, indicating a reliance on “piecemeal” governance.

## Significance  
The findings matter because they expose a regulatory blind spot: federal court outcomes are shaped more by which harms fit within current statutes than by the occurrence of AI‑induced damage. This insight underscores the need for coordinated policy that creates coherent, forward‑looking AI regulations rather than relying on fragmented case law.
