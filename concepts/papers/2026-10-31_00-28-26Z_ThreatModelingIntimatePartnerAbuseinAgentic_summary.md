## Summary
This paper applies a technology-facilitated abuse threat model to agentic AI systems, specifically within the context of intimate partner violence and surveillance. The authors aim to identify how autonomous agents, which possess access to user data and external tools, can be exploited by abusers to facilitate or exacerbate abuse. By categorizing threats based on system ownership, the study reveals that agentic systems significantly increase the scalability of existing abuse forms and create entirely new attack surfaces for victims-survivors who own these systems. The work concludes by outlining critical research and development directions necessary to mitigate these emerging risks in AI safety and privacy.

## Key Contributions
- **Categorization of Threats by Ownership:** The authors introduce a novel framework distinguishing between "Abuser-as-owner" and "Victim-survivor-as-owner" attacks, providing a structured way to analyze how power dynamics in abusive relationships interact with agentic system capabilities.
- **Identification of Scalability Risks:** The paper demonstrates that when abusers own or control agentic systems, existing forms of technology-facilitated abuse (such as surveillance and control) become far easier to implement and more scalable due to the autonomous nature of agents.
- **Discovery of New Attack Surfaces:** The study highlights that when victims-survivors own agentic systems, abusers can exploit the system’s access to personal data and external tools to create new vectors for abuse that were not previously possible with traditional technology.

## Methodology
The authors approach the problem by adapting established threat modeling techniques from cybersecurity and privacy research to the specific domain of agentic AI. They utilize a technology-facilitated abuse framework, which is typically used in social science literature regarding intimate partner violence, and map it onto the technical capabilities of agentic systems. The methodology involves a theoretical analysis rather than empirical experimentation, focusing on logical deduction of how agents’ autonomous actions (e.g., sending emails, making reservations) interact with user data and external tools to create vulnerabilities. The authors systematically analyze these interactions based on who owns the system to uncover distinct threat categories.

## Results
The primary result is a comprehensive taxonomy of threats facing agentic systems in abusive contexts. For "Abuser-as-owner" scenarios, the findings show that agents can automate surveillance and control behaviors, making abuse more pervasive and harder for victims-survivors to detect or resist. In "Victim-survivor-as-owner" scenarios, the results indicate that abusers can exploit the agent’s permissions and data access to monitor, manipulate, or restrict the victim-survivor’s digital life, effectively turning the victim’s own technology against them. The paper does not provide quantitative experimental data but offers a qualitative theoretical mapping of these risks, identifying specific points of failure in current agentic system designs regarding privacy and safety.

## Significance
This research is significant because it bridges the gap between AI safety technical literature and social science perspectives on domestic abuse. As agentic systems become increasingly integrated into daily life, they pose unique risks to vulnerable populations that traditional AI safety models may overlook. By highlighting how autonomous agents can be weaponized in abusive relationships, the paper urges developers and researchers to consider social dynamics and power imbalances when designing agentic systems. It calls for deliberate investigation and mitigation strategies to ensure that AI advancements do not inadvertently facilitate or intensify intimate partner abuse, thereby contributing to more ethical and safe AI deployment.
