## Summary  
This paper investigates caregivers’ perceptions of four categories of care robots—delivering supplies, helping patients into bed, monitoring vital signs, and assisting with mobility—to understand how these technologies are received in healthcare settings. By combining quantitative scales derived from the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) and the Cognitive‑Affective‑Normative model with qualitative analysis through an ethical framework, the authors aim to produce a context‑sensitive view of responsible design and implementation. The study draws on 298 caregivers from the United States, Mexico, and Chile, enabling cross‑national comparison while maintaining a human‑centered focus.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Caregivers generally evaluate care robots positively when they perform logistical or physically demanding tasks such as delivering supplies or assisting with mobility.  
- [Finding 2] Ethical concerns about dependability, the need for human oversight, and potential job displacement are repeatedly raised across all four robot types.  
- [Finding 3] While overall sentiment is favorable, countries differ in how they prioritize these ethical issues, revealing culturally shaped interpretations of responsible deployment.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a mixed‑methods approach using a mixed‑factorial design. Quantitative measures integrated constructs from UTAUT, the Cognitive‑Affective‑Normative model, and overall acceptance ratings to capture attitudes toward each robot category. Qualitative data were gathered via open‑ended questions and analyzed through an ethically informed framework that explores stakeholders’ values and concerns.

## Results  
Participants gave higher scores for supply delivery and mobility assistance robots than for bed‑assistance or vital‑sign monitoring robots, indicating a preference for tasks that reduce physical strain over those requiring close interpersonal interaction. Qualitative insights highlighted benefits such as reduced workload, lower risk, and greater patient autonomy, while also expressing worries about robot dependability, the necessity of human oversight, and possible job displacement.

## Significance  
These findings advance a socially informed understanding of care‑robot implementation, offering designers and policymakers concrete evidence on which functions are most acceptable and where ethical safeguards are needed. The study’s cross‑cultural perspective enriches the literature by showing that acceptance is not uniform but shaped by local priorities.
