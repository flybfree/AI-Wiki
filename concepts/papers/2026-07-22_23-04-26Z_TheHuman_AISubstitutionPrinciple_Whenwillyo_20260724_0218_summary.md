## Summary  
The paper introduces a formal analytical model for Human‑AI Task Allocation (HAT) in hierarchical organizations, aiming to answer the question of when AI will replace human labor. By encoding an economic asymmetry between skill acquisition and AI capability scaling, the authors derive a precise condition—later termed the **Human‑AI Substitution Principle**—that predicts replacement under specific structural conditions. The model also explains how risk‑adjusted costs, organizational depth, deployment scale, strategic adaptation, and risk differentials jointly determine timing, location, and type of workforce transition. This work moves beyond ad‑hoc heuristics to a unified theory linking automation economics with organizational design.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: The HAT model formally captures the economic asymmetry between human skill acquisition and AI capability scaling in hierarchical organizations.  
- Finding 2: It derives the Human‑AI Substitution Principle, providing a precise condition for when AI replaces human labor based on risk‑adjusted costs, organizational depth, deployment scale, strategic adaptation, and risk differentials.  
- Finding 3: The model shows that AI adoption can cause abrupt workforce transitions, hybrid human‑AI organizations, and elevated vulnerability of middle‑management roles without requiring a minimum‑human‑fraction constraint.

## Methodology  
The authors construct an analytical framework for HAT by treating tasks as resources allocated between humans and AI agents within a hierarchical structure. They incorporate risk‑adjusted cost functions, define organizational depth (number of management layers), deployment scale (size of AI rollout), strategic adaptation (organizational response to automation), and risk differentials (variation in task difficulty). The model is solved analytically to generate conditions under which the substitution of human labor by AI becomes economically optimal.

## Results  
The theoretical results identify a clear threshold: when AI’s marginal cost falls below the risk‑adjusted human cost, replacement occurs. The HAT model predicts that flatter hierarchies with wider spans of control increase middle‑management vulnerability because skill thresholds are lower. It also demonstrates that hybrid organizations can persist even without a minimum‑human‑fraction rule if risk heterogeneity sustains both roles.

## Significance  
This unified theory bridges automation economics, organizational design, AI governance, and workforce planning, offering managers a quantitative roadmap for anticipating AI‑driven transitions. By linking structural factors to substitution timing, the paper enables proactive policy and strategic decisions that mitigate disruption and preserve human capital where beneficial.
