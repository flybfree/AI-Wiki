## Summary  
This paper addresses the challenge of transferring wind‑farm power‑curve models from an operational site to a new, undeveloped farm, which is essential for reliable site‑planning decisions. By treating each farm as a domain defined by its temporal weather patterns and spatial terrain features, the authors propose a domain‑adapted transfer‑learning framework that learns a similarity metric between domains rather than relying on simplistic distance or layout assumptions. Their approach consistently improves power‑curve predictions compared with traditional methods, demonstrating a more robust and adaptable solution for cross‑farm applications.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The domain is explicitly characterized by both temporal environmental variates (e.g., wind speed distribution) and spatial terrain variables (e.g., height profile), enabling a unified representation of the source and target farms.  
- [Finding 2] A learned similarity metric, derived through adversarial training, captures these multi‑modal differences while preserving turbine‑specific power relationships.  
- [Finding 3] The adapted power curve achieves up to 15 % higher forecast accuracy on unseen farms than baseline distance‑based or terrain‑only methods.

## Methodology  
The authors first collect high‑resolution turbine data from an existing farm, including instantaneous wind speeds and corresponding electrical outputs. They then extract domain embeddings that encode temporal weather statistics (mean, variance, skewness) and spatial terrain descriptors (terrain height map, slope). Using a contrastive loss, they train a neural network to produce embeddings for both the source and target farms such that samples from the same domain are close while those from different domains are far. The similarity metric is obtained as the cosine distance between these embeddings. Finally, the adapted power curve is generated by applying this metric to the source farm’s measured curve, adjusting turbine parameters (e.g., cut‑in speed) to align with the target domain.

## Results  
Experiments on three real wind farms in different geographic regions show that the domain‑adapted model outperforms distance‑based transfer (average RMSE reduction of 12 %) and terrain‑only adaptation (RMSE reduction of 8 %). The improvement is most pronounced for low‑power regimes where turbine behavior is highly sensitive to local conditions. Statistical tests confirm the superiority across multiple evaluation sets, with p‑values <0.01.

## Significance  
Accurate power‑curve transfer directly influences wind‑energy project feasibility and cost estimation. By eliminating reliance on simplistic assumptions, this method enables faster, more reliable site‑planning decisions, reduces over‑estimation of renewable potential, and supports better investment planning for developers and policymakers.
