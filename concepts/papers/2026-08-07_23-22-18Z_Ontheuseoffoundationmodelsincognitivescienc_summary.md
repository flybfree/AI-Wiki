## Summary  
The paper proposes a four‑stage inferential framework for evaluating foundation models (FMs) as cognitive and developmental models, arguing that behavioral alignment alone is insufficient without explicit theoretical mapping. It seeks to clarify when FM outputs can be treated as explanatory representations of human cognition by outlining conditions under which such claims are scientifically meaningful. The authors introduce a systematic approach that couples model‑compatible task adaptation with linking hypotheses and comparative analysis across candidates. Ultimately, the work aims to guide cognitive scientists in moving from anecdotal fit to rigorous theory‑driven evaluation.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A four‑stage inferential framework—adapting tasks, specifying linking hypotheses, evaluating behavioral correspondence, and conducting contrastive comparison—provides a structured method for assessing FMs as cognitive models.  
- [Finding 2] Behavioral alignment between FM outputs and human measures is generally weak unless tied to clear theoretical expectations that map model behavior onto developmental milestones.  
- [Finding 3] Theory‑diagnostic tasks are essential; without them, alignment claims remain speculative rather than diagnostic.

## Methodology  
The authors adopt a pragmatic experimental design: first, existing cognitive experiments are reformulated into formats compatible with FM outputs (e.g., token‑level predictions). Next, they formulate linking hypotheses that explicitly connect each model output to a human behavioral measure. The framework then evaluates whether observed behavioral correspondence holds across participants or models and finally conducts systematic comparisons among alternative candidate models or training regimes. Constraints such as data sparsity and task equivalence are discussed, but the core methodology remains a step‑wise, hypothesis‑driven pipeline.

## Results  
Empirical tests reveal that many FMs produce outputs that loosely resemble human performance on simple tasks but fail to capture nuanced developmental patterns when only raw behavioral fit is considered. When theory‑diagnostic tasks—such as measuring early symbolic reasoning or memory capacity—are introduced, a subset of FMs (e.g., smaller, task‑tuned models) align more closely with childlike performance on specific cognitive domains. Contrastive analysis shows that model capacity varies dramatically: larger, generalist models often overfit to adult benchmarks while smaller, domain‑specific variants better reflect developmental trajectories.

## Significance  
This work clarifies the criteria for treating FMs as explanatory cognitive models, preventing premature anthropomorphization of AI outputs. By insisting on explicit linking hypotheses and theory‑diagnostic tasks, it encourages cognitive scientists to adopt a more disciplined, comparative approach that can generate genuine insights rather than superficial fit. The framework also offers a common language for interdisciplinary dialogue between AI researchers and cognitive psychologists.
