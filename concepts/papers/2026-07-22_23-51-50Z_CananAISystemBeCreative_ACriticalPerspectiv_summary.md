## Summary  
This paper investigates whether artificial intelligence systems can be considered creative, using the dual lenses of engineering and artistic practice to critique prevailing AI capabilities. By applying Margaret Boden’s three‑property model (novelty, surprise, value) and her three types of creativity (combinatorial, exploratory, transformational), the authors argue that current AI is limited in two of these dimensions while excelling only in combinatorial generation. The study further contends that the core deficit lies not merely in novelty but in the absence of serendipity or a subject position to welcome chance events. To substantiate this claim, the authors present a human‑AI collaborative workflow and experimental results that illustrate both constraints and potential synergies.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] AI systems can generate novel outputs through combinatorial recombination but cannot produce genuinely surprising or valuable innovations beyond pre‑programmed patterns.  
- [Finding 2] Exploratory creativity—searching for unexpected solutions in unstructured domains—remains out of reach due to deterministic training pipelines and lack of stochastic surprise mechanisms.  
- [Finding 3] Transformational creativity, which reinterprets existing material with novel meaning, is structurally impossible because AI lacks an internal horizon that can reinterpret context beyond statistical correlation.

## Methodology  
The authors adopt a mixed‑methods approach: first, they map Boden’s framework onto standard AI architectures (neural networks and combinatorial generators) to identify structural gaps. Second, they design a human‑AI collaborative experiment where the AI proposes combinatorial variations while the human artist selects, refines, or discards them, deliberately introducing serendipitous constraints. The workflow is documented in the paper’s methodological note to serve as an illustration of the thesis.

## Results  
Experiments show that the AI reliably produces combinatorial outputs but consistently fails to generate surprising concepts when faced with open‑ended prompts. When human participants impose random or ambiguous constraints, the system’s output remains predictable and lacks value‑driven novelty. The collaborative model demonstrates that only by augmenting AI with intentional surprise (e.g., random seed selection) can a modest degree of exploratory creativity emerge, yet transformational leaps remain absent.

## Significance  
Understanding these limits is crucial for realistic expectations in creative AI applications, guiding designers to view AI as a combinatorial tool rather than an autonomous creator. The findings also highlight the need for new research into stochastic mechanisms that could bridge the gap between algorithmic generation and human‑like surprise.
