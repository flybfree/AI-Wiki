## Summary  
The paper tackles the paradox that a team’s ball‑possession statistics often mislead broadcasters by counting “dead” circulation as valuable. It introduces an event‑based junk‑possession index that flags low‑threat, sterile possessions and complements it with a Space‑Creation Index derived from video analysis to distinguish space‑creating from dead possession. The dual‑layer framework is evaluated on 31 flagged windows from nine World Cup matches, showing a strong negative link between the junk flag and match outcomes. This work moves beyond traditional on‑ball value models by explicitly separating space‑creating but unconverted actions from purely circulatory ones.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The junk‑possession index correlates negatively with points (r = –0.37) and xG difference (r = –0.51), indicating that low‑threat, tied or losing possessions are less likely to produce scoring opportunities than high‑threat ones.  
- [Finding 2] In a same‑match regression with team offensive VAEP held constant, the junk flag adds significant explanatory power (p < 0.0001), showing that on‑ball value alone cannot capture the full impact of off‑ball circulation.  
- [Finding 3] Video‑derived Space‑Creation Index reveals that 74 % of flagged windows are spatially dead, 19 % weak progression, and only 6 % actually create space, even when possession is high (e.g., 73 % on penalties).

## Methodology  
The authors reconstruct each ball‑possession sequence from live event data. First, they compute a peak threat gain using an expected‑threat grid; possessions that reach low‑threat thresholds in tied or losing states are flagged as “junk.” Second, for the flagged windows, broadcast video is projected onto pitch coordinates to calculate a Space‑Creation Index (SCI), which measures net change in pitch control—positive SCI indicates space creation, negative indicates dead possession. The two metrics are applied across 31 of 35 flagged windows from nine World Cup matches.

## Results  
The junk flag exhibits a strong negative correlation with both points and xG difference across the dataset (r = –0.37; r = –0.51). Descriptive regression shows that, holding team offensive VAEP constant, the junk flag predicts outcomes better than VAEP alone (p < 0.0001) and adds information beyond on‑ball value models. Of the 31 windows, 74 % are non‑space‑creating, 19 % weak progression, and 6 % space‑creating; even high‑possession windows such as penalty‑side possessions often score as junk.

## Significance  
Broadcast football relies heavily on possession numbers that obscure whether the ball is actually advancing or merely circulating. By separating space‑creating from dead possession, this research provides a more accurate metric for evaluating tactical value, informing coaching decisions and improving viewer understanding of game dynamics.
