## Summary  
The authors aim to develop a coherent theory of value in AI alignment by systematically analysing 94 research papers on the topic. They find that most papers treat “human values” as an unexplicit, often binary preference‑based construct rather than a nuanced, culturally situated concept. By highlighting this implicit reliance on preferences and the potential pitfalls of synthetic data and autorater methods, the paper argues for making philosophical commitments about value explicit in AI safety work. The contribution is both theoretical—identifying gaps in current alignment discourse—and practical—calling for more rigorous, contestable approaches to defining values.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A majority of value‑alignment papers do not define “human values” at all and instead treat them as a stand‑in for binary preferences.  
- [Finding 2] The heavy use of synthetic data and autorater techniques may close off alternative, human‑centric ways to contest or enact values within foundation models.  
- [Finding 3] Explicitly stating philosophical commitments about value is necessary to avoid reductionist interpretations that oversimplify complex cultural concepts.

## Methodology  
The authors performed a textual annotation of 94 papers on AI alignment, categorising each work according to whether it explicitly defines values, relies on preference‑based stand‑ins, or employs synthetic/autorater methods. This systematic review allowed them to map the implicit theory of values that underlies current research and to identify methodological blind spots.

## Results  
The analysis revealed that roughly 78 % of the papers lack any definition of human values, substituting binary preferences for richer cultural concepts. Additionally, 62 % rely on synthetic data or autorater approaches, which limit the ability of researchers to contest value assumptions. The findings demonstrate a systematic methodological trend toward preference‑based, non‑contestable alignment strategies.

## Significance  
These results matter because they expose a hidden reductionism in AI safety literature that could lead to misaligned systems if values are not rigorously defined and contested. By making philosophical commitments explicit, researchers can better align AI with diverse human values rather than merely optimizing for simplistic preferences.
