## Summary  
This paper investigates how blind and low‑vision (BLV) scientists as well as sighted researchers use AI‑driven question‑answering tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini to explore multimodal scientific papers that contain figures, tables, and diagrams. The authors aim to characterize existing practices—including accessibility workarounds—and identify factors that lead participants to abandon these workflows when AI responses are vague or inaccurate. By conducting semi‑structured interviews with ten scientists across diverse STEM fields and collecting a dataset of 115 real‑world queries and model outputs, the study bridges qualitative insight with quantitative evidence on user experience. The contribution is both conceptual (a taxonomy of visual QA practices) and practical (a curated interaction corpus for future research).  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Blind and low‑vision scientists often rely on static alternative text while AI QA provides an interactive, dynamic way to explore images, yet many still depend on the limited alt‑text rather than the full visual content.  
- [Finding 2] Vague or incomplete image descriptions from AI models cause both BLV and sighted participants to abandon the workflow, highlighting a critical need for precise multimodal grounding.  
- [Finding 3] The dataset of 115 queries and responses demonstrates that accessibility workarounds (e.g., reading alt‑text first) are frequently combined with AI assistance, revealing distinct but complementary strategies across abilities.  

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem through a mixed‑methods design: they conducted ten in‑depth interviews—five participants who are blind or low‑vision and five sighted scientists from different STEM disciplines (physics, biology, computer science). Each participant was asked to use ChatGPT and Gemini to answer questions about multimodal papers, while simultaneously describing their workflow. The researchers recorded every query issued, the AI’s response, and any follow‑up actions taken by participants. From these interactions a dataset of 115 queries and model outputs was extracted for analysis.  

## Results  
Analysis revealed that BLV scientists frequently start with static alt‑text before engaging AI, whereas sighted scientists scan images directly. When AI responses were unclear or factually incorrect, both groups reported frustration and stopped the task. The dataset shows a high rate of “abandonment” (≈40 % of queries) linked to poor image description quality. Moreover, BLV participants used more explicit accessibility steps (e.g., confirming alt‑text), while sighted users relied on visual cues only.  

## Significance  
These findings underscore the importance of designing AI‑powered multimodal QA systems that are both accurate and accessible across abilities. By integrating precise image grounding with robust alternative‑text support, researchers can reduce abandonment rates and promote equitable scientific exploration. The study also provides a reusable interaction corpus that can inform future accessibility testing and model evaluation in STEM contexts.
