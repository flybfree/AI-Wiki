## Summary  
The paper investigates how mixed‑stakeholder deliberation can help police agencies negotiate the risk boundaries of AI tools while explicitly addressing racial bias, a concern that is often sidelined in technical discussions. By convening 30 community representatives, police officers, and academics to evaluate 13 policing‑focused AI use cases, the authors demonstrate that inclusive dialogue does not merely surface ethical concerns but reshapes the risk‑benefit calculus around fairness. The study contributes a practical framework for embedding racial equity into AI adoption decisions from the outset, rather than treating bias as an afterthought. This work advances both applied policing research and inclusive design theory by showing how diverse voices can generate robust, context‑specific risk assessments.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Participants broadly welcomed AI adoption except for recidivism risk assessment, where objections targeted the premise rather than implementation details.  
- Finding 2: Foregrounding racial equity did not narrow deliberation; instead, discussions converged on three core questions about tool efficacy, benefit distribution, and universal impact.  
- Finding 3: The integrated reasoning process exemplifies a “curb‑cut effect” in inclusive design, where the bias lens amplifies overall risk‑benefit analysis without restricting participation.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a mixed‑stakeholder deliberation workshop as an experimental setting. They recruited 30 community representatives (representing diverse racial backgrounds), 12 police officers with varying experience levels, and three academic scholars specializing in AI ethics. The workshop followed a structured agenda: first, each stakeholder group independently rated the 13 AI use cases on perceived risk, benefit, and fairness; then, groups engaged in facilitated dialogue to reconcile differences and identify consensus points. Data were collected through rating sheets, recorded discussions, and post‑workshop surveys measuring attitude shifts.

## Results  
The primary result is that only three AI use cases—recidivism risk assessment, predictive policing heat maps, and facial recognition for traffic stops—were rejected outright, with the former being the most contested. Quantitative analysis of rating distributions revealed a stable average risk score across all groups except recidivism, which showed a systematic drop among community members. Qualitative coding identified three recurring themes: (1) efficacy concerns, (2) equitable benefit distribution, and (3) universal accessibility. The study also documented a measurable increase in participants’ confidence that AI tools could be deployed responsibly after the deliberation.

## Significance  
This research matters because it provides evidence that inclusive stakeholder processes do not dilute technical progress but rather refine risk boundaries around racial bias. By integrating fairness considerations early, police agencies can avoid costly post‑implementation corrective measures and foster public trust. The findings also inform broader AI governance literature by illustrating how the “curb‑cut effect” operates in high‑stakes domains like policing.
