## Summary  
This paper investigates the linguistic and narrative differences between British and American audio description (AD) in film, aiming to provide a quantitative comparison of how each region’s AD conventions shape storytelling. By testing hypotheses derived from regional guidelines and practitioner insight against a corpus of 206 movies with human‑authored AD texts, the authors demonstrate that systematic variations exist across lexicon, aspectual choice, syntactic patterns, character naming, scene cueing, and overlap with dialogue or music. The study therefore offers a rigorous, data‑driven view of how cultural norms influence an otherwise functional accessibility practice.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** British AD employs a richer set of subjective adjectives and modifiers than American AD, reflecting regional lexical preferences.  
- **Finding 2:** Progressive aspect markers are used more frequently in UK descriptions, whereas US texts favor simple present/past constructions.  
- **Finding 3:** Passive voice appears significantly less in British AD, while both regions differ in the frequency of character name usage and scene cueing conventions.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed a comparative corpus containing human‑authored audio description for each of 206 movies from both the United Kingdom and the United States. They applied region‑specific AD guidelines and consulted an industry practitioner to formulate testable hypotheses about lexical, syntactic, and narrative differences. The hypotheses were evaluated by counting occurrences of relevant linguistic features across the two corpora.

## Results  
Quantitative analysis confirmed that all hypotheses held: UK AD texts contained 27 % more subjective adjectives, used progressive aspect in 41 % of scenes versus 30 % in US AD; passive constructions were 68 % less common in the UK set (p < .01); character names were named 19 % fewer times in British scripts; scene cues employed a distinct cueing pattern with 22 % higher overlap to dialogue/music in the US corpus.

## Significance  
These findings provide a quantitative lens into how cultural norms shape audio description, an essential tool for blind and visually impaired audiences. By quantifying regional variation, the study can inform guideline revisions, improve consistency across productions, and ultimately enhance accessibility quality.
