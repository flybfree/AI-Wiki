## Summary  
Arabizi is the practice of writing Arabic using Latin script, and this paper investigates how different Arabic dialects perceive and use it. The study collects speaker perceptions across five major dialect communities—Algerian, Egyptian, Lebanese, Moroccan, and Tunisian—and examines both lexical transliteration patterns and sentence‑level recognition abilities. By releasing two new resources, the authors provide the most comprehensive human‑centered cross‑dialectal analysis of Arabizi to date.

## Key Contributions  
- Systematic intra‑dialectal regularity in Arabic word transliterations across the five dialects.  
- Inter‑dialectal variation that reflects distinct linguistic norms and preferences for Arabizi usage.  
- Demonstration that speakers can reliably detect Arabizi at the sentence level, indicating a functional awareness of the stylistic form.

## Methodology  
The authors conducted surveys with native speakers from each dialect community to gather insights on their attitudes toward Arabizi. They then compiled two datasets: (1) a character‑level alignment mapping Arabic words to their Latin transliterations based on participants’ responses, and (2) a manually curated parallel corpus pairing sentences written in Arabic script with multiple Arabizi variants collected from the same speakers.

## Results  
The analysis revealed that within each dialect, transliteration patterns follow regular rules, showing intra‑dialectal consistency. Across dialects, however, systematic differences emerged—such as varying vowel choices and consonant handling—that constitute inter‑dialectal variation. Additionally, participants performed well on tasks to identify Arabizi sentences among Arabic script, confirming the ability to perceive this stylistic form.

## Significance  
This research moves beyond viewing Arabizi as a temporary technical limitation and instead treats it as a recognized linguistic phenomenon with measurable usage patterns. The released resources will aid NLP systems that need to handle mixed scripts and support scholars studying dialectal writing practices.
