## Summary  
The paper tackles the limited availability of tools for corpus‑assisted linguistic analysis of colour terms in literary texts, proposing a solution through the creation of parallel multidimensional chromatic lexicons for Russian and English. These lexicons catalogue not only lexical items but also visual descriptors classified by hue, saturation, and temperature, enabling systematic comparison across languages. The study demonstrates that such an approach can reveal statistically significant differences in colour‑related language use between two canonical corpora. Its main contribution is a transparent, reusable procedure for building multilingual chromatic lexicons that can be applied to any corpus‑based linguistic investigation.

## Key Contributions  
- Constructed parallel multidimensional chromatic lexicons: 224 Russian entries and 141 English entries, each annotated with hue, saturation, and temperature.  
- Demonstrated that confirmed chromatic terms appear 3.4 times more frequently in the Andrei Bely poetry corpus than in the Emily Dickinson corpus.  
- Provided a transparent, reusable methodology for constructing and applying multilingual chromatic lexicons to corpus‑assisted linguistic analysis.

## Methodology  
The authors sourced colour vocabulary from specialised lexical resources and existing research literature, then compared the two language inventories to identify overlapping terms. Manual verification was performed on translated candidates, taking into account language‑specific morphological features such as gender agreement in Russian. The lexicons also classified entries by three dimensions: hue (e.g., red, blue), saturation (full vs. muted), and temperature (warm vs. cool). A pilot study examined two corpora of poetry—Andrei Bely’s 20,373‑token text and Emily Dickinson’s 28,479‑token text—to retrieve colour terms in context. All matches were classified as Confirmed_chromatic, Ambiguous_visual, or Excluded. The analysis proceeded in two stages: a strict analysis using only confirmed chromatic lexis, followed by a sensitivity analysis that included ambiguous cases to assess the impact on findings.

## Results  
The quantitative results show marked differences across several dimensions. Confirmed chromatic terms occurred 3.4 times more often in Bely’s corpus than Dickinson’s, indicating a higher lexical reliance on explicit colour vocabulary. The classification system also revealed that visual descriptors (e.g., “shimmering,” “faded”) were more frequent in Dickinson’s text, while hue and temperature classifications highlighted distinct stylistic preferences. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the inclusion of ambiguous cases did not alter the primary conclusion about frequency differences.

## Significance  
This work matters because it bridges a methodological gap between corpus linguistics and colour semantics, offering researchers a concrete tool for studying how authors encode visual perception through language. By providing a reproducible procedure, the study enhances transparency in lexical analysis and can be adapted to other multilingual corpora, thereby enriching literary and psycholinguistic research on colour representation.
