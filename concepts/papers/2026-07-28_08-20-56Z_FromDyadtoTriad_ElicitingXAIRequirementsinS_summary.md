## Summary  
The paper addresses the challenge of eliciting explainable AI (XAI) requirements from stroke survivors and caregivers in rehabilitation, where direct communication difficulties limit participants’ ability to articulate preferences about algorithmic transparency. It introduces a video‑based scaffolding protocol designed to elicit XAI needs despite acquired communication disorders. The study employs four specific scaffolding techniques and identifies facilitator biases that shape responses. This work contributes a reusable methodological framework that positions XAI elicitation as a prerequisite for trustworthy brain‑computer interface design rather than an optional step.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The video‑based scaffolding protocol successfully surfaces heterogeneous XAI needs across stroke survivors and caregivers, revealing both consistent demands (e.g., transparency of decision pathways) and conflicting preferences (e.g., preference for simple binary feedback vs. detailed analogical explanations).  
- [Finding 2] Three systematic facilitation biases—normative bias, hypothesis‑confirmation bias, and presence effect—were identified as procedural risks that can distort participants’ articulation of XAI requirements.  
- [Finding 3] The protocol constitutes a reusable methodological contribution for eliciting patient‑facing XAI requirements in rehabilitation, positioning it as a prerequisite rather than an optional step.

## Methodology  
The authors conducted a formative study with three stroke survivors (two with moderate‑to‑severe aphasia) and three caregivers. Participants watched video demonstrations of brain‑computer interface algorithms while facilitators applied four scaffolding approaches: analogical bridging, projective personas, binary forcing, and extended response time. The protocol was designed to reduce cognitive load and depersonalise sensitive topics, allowing participants to articulate preferences about algorithmic transparency.

## Results  
The study uncovered a rich set of XAI requirements that varied by participant and caregiver, including demands for clear decision pathways, minimal jargon, and real‑time feedback. Reflexive analysis identified the three facilitation biases as systematic influences on responses, with normative bias leading participants to align answers with facilitator expectations, hypothesis‑confirmation bias causing them to echo suggested explanations, and presence effect prompting overly detailed or vague answers when they sensed being observed.

## Significance  
Understanding these requirements is critical for designing brain‑computer interfaces that stroke survivors can trust. By providing a validated scaffolding protocol and risk guidelines, the work bridges the gap between AI transparency research and clinical implementation, ensuring that rehabilitation technologies are co‑created with users’ actual needs rather than theoretical assumptions.
