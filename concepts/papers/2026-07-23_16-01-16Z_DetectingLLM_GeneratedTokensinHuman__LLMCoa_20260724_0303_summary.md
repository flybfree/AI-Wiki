## Summary  
The rapid growth of human‑AI collaborative writing has created a demand for methods that can pinpoint which parts of mixed‑authorship documents are likely generated by large language models (LLMs). This paper proposes a token‑level detection framework that smooths adjacent token scores and selects an adaptive bandwidth using a Lepski‑type rule, achieving favorable mean‑square error performance without requiring labeled training data. The authors demonstrate strong empirical results on both synthetic datasets and a realistic human‑LLM coauthored corpus.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** A token‑level detection method that smooths adjacent scores to reduce variability in the underlying signal.  
- **Finding 2:** An adaptive Lepski‑type rule that dynamically chooses bandwidth based on local authorship structure, improving robustness across varying context lengths.  
- **Finding 3:** The ability to implement the detector without token‑level labeled data, making it simple and scalable for real‑time use.

## Methodology  
The authors operate at the token level—the basic unit of modern language models—by extending existing token‑level detection scores. Their approach first smooths each score with its neighboring tokens to mitigate random fluctuations. Then they apply an adaptive Lepski rule that computes a bandwidth per local segment, adjusting it according to the observed pattern of human versus LLM authorship within that segment. The entire pipeline is offline and does not involve training on labeled token pairs; thus, no annotated data are needed.

## Results  
Theoretically, the authors characterize the trade‑off between smoothing strength and bandwidth adaptivity, showing that the proposed balance yields a low mean‑square error in estimating the true underlying signal. Empirically, their method outperforms several strong baselines on both synthetic datasets (e.g., random human‑LLM mixtures) and a realistic dataset of coauthored scientific papers. The implementation is publicly available via a website hosted by the authors.

## Significance  
Fine‑grained detection of LLM‑generated tokens enables transparency, plagiarism checking, and quality control in collaborative writing environments where authorship is shared between humans and AI systems. By providing per‑token confidence scores, the method supports downstream tasks such as editing suggestions, content moderation, and research integrity verification.
