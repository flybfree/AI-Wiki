## Summary  
The paper investigates preverbal uninflected and underived roots in Mapudungun, focusing on the element *wuno* ‘return/re‑’ to critique existing grammatical classifications such as auxiliaries (Smeets, 2008), modal prefixes (Longkon, 2011) and complex verb stems (Zúñiga, 2006). It demonstrates three distinct profiles of these elements across a four‑century diachronic corpus (1606–present) and shows that V2 attestations confirm *kim* and *kupa* are full lexical verbs. The authors propose a prosodic‑orthographic hypothesis linking fossilized pauses to syntactic boundaries, arguing for Zúñiga’s radical concatenation as the correct grammatical model. This work has implications for typology of languages lacking pre‑contact written tradition.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Preverbal uninflected roots display stable V1 compounds (kim, shinge) and volatile forms reflecting orthographic shift (pepi, wuno).  
- [Finding 2] V2 attestations confirm *kim* and *kupa* are full lexical verbs.  
- [Finding 3] The fossilized prosodic pauses were reanalysed by speakers as syntactic boundaries, supporting Zúñiga’s radical concatenation as the grammatical model.  

## Methodology  
The authors employed a diachronic corpus analysis of Mapudungun texts from 1606 to the present, cross‑referencing orthographic forms with historical records and conducting speaker interviews. They compared these findings against three scholarly classifications—auxiliaries (Smeets, 2008), modal prefixes (Longkon, 2011) and complex verb stems (Zúñiga, 2006)—to identify systematic gaps in the literature.  

## Results  
The analysis reveals three distinct profiles of preverbal uninflected and underived roots: stable V1 compounds that remain constant over time; volatile forms whose orthographic spelling fluctuates due to phonological change; and a true particle (*kalli*) that never varies. Crucially, the presence of V2 forms for *kim* and *kupa* demonstrates their status as full lexical verbs rather than auxiliary or prefixal elements. The authors argue that apparent “variable binding” is the fossilization of prosodic pauses transcribed by early missionaries as spaces, later reanalysed as syntactic boundaries.  

## Significance  
This study matters because it challenges conventional typological frameworks for languages without pre‑contact written records, offering a concrete model for reconstructing grammatical structures from orthographic evidence. By linking prosody to syntax through the lens of radical concatenation, it illuminates how early missionaries’ transcription conventions may have misinterpreted native phonological processes. The findings also provide methodological guidance for analyzing similar cases in other typologically complex languages.
