## Summary  
The paper argues that AI is excelling at physics prediction but moving away from deep theory building, a trend that reverses the historical trajectory of human discovery. It identifies a critical gap: modern AI models can generate accurate predictions without providing clear theoretical principles or falsifiable hypotheses. The authors review recent AI contributions—from early symbolic regression to AlphaFold and GraphCast—and conclude that equipping AI with hypothesis‑generation skills could enable paradigm‑level breakthroughs such as quantum gravity.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A reversal in the direction of AI’s physics impact: from explicit equation discovery to powerful predictive models lacking theoretical grounding.  
- [Finding 2] The absence of a core skill—posing the right questions, inventing new principles, and designing falsifiable tests—that has driven historic paradigm shifts.  
- [Finding 3] A proposal that AI systems need to be trained not only in data‑driven prediction but also in theory‑building processes to move beyond incremental improvements.  

## Methodology  
The authors conducted a literature review of AI applications in physics, categorizing them chronologically and contrasting them with the historical progression of human discovery. They identified patterns, highlighted the shift from symbolic regression (early hypothesis generation) to purely predictive models (AlphaFold, GraphCast), and formulated a conceptual framework for the missing skill set.  

## Results  
The review demonstrates that early AI breakthroughs were theory‑oriented, while recent frontier work excels at prediction but offers no clear explanatory principle. The authors conclude that this trend suggests AI may remain limited to within existing frameworks unless it acquires hypothesis‑generation capabilities.  

## Significance  
This matters because the ability of AI to propose and test new principles determines whether it can contribute to the next paradigm‑level theories in physics, such as a unified theory of quantum gravity, rather than merely refining current models.
