## Summary  
This paper investigates whether having students evaluate GenAI‑generated algorithmic solutions yields learning outcomes comparable to traditional problem solving in upper‑division algorithms courses. A randomized A/B crossover study was conducted to compare performance and perceived usefulness of evaluation tasks versus direct solution construction. The authors aim to clarify the impact of GenAI‑evaluation activities on both summative grades and student metacognition.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: No statistically significant differences between groups in midterm scores, final exam scores, or overall course grades.  
- Finding 2: Students earned significantly higher homework scores when evaluating GenAI‑generated solutions, but this localized advantage did not translate into downstream summative gains.  
- Finding 3: Survey data show that most students reported no change in study habits; only those who adapted their strategies found the evaluation tasks helpful.

## Methodology  
The authors carried out a randomized A/B crossover experiment with N = 220 junior‑level algorithms students across six assignments. In each cycle, one half of the cohort solved challenging algorithmic problems directly while the other half evaluated often‑flawed GenAI‑generated solutions; after three weeks the roles were reversed to control for order effects. The evaluation component required verification, diagnosis, and judgment tasks rather than constructing a solution from scratch.

## Results  
Midterm and final exam scores were not significantly different between the direct‑solution group (M = 78.4) and the evaluation group (M = 79.1), p > 0.05. Overall course grades showed no advantage for either condition (p = 0.23). Homework scores were higher in the evaluation condition (mean = 86 vs. 84, t(219) = 2.1, p < 0.05), yet this gain did not correlate with later exam performance. Survey responses indicate that 78 % of students reported no change in study habits; among the 22 % who adapted their strategies, the GenAI tasks were rated significantly more helpful (p = 0.004).

## Significance  
These findings suggest that embedding GenAI evaluation can shift student effort toward verification without harming summative learning outcomes, but it does not automatically produce deeper conceptual transfer. The paper argues that meaningful gains require deliberate scaffolding that moves students beyond simple error diagnosis.
