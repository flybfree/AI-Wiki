## Summary  
The paper proposes a new CAPTCHA signal based on the physical timing of GPU rendering rather than visual output, aiming to create an AI‑resistant and privacy‑preserving challenge. By measuring how long WebGL frames take to render on real silicon, the authors can classify devices without exposing any persistent identifier. Their contribution is threefold: (1) a passive signal that evades both behavioral and cryptographic defenses; (2) empirical evidence that headless automation behaves markedly differently from genuine browsers; and (3) a clear quantitative separation between human‑driven rendering and software‑rendered bots on real hardware.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Render‑timing dynamics provide a passive, AI‑resistant CAPTCHA signal that leaks no persistent identifier.  
- [Finding 2] Real browsers exhibit distinct GPU‑timing signatures; headless automation on actual GPUs separates from human samples by 75–106 % in frame jitter, timer‑quantization ratio, and coefficient of variation.  
- [Finding 3] Software‑rendered automation can be distinguished with roughly a five‑fold increase in mean render time compared to genuine GPU rendering.

## Methodology  
The authors deployed a passive attack over twelve hours, issuing 207 unsolicited WebGL requests that logged the timing of each frame. They collected labeled samples through a public endpoint: positive examples came from real browsers (13 distinct GPUs) and negative examples were generated by keyed headless automation across a matrix of render back‑ends. The comparison isolates two execution modes—interactive human use versus automated, software‑rendered bots—to quantify the timing differences.

## Results  
The passive deployment recorded 86 % automated requests; 85 % of browser‑claiming clients failed HTTP header‑consistency checks, indicating strong CAPTCHA efficacy. Positive class data comprised 13 unique GPU instances, while negative class data showed software‑rendered automation dominating the adversary pool. Empirically, mean render time for real GPUs was about five times lower than that of headless bots. In a controlled experiment (same GPU family and browser engine, only interactive vs. headless), human samples were separated from automated ones by 75–106 % on frame jitter, timer‑quantization ratio, and coefficient of variation.

## Significance  
This work introduces a privacy‑preserving CAPTCHA that does not rely on visual puzzles or cryptographic attestation, thereby avoiding enrollment costs and the risk of AI solving them. Because it measures an immutable hardware characteristic rather than observable output, it is resistant to both behavioral evasion and fingerprinting attacks. The findings suggest a scalable, cross‑architecture signal that can be integrated into web services without compromising user experience.
