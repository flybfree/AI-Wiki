## Summary  
This study investigates whether conversational interactions with a large language model (LLM) can diminish the adoption of conspiracy theories that emerge in real‑time after high‑profile events such as assassination attempts. By exposing U.S. adults who initially held conspiratorial views to multi‑turn dialogues with an LLM prompted to counter those beliefs, the authors compare outcomes with participants who either discussed unrelated topics or viewed static fact sheets. The experiments reveal a clear reduction in conspiracy belief among the LLM group and suggest that this benefit may extend to later conspiracies triggered by subsequent crises. The work thus contributes a scalable, cognitively‑focused intervention that can be deployed during periods of societal upheaval.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Conversations with an LLM significantly lower participants’ immediate belief in the unfolding conspiracy relative to control conditions.  
- [Finding 2] The reduction in belief persists, as measured two months later when new crisis events generate fresh conspiracies.  
- [Finding 3] The intervention is scalable and cognitively‑focused, indicating that LLMs can serve as a practical tool for misinformation mitigation.

## Methodology  
The authors recruited U.S. adults (N = 472 in Experiment 1; N = 1035 in Experiment 2) who expressed conspiratorial attitudes toward the July 2024 Donald Trump assassination attempt and the September 2025 Charlie Kirk assassination, respectively. Participants were randomly assigned to three conditions: (a) an LLM‑mediated multi‑turn conversation where prompts explicitly encouraged rationalizing the event without conspiracy framing; (b) a control group discussing a neutral topic with the same LLM; and (c) a static fact‑sheet viewing task. Belief was measured via Likert‑scale items administered before, during, and after each session.

## Results  
Both experimental groups showed lower conspiracy belief scores than controls at the end of the interaction, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.01). The LLM treatment produced a larger effect size (Δ = 0.42) compared to the neutral‑topic control (Δ = 0.18). Follow‑up surveys two months later revealed that individuals who had engaged in the LLM conversation also reported lower susceptibility to subsequent conspiracy narratives, suggesting lasting cognitive benefit.

## Significance  
The findings demonstrate that real‑time, AI‑driven dialogue can counteract the rapid spread of misinformation during crises, offering a low‑cost alternative to traditional media campaigns. By targeting belief formation directly through conversational reasoning, the intervention addresses both immediate and delayed misinformation challenges, potentially reshaping public discourse in high‑stress contexts.

## Related Concepts  
- Conspiracy theories: narratives that attribute extraordinary events to secret, malevolent plots.  
- Large language models (LLMs): AI systems capable of generating human‑like text through deep neural networks.  
- Cognitive bias: systematic patterns of deviation from rational judgment, such as confirmation bias in conspiracy acceptance.  
- Misinformation mitigation: strategies aimed at reducing the spread and impact of false or misleading information.
