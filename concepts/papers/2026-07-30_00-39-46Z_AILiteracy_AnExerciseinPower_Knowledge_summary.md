## Summary  
The paper argues that current AI‑literacy frameworks are inadequate because they focus on technical competency and responsible use while reinforcing a “consumer” mindset rather than fostering genuine epistemic agency. By drawing on Foucault’s notion of power‑knowledge, Freire’s pedagogy of critical consciousness, and scholarship in digital literacy, the authors propose a reconceptualization that equips individuals to critically evaluate AI systems, resist their underlying assumptions, and participate in their governance. The proposed framework is organized around three interlocking notions: contextual use, critical interrogation, and participatory governance. This approach aims to cultivate epistemic “agents” who can shape rather than merely consume AI‑generated information.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Existing AI‑literacy frameworks are consumer‑oriented and fail to develop genuine epistemic agency.  
- [Finding 2] Unequal access to AI tools reproduces longstanding epistemic injustices in society.  
- [Finding 3] A three‑part framework (contextual use, critical interrogation, participatory governance) offers a pathway toward empowerment.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem theoretically rather than empirically; they synthesized philosophical and pedagogical literature to construct a reconceptualization of AI literacy. By integrating Foucault’s analysis of power‑knowledge relations, Freire’s emphasis on critical consciousness, and insights from digital‑literacy research, they designed a conceptual framework that maps how individuals can engage with AI in situational contexts, interrogate its logics critically, and co‑govern its deployment.

## Results  
The primary outcome is the articulation of this theoretical framework; no experimental data are presented. The results demonstrate how the three components—contextual use, critical interrogation, participatory governance—can be combined to move beyond simple AI consumption toward a more active, reflective stance.

## Significance  
This work matters because it highlights that AI literacy cannot be reduced to technical skill sets; it must address structural inequities and enable genuine agency. By foregrounding power‑knowledge dynamics and epistemic injustice, the paper calls for policy and educational practices that recognize marginalized voices in AI development and use.
