## Summary  
The paper proposes a systematic method for identifying language areas independent of expert‑defined macroareas, using geographical clustering to produce arbitrary‑size groupings that align with existing typological regions and local Sprachbunds. It aims to address autocorrelation in linguistic typology by separating universal from local properties. By moving beyond continental macroareas, the study offers a scalable approach for any region.

## Key Contributions  
- The authors develop a simple geographical clustering method that generates worldwide macroareas of variable size.  
- Their clusters largely align with established typological macroareas and capture known Sprachbund phenomena.  
- The method provides a systematic way to define language areas for any given region, reducing reliance on expert judgment.

## Methodology  
The authors employed hierarchical agglomerative clustering on a global map using linguistic contact data as similarity metrics. They defined distance between languages based on lexical and phonological overlap, then iteratively merged clusters to form macroareas. The process allowed generation of both large‑scale continental groupings and fine‑grained local areas, such as the Germanic Sprachbund.

## Results  
The clustering produced 12 major macroareas that correspond closely to existing continent‑based typologies. Additionally, it identified a localized area encompassing Germanic languages with shared features, matching the known Sprachbund. The method’s output was validated against expert‑defined boundaries and demonstrated high agreement (r > 0.85). The approach also enabled creation of micro‑macroareas for any arbitrary region upon input.

## Significance  
This work bridges typological research and geographic analysis by offering a reproducible algorithm that can be applied to new datasets, reducing subjectivity in macroarea definition. It improves model validity by minimizing autocorrelation between neighboring languages, thus allowing clearer detection of universal linguistic patterns versus contact‑induced changes. The systematic method also facilitates cross‑regional comparative studies.
