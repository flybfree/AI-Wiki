## Summary  
The paper investigates how linguistic construction choices affect large language model (LLM) stance judgments and seeks to locate the causal impact of these changes within the model’s architecture. By extending a political‑statement dataset with six controlled rewrite types that either preserve or invert meaning, the authors test whether such constructions systematically shift LLM decisions and identify which internal components can restore the original stance distribution.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Linguistic construction (rather than merely lexical realization) can systematically shift LLM stance judgments.  
- [Finding 2] Both meaning‑preserving and meaning‑inverting rewrites cause instability in the model’s stance predictions.  
- [Finding 3] Mid‑to‑late decoder layers, especially block outputs at the final prompt position, provide the strongest restoration signal when using activation patching.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed six controlled rewrite types that preserve or invert the original statement’s meaning. They applied these rewrites to four open‑weight LLMs and measured stance predictions on each. To isolate the causal effect of construction, they employed activation patching: activations generated by the forward pass for the original statement were substituted into the forward pass for the rewritten statement, allowing a direct comparison of output distributions.

## Results  
The experiments demonstrate that stance instability occurs across all six rewrite types, indicating that linguistic construction matters. Activation patching reveals that later decoder layers—particularly those at the final prompt position—are most responsible for restoring the original stance distribution, while earlier layers contribute comparatively little. This localization suggests that the model’s stance inference is concentrated in these specific components.

## Significance  
Understanding where linguistic choices influence LLM reasoning helps design more robust and interpretable models. It also highlights the need for construction‑aware training or prompting strategies to mitigate unintended shifts in model outputs.
