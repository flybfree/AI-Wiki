## Summary  
This paper addresses a fundamental challenge in training long-horizon coding agents: the mismatch between agent trajectories and the credit units available for supervised learning. The authors introduce *collection-time semantic self-segmentation*, a novel framework where agents declare their own boundaries during trajectory generation, enabling precise segmentation of meaningful phases rather than relying on fixed or retrospective heuristics. By treating these declared segments as training targets, the method generates high-quality, hypothesis-grounded supervision that improves downstream model performance without requiring external labels or complex post-processing.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The framework enables agent-declared boundaries to serve as explicit training units, producing four supervised targets per collection: the correct segment, a falsified boundary, and two control segments derived from failed hypothesis corrections. This creates richly annotated data that capture both positive and negative learning signals.  
- [Finding 2] When only cut points are provided without the original hypothesis, a model can still attribute action blocks to their governing hypotheses with over twice chance accuracy (paired sign test $p = 0.0002$), demonstrating that the boundary itself encodes semantic structure even in isolation.  
- [Finding 3] Human annotators and mechanical rule-based systems consistently outperform random placement when segmenting trajectories, confirming that the declared boundaries are coherent, non-trivial, and not merely artifacts of labeling noise.

## Methodology  
The authors generate coding-agent trajectories where each agent self-declares its own logical or functional boundaries during execution. These declarations are falsifiable and tied to specific causal hypotheses about task progress. The system then creates four training targets per trajectory: the actual correct segment, a deliberately broken boundary (wrong cause), and two control segments derived from failed hypothesis corrections. This protocol avoids milestone vocabulary, teacher logits, environment replay, or retrospective segmentation, instead relying solely on agent-declared events as ground truth.

## Results  
Downstream Distributional Policy Optimization (DPO) on 2,551 phase-boundary pairs shows no change in decisions on 91 adversarial held-out items, while four of 60 change only on matched-construction items—all from wrong to right. Crucially, with 1,825 pairs generated by a single generator, the variable that affects performance is corpus diversity, not boundary placement, indicating robustness beyond boundary quality. A code-blind annotator matches boundaries correctly in 24 of 40 cases (60%), versus only 11.5% random chance, and mechanical rule-based systems fail to beat chance at either end of a strict-to-permissive sweep.

## Significance  
This work redefines how we segment long-horizon agent trajectories for training, shifting from retrospective or environment-driven heuristics to proactive, hypothesis-anchored segmentation. By making boundaries explicit and falsifiable during generation, the method produces interpretable, high-quality supervision that enhances model generalization without external labels. It also reveals that boundary coherence is not trivial—it requires genuine semantic structure—and provides a scalable protocol for training agents in complex, long-horizon environments.
