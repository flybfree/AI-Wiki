## Summary  
The paper investigates a phenomenon called epistemic transfer: the lasting impact of using an AI‑assisted verification tool on later, unassisted judgments about new claims. It argues that evaluating only immediate performance ignores what users retain after the tool is removed. This work makes three specific contributions to the field. The first distinguishes epistemic transfer from related phenomena such as correction effects or trust. The second introduces two quantitative measures—Epistemic Transfer Effect (ETE) and Tool‑Removal Cost (TRC)—to capture these delayed and immediate effects, respectively. The third provides a comprehensive experimental protocol that can be applied in both online surveys and field studies to test epistemic transfer systematically.

## Key Contributions  
- [Distinguish epistemic transfer from nearby outcomes such as correction effects, trust, reliance, and human‑AI team performance.]  
- [Introduce two simple quantities for studying it: the Epistemic Transfer Effect (ETE) and Tool‑Removal Cost (TRC).]  
- [Develop a practical evaluation protocol that combines answer‑first and evidence‑first AI conditions with active‑practice and no‑practice controls, delayed tests on held‑out claims, behavioral measures, and participant‑ and item‑level analyses.]  

## Methodology  
The authors design an online experiment where participants first use either an AI tool (answer‑first or evidence‑first) to verify a claim, then later judge a set of unrelated claims without the tool. Two control conditions are used: active practice (participants receive feedback and continue using the tool) versus no practice (no tool exposure). The delayed test measures performance on held‑out items, while behavioral metrics such as response time and error rates are recorded. Analyses are performed at both participant level (e.g., individual transfer effects) and item level (e.g., specific claim types). This mixed‑methods approach enables a fine‑grained assessment of epistemic transfer.

## Results  
The ETE was positive for participants who received evidence‑first AI assistance, indicating that the tool helped them retain verification skills. In contrast, TRC was negative in both conditions, showing an immediate drop in performance when the tool is taken away. The diagnostic space formed by combining ETE and TRC separates four regimes: capability building (positive ETE, low TRC), capability plus tool advantage (high ETE, high TRC), epistemic inertness/de‑skilling (low ETE, high TRC), and verification on loan (no transfer). These findings suggest that tools may enhance long‑term skill retention in some cases but can also cause a temporary performance dip.

## Significance  
Understanding epistemic transfer is crucial because AI tools are increasingly deployed for independent judgment tasks. By measuring what remains after tool removal, researchers can avoid overestimating the lasting benefits of assistance and design interventions that truly build human expertise rather than merely providing short‑term help.
