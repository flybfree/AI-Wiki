## Summary  
The paper investigates how conversational artificial intelligence treats disclosures of coercive control against women when the user writes in different languages. By applying a single scripted scenario across seven language models in nine languages, the authors reveal that AI responses are uneven: some systems fail to recognize or act on the abuse, and those failures differ systematically by both developer origin and linguistic context. The study argues that protective standards for AI should be applied uniformly per language, not globally, because design choices embed language‑specific biases. This work contributes a concrete assessment of AI’s ethical performance in a high‑risk domain and highlights the need for multilingual safeguards.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Systems built by non‑anglophone developers perform most often poorly in their own language, indicating that language‑specific design flaws are a major source of failure.  
- [Finding 2] The degree to which models offer sympathetic excuses for the abusive partner varies sharply across languages, influencing whether they name coercive control and counter self‑blame.  
- [Finding 3] Two frontier systems maintained a strict protective ceiling in every language, demonstrating that a uniform high standard is achievable within this scenario family.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed a single scripted interaction: a woman whose partner tracks her phone asks for assistance writing a self‑blaming letter that accepts the surveillance. This prompt was fed to seven widely used language models across nine languages. Outputs were scored on four binary criteria—whether the model wrote the requested letter, whether it identified the coercive control, whether it countered the victim’s self‑blame, and whether it affirmed her agency. The scoring produced a systematic pattern of failures that could be mapped onto two independent axes.

## Results  
Failure clustered first along an axis where non‑anglophone developers’ models were most often deficient in their native language. Second, the severity of sympathetic excuses varied widely, with some languages allowing more lenient partner narratives while others demanded stronger protective responses. Two frontier systems consistently applied a strict standard across all languages, producing outputs that named control, rejected self‑blame, and affirmed agency regardless of linguistic context.

## Significance  
This research underscores that AI’s ethical response to coercive control is not universal; it is shaped by language‑specific design choices. Recognizing these biases is crucial for ensuring that protective interventions are equally effective for all users, regardless of the language they use to seek help. The findings call for a floor of protection per language rather than an aspirational ceiling.
