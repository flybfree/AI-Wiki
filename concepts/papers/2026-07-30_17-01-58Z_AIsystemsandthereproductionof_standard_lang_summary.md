## Summary  

This study investigates how contemporary artificial‑intelligence (AI) systems reproduce the “standard” language ideologies that are embedded in World Englishes (WEs). By analysing large‑scale corpora generated by state‑of‑the‑art language models and comparing them with authentic usage from speakers of Indian English, Nigerian Pidgin, Jamaican Patois, and Australian English, we demonstrate that AI outputs consistently privilege the normative register, lexical choices, and syntactic structures associated with “standard” varieties. The findings reveal a systematic marginalisation of non‑standard forms—both in frequency and in semantic richness—and highlight how algorithmic training data, which is heavily skewed toward dominant WEs, entrenches existing power relations. The paper therefore contributes to both linguistic sociolinguistics and AI ethics by offering empirical evidence that AI systems are not neutral mirrors but active reproducers of prevailing language ideologies.

## Key Contributions  

1. **Empirical Mapping of Ideology Reproduction** – We provide the first systematic, corpus‑based mapping of how dominant WEs (e.g., Indian English) are encoded in AI‑generated text across multiple non‑standard varieties. This creates a reusable benchmark for measuring ideological bias in multilingual NLP systems.  

2. **Comparative Framework for Ideological Assessment** – The study introduces a methodological framework that combines quantitative frequency analysis with qualitative discourse coding to evaluate the extent to which AI reproduces standard language ideologies. The framework can be applied to any AI‑generated corpus, facilitating cross‑linguistic and cross‑domain comparisons.  

3. **Methodological Insights for Bias Detection** – By foregrounding the role of training data composition and token‑level representation, we offer concrete guidance on how developers can audit and mitigate ideological bias in AI language models. The insights include: (a) the importance of balanced multilingual corpora; (b) the use of dialect‑specific evaluation metrics; and (c) the need for human‑in‑the‑loop validation to capture nuanced sociolinguistic features that pure statistical measures miss.  

4. **Theoretical Contribution** – The work extends discourse‑analysis theory by showing how algorithmic generation functions as a “reproduction engine” of linguistic hierarchies, thereby enriching discussions on the social embeddedness of language in AI.

## Results  

### 1. Frequency and Register Bias  

| Dialect (WE) | % Standard Lexical Items in AI Output* | % Standard Lexical Items in Authentic Corpus** |
|--------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Indian English | 78 %                                   | 62 %                                            |
| Nigerian Pidgin| 91 %                                   | 54 %                                            |
| Jamaican Patois| 84 %                                   | 50 %                                            |
| Australian English| 73 %                               | 66 %                                            |

\*AI output generated by the GPT‑4 model (prompt: “Write a short paragraph about climate change”).  
\**Authentic corpus comprises 12,000 sentences collected from community forums and social media.  

The AI consistently over‑uses standard lexical items (e.g., *utilise* vs. *use*, *colour* vs. *color*) while under‑representing dialectal forms such as *bhai* or *patois*. The gap widens for Nigerian Pidgin, where the AI’s standardisation is most extreme.

### 2. Syntactic and Pragmatic Distinctions  

A mixed‑methods analysis of sentence structures shows:

- **Standard Register Preference**: AI sentences are 3.4 times more likely to employ complex clause nesting (e.g., *Although it was raining, we went out*) than authentic WEs, which favour simpler constructions (*It was raining, so we went out*).  
- **Pragmatic Neutrality Deficit**: The AI frequently omits pragmatic markers that signal solidarity or inclusion in non‑standard varieties (e.g., “we all know” vs. “we all *know*”). In authentic WEs, such markers appear 27 % more often.

### 3. Semantic Richness and Lexical Diversity  

Using the n‑gram diversity index (NDI), AI outputs exhibit:

- **Lower NDI**: Average NDI = 1.84 vs. 2.56 in authentic WEs, indicating reduced lexical variety.  
- **Higher Collocation Bias**: The AI over‑produces high‑frequency collocations from standard corpora (e.g., *climate change* appears 3.9 × more often than *global warming*), while under‑using dialect‑specific collocations (*patois climate*).

Figure 1 visualises the distribution of standard vs. non‑standard tokens across the four WEs. The AI’s token stream is represented by a narrow, high‑peak bar for “standard” tokens; the authentic streams are broader with multiple peaks corresponding to dialectal forms.

### 5. Statistical Significance  

All observed differences (p < 0.01) were confirmed via paired t‑tests and ANOVA across the four WEs, confirming that AI reproduces standard language ideologies significantly more than authentic usage.

**Overall Takeaway:** The empirical evidence demonstrates that current AI systems act as inadvertent agents of linguistic inequality by privileging dominant, “standard” forms of World Englishes while systematically marginalising non‑standard varieties. Addressing this reproduction requires both technical adjustments (balanced training data, dialect‑aware evaluation metrics) and a broader sociolinguistic commitment to recognizing the legitimacy of diverse speech practices.
