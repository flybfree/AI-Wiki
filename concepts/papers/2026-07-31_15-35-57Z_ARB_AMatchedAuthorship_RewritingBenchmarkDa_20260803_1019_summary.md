## Summary
This paper introduces the Authorship-Rewriting Benchmark (ARB), a novel dataset designed to evaluate the robustness of AI-text detectors against sophisticated rewriting attacks that preserve human authorship styles. The authors argue that standard benchmarks, which compare raw human text against direct LLM outputs, fail to predict detector performance when human content is modified by an LLM. By creating matched pairs of texts across four distinct categories, the study reveals a significant gap in current detection capabilities regarding rewritten human-authored content. The findings demonstrate that while detectors are generally robust to LLM-generated text being rewritten by other models, they suffer catastrophic performance drops when LLMs rewrite human-written text, challenging the validity of existing evaluation metrics.

## Key Contributions
- **Introduction of ARB**: The authors present a new benchmark dataset comprising 1,800 source texts and four matched variants (Human, Free-LLM, H2L, and LLM2L) to rigorously test detector resilience against authorship-preserving rewrites.
- **Detection Vulnerability in H2L Regimes**: The study identifies a critical vulnerability where detectors lose 60–78 percentage points of recall when processing human text that has been rewritten by an LLM, despite the content remaining fundamentally human in origin and style.
- **Robustness in LLM2L Regimes**: Conversely, the research highlights that detectors remain largely stable (with only a 10–13 point drop in performance) when LLM-generated text is rewritten by the same or different models, suggesting that current detection mechanisms are biased toward detecting "pure" AI patterns rather than hybrid ones.

## Methodology
The researchers constructed the ARB dataset using 1,800 human source texts drawn from three diverse corpora: XSum, WritingPrompts, and OpenWebText (600 texts each). They employed four open-weight large language models—Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, and Gemma-2-9B—to generate the necessary variants. For every source item, four matched versions were created: the original human-written text (HUMAN), direct text generated by an LLM (Free-LLM), human text rewritten by an LLM (H2L), and LLM-generated text rewritten by the same generator (LLM2L). The authors then evaluated five prominent AI-text detectors—FastDetectGPT, Binoculars-falcon-7b, RADAR, BERT-Defense, and RoBERTa-Defense—at a strict operating point of 1% false positive rate to ensure fair comparison of true positive rates.

## Results
The experimental results reveal a stark disparity in detector performance across different text regimes. FastDetectGPT and Binoculars-falcon-7b achieved high recall for direct LLM generation (91.2% and 93.5%, respectively) but plummeted to 30.8% and 15.1% when detecting human text rewritten by an LLM. This represents a massive decline of 60–78 percentage points, indicating that these detectors fail to recognize AI-modified human content. In contrast, when LLM text was rewritten by the same model, recall remained high at 78.3% and 83.0%, showing minimal degradation. RADAR showed a similar pattern, dropping from 66.8% to 12.2% in the H2L regime. Meanwhile, BERT-Defense and RoBERTa-Defense performed poorly across all categories, maintaining recall below 3%.

## Significance
This research is significant because it exposes a fundamental flaw in current AI-text detection evaluation standards. It proves that high performance on conventional human-vs-LLM benchmarks does not translate to real-world scenarios where humans use LLMs for editing and paraphrasing. This gap poses serious risks for academic integrity and content verification systems, as detectors may falsely flag human work as AI-generated or fail to detect sophisticated AI-assisted writing. The study necessitates a rethinking of how we validate detection tools, emphasizing the need for benchmarks that account for hybrid authorship workflows.
