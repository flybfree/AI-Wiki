## Summary  
The paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) align with human judgments of Japanese language attitudes toward non‑native speakers by comparing evaluations of parallel L1‑ and L2‑written Japanese emails across three dimensions: fluency, status, and solidarity. It shows that both humans and LLMs rate L2 texts lower than L1 texts, but the magnitude of these biases differs systematically. This work extends the language attitudes framework to provide a yardstick for auditing LLM performance beyond English.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: LLMs reproduce the direction of human bias in rating L2 Japanese emails lower across all three dimensions (fluency, status, solidarity).  
- Finding 2: The fluency gap is roughly twice as large as the status or solidarity gaps, and LLMs understate the solidarity gap more than humans.  
- Finding 3: LLMs differentiate evaluations by learner L1 background, whereas human raters did not.

## Methodology  
The authors constructed a corpus of Japanese emails written in native (L1) and non‑native (L2) Japanese. Human raters completed a standardized questionnaire rating each email on fluency, status, and solidarity. Six LLM judges were prompted to evaluate the same texts, generating scores for each dimension. The study compared human and model ratings across dimensions and by L1 background.

## Results  
Human raters consistently rated L2 emails lower than L1 emails in all three dimensions; the fluency gap was roughly twice as large as status or solidarity gaps. All six LLMs reproduced the direction of these biases, with five also reproducing the ordering (fluency > status > solidarity). Notably, LLMs underrated the solidarity dimension more than humans and showed variability across L1 backgrounds that humans did not.

## Significance  
This research demonstrates that LLMs inherit and partially amplify human language attitudes toward non‑native speakers, which could affect high‑stakes applications such as hiring or academic assessment. By providing a structured metric (the language attitudes framework) for auditing model fairness, the study offers practical guidance for mitigating bias in AI‑driven decision making.
