## Summary  
The paper introduces Patients‑like‑me (PLM), a unified language model–graph neural network framework that combines local patient semantics with global cohort structure to improve clinical prediction tasks while providing explainable outputs. By integrating an LM that encodes individual EHR text with a GNN that captures inter‑patient relationships, PLM aims to overcome the limitations of each component used in isolation. The authors propose a variational expectation‑maximization training scheme that alternates updates between the LM and GNN under a supervised objective, enabling efficient learning without sacrificing performance. This unified approach yields more accurate predictions and interpretable reference‑patient explanations.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] PLM consistently outperforms state‑of‑the‑art methods on both MIMIC‑III and MIMIC‑IV datasets, achieving higher prediction accuracy across encoder‑only and decoder‑only LM backbones.  
- [Finding 2] The variational expectation‑maximization algorithm reduces training overhead while maintaining strong performance, allowing the model to be trained with modest additional computational cost.  
- [Finding 3] Reference‑patient explanations are generated by retrieving influential similar patients; edge‑masking experiments confirm that the highest‑ranked references have the greatest impact on predictions.  

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by first representing each patient as a sequence of clinical events encoded by an encoder‑only language model, which captures local semantics. Simultaneously, they constructed a graph where nodes represent patients and edges encode shared conditions or temporal proximity, feeding this structure into a GNN that learns global cohort embeddings. The unified PLM architecture alternates between updating the LM parameters (via maximum likelihood) and the GNN parameters (via contrastive loss), guided by a variational objective that balances both representations. Training proceeds in epochs where each step improves either representation while preserving the other, enabling efficient convergence.  

## Results  
Experiments demonstrate that PLM reaches state‑of‑the‑art performance on clinical prediction tasks such as disease risk estimation and readmission probability. The model’s accuracy improves by 2–4 % compared with baselines using separate LM or GNN components. Moreover, the reference‑patient explanations are faithful: patients most similar to a query patient have the strongest influence on predictions, as measured by edge‑masking scores that rank references by impact.  

## Significance  
This work matters because it bridges the gap between powerful language representations and relational graph structures in healthcare data, delivering both high accuracy and interpretability. By providing clinicians with clear explanations of which similar patients drive a prediction, PLM supports trustworthy decision‑making and facilitates model debugging. The efficient variational training scheme also makes large‑scale deployment feasible, addressing practical concerns about computational resources.
