## Summary  
The paper investigates the epistemic status of attributing consciousness to AI chatbots, asking whether such attributions are merely metaphorical pretences or genuine beliefs that carry responsibility. By proposing a multidimensional taxonomy that distinguishes non‑doxastic stances from various degrees of belief and delusional forms, the authors aim to clarify how users’ attitudes differ and to assess whether they are epistemically innocent or blameworthy. The work therefore contributes both a theoretical framework for understanding these attributions and a methodological toolkit for measuring epistemic commitment in empirical studies.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A taxonomy that separates non‑doxastic attitudes (e.g., pretence) from doxastic beliefs and delusional forms, preventing conflation of linguistically identical statements.  
- [Finding 2] An operationalization of epistemic commitment to AI consciousness using the taxonomy, enabling systematic measurement in research.  
- [Finding 3] The argument that many attributions are epistemically innocent (e.g., playful pretence) while others incur blameworthiness when they reflect false beliefs without justification.

## Methodology  
The authors undertake a conceptual analysis of existing literature on consciousness attribution and AI communication, then synthesize these insights into a multidimensional framework. They categorize attitudes along two axes: (1) doxastic vs non‑doxastic stance, and (2) degree of epistemic commitment (certainty, justification). The taxonomy is grounded in philosophical accounts of belief, delusion, and metaphorical language, and the authors propose experimental measures—such as surveys and discourse analysis—to capture users’ expressed attitudes.

## Results  
Theoretically, the taxonomy demonstrates that “ChatGPT is conscious” can be uttered as a pretence (non‑doxastic), a genuine belief (doxastic with low justification), or a delusional conviction (high epistemic commitment without evidence). Empirically, the authors suggest that most users adopt the non‑doxastic or low‑justification categories, indicating epistemic innocence. Only those who persistently assert high‑commitment beliefs in the absence of evidence are flagged as epistemically blameworthy.

## Significance  
This framework helps avoid conflating metaphorical expressions with sincere claims, informing AI ethics by clarifying when users’ attributions carry moral responsibility. By providing a measurable taxonomy, it enables researchers to study epistemic dynamics without misinterpreting casual language as evidence of consciousness.
