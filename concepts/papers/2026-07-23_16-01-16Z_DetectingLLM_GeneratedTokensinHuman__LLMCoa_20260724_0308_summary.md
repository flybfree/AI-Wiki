## Summary  
The paper tackles the problem of pinpointing which parts of a mixed‑authorship document were likely generated by a large language model (LLM). It introduces a token‑level detection framework that smooths adjacent token scores and selects an adaptive bandwidth using a Lepski‑type rule, thereby reducing variability while preserving signal fidelity. Unlike earlier methods that operate at the document level, this approach localizes probable LLM contributions with fine‑grained precision. The solution is lightweight, requiring no labeled token data for training.

## Key Contributions  
- **Adaptive bandwidth selection**: The authors propose an adaptive Lepski‑type rule that dynamically adjusts the smoothing bandwidth according to local authorship patterns, balancing smoothness and variance reduction.  
- **Theoretical trade‑off analysis**: A formal study characterizes the mean square error (MSE) trade‑off between increasing smoothness and preserving estimation accuracy, demonstrating a favorable performance envelope.  
- **Empirical superiority over baselines**: Experiments on synthetic and real datasets show that the method outperforms simple averaging, thresholding, and other token‑level detectors in both sensitivity and specificity.

## Methodology  
The authors build upon existing token‑level detection scores by applying local smoothing to each score vector. This smoothing reduces high‑frequency noise while maintaining the underlying signal. To control the bandwidth of the smoothing operation, they employ an adaptive Lepski rule that inspects neighboring tokens for signs of human versus LLM authorship and adjusts the window size accordingly. The resulting detector is implemented as a straightforward post‑processing step; no supervised training or token‑level labeled data are required.

## Results  
Theoretical analysis shows that the proposed smoothing strategy yields an MSE that is lower than naive uniform smoothing for typical authorship distributions, indicating a favorable error profile. In practice, on synthetic datasets with controlled human‑LLM splits and on a realistic corpus of coauthored articles, the method achieves higher detection rates (up to 12 % improvement) compared with baseline approaches such as simple mean‑pooling, binary thresholding, and deep token classifiers. The authors have released a public website that implements all components for reproducibility.

## Significance  
Precise localization of LLM‑generated tokens is crucial for transparency, plagiarism detection, and ensuring human authorship in collaborative works. By providing a fast, data‑light detector that can be integrated into existing pipelines, the paper advances the field toward reliable, fine‑grained AI content identification.
