## Summary  
The paper investigates the limitations of existing verifier‑free test‑time scaling (VF‑TTS) methods that rely solely on rollout confidence to select high‑quality completions, especially for complex tasks such as graduate‑level mathematics and free‑form code generation. It reveals a systematic failure: uniformly high confidence often masks poor exploration and leads to systematically wrong answers. The authors propose **Consilience**, a selection framework that captures the required temporal asymmetry of confidence—low initial uncertainty followed by high final certainty—to guide robust cognitive search without external verification. This work advances the field by providing a principled, verifier‑free strategy for scaling LLM reasoning.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Uniformly high rollout confidence on complex tasks correlates with exploration failure and poor answer quality.  
- Finding 2: A robust cognitive search must exhibit low early confidence to enable branching and eventual convergence to a confident solution.  
- Finding 3: Consilience, defined by a combinatorial metric that penalizes high initial confidence while demanding final certainty, outperforms existing baselines across diverse benchmarks.

## Methodology  
The authors first formalize the problem of selecting completions under the constraint of verifier‑free evaluation. They derive a **confidence trajectory** as a sequence of per‑step confidence scores generated by the LLM during reasoning. The core insight is that successful reasoning follows a specific pattern: initial low confidence encourages exploration, while later high confidence signals convergence to an accurate answer. Consilience computes a combinatorial metric that evaluates this trajectory, assigning higher scores when early confidence is low and final confidence is high, thereby discouraging prematurely confident but incorrect completions.

## Results  
Experimental evaluation on two benchmark suites—(i) graduate‑level mathematics problems (e.g., symbolic integration, proof generation) and (ii) free‑form code generation tasks—shows that Consilience yields a 12 % average improvement in solution correctness compared to the strongest confidence‑based baselines. The metric also reduces the proportion of “exploration failures” by 38 %, as measured by early‑stage confidence spikes. Ablation studies confirm that the combinatorial penalty on initial confidence is essential, while the final certainty requirement alone does not improve performance.

## Significance  
Consilience addresses a critical gap in verifier‑free scaling: it provides a principled way to guide LLM reasoning without relying on external verification tools, making it applicable across domains where such tools are unavailable. By linking confidence dynamics to cognitive search behavior, the method offers a scalable, interpretable framework that can be integrated into existing inference pipelines.
