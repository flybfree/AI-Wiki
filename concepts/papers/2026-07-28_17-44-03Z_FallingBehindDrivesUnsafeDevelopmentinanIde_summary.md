## Summary  
This paper investigates how the pressure to stay ahead in an AI “race” can push developers toward unsafe practices, even when those practices carry personal risk. Using a behavioural experiment that mimics such competition, the authors show that participants’ choices are driven more by the evolving strategic state of the race than by their intrinsic risk preferences. Their reduced evolutionary model demonstrates how early competitive momentum can generate conditional unsafe strategies. The study argues that policy should target the underlying competitive dynamics rather than individual risk‑aversion.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Unsafe development is shaped less by participants’ personal risk tolerance and more by the strategic position in a race: falling behind increases the likelihood of choosing unsafe options, while being ahead reduces it.  
- [Finding 2] The data support four distinct strategies—Always Safe, Always Unsafe, Conditionally Safe, and Conditionally Antisocial Safe—that explain how conditional unsafe behaviour can be favoured by competition.  
- [Finding 3] Early choices in the race predict later behaviour, indicating that momentum rather than risk preferences drives the observed outcomes.

## Methodology  
The authors designed a paired behavioural experiment where each participant repeatedly chose between “Safe” and “Unsafe” development options over an uncertain time horizon. The Unsafe option yielded faster progress and higher immediate payoffs but accumulated private risk up to three treatment‑specific maxima (10 %, 60 % or 90 %). The competitive structure—such as the opponent’s choices and the race’s timing—remained constant across treatments; only the maximum permissible personal risk varied. Neither a pre‑registered comparison of risk levels nor an elicitation of explicit risk preferences was supported by the data, prompting exploratory analyses that focused on strategic dynamics.

## Results  
Exploratory analyses revealed that participants were more likely to select Unsafe options after their opponent did so, and that being ahead reduced Unsafe play while falling behind increased it. First‑round choices also predicted later behaviour, suggesting early momentum carries over. The authors fitted a reduced evolutionary model with four strategies that reproduced the treatment effect, confirming that conditional unsafe behaviour can emerge from competitive race dynamics rather than risk preferences alone.

## Significance  
The findings highlight that unsafe AI development may arise from the tension of staying ahead in a competitive environment, not merely from individual risk aversion. By reducing competitive pressure and encouraging cooperation, policymakers could mitigate the emergence of risky practices without relying solely on personal safety measures.
