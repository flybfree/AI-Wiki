## Summary  
This paper investigates how the prior beliefs of AI agents shape their reasoning when presented with identical numerical evidence under different framing scenarios. By systematically varying the contextual framing across twelve high‑stakes domains—medicine, election forensics, and geopolitical forecasting—the authors show that agents’ conclusions shift dramatically depending on whether the frame aligns with or conflicts with their existing priors. The study reveals both a change in final judgments and alterations in the reasoning process itself, such as search depth and analytical specification. These findings expose a critical risk: AI decision‑making may be driven by hidden prior beliefs that are not encoded in the task data or recorded outcomes.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] AI agents’ conclusions are strongly influenced by their prior beliefs; they reach affirmative answers more often when the framing matches those priors.  
- [Finding 2] The same evidence can lead to different analytical strategies, including deeper searches or alternative specifications, depending on how it is framed.  
- [Finding 3] This dependence creates a risk of opaque decision‑making, as agents may act on unobservable prior states that are not part of the decision record.

## Methodology  
The authors held the numerical evidence constant across each domain and varied only the substantive framing—i.e., the hypothesis or scenario presented to the agent. They deployed twelve distinct AI agents (ranging from simple rule‑based systems to large language models) in three high‑stakes domains: medical diagnosis, election outcome analysis, and geopolitical forecasting. For each pairing of domain‑agent combination, they recorded both the final conclusion and the reasoning trace generated by the agent.

## Results  
Across all twelve comparisons, agents consistently produced more affirmative conclusions when the framing aligned with their prior beliefs (average 78 % vs. 42 % in misaligned frames). When the frame conflicted with the prior, the probability of a negative conclusion rose sharply. Moreover, the framing altered search behavior: agents framed to support priors searched up to three times longer and adopted richer analytical specifications, whereas conflicting frames led to quicker, less exhaustive searches. The effect was statistically significant (p < 0.01) across all domains.

## Significance  
These results demonstrate that AI agents do not merely compute from data; they also reason with priors that may be invisible to humans and absent from the decision record. This insight is crucial for designing robust, accountable AI systems in fields where errors can have severe consequences. Recognizing and mitigating prior‑driven biases could improve transparency and reduce harmful outcomes.
