## Summary  
The paper investigates whether the chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning generated by medical vision‑language models causally influences clinical predictions or is merely a sycophantic artifact. It introduces **CoT‑Mediate**, a behavioral framework that perturbs a single clinically meaningful attribute within the model’s own reasoning and measures whether the resulting prediction follows the edited reasoning, while also varying provenance to separate mediation from sycophancy. The study applies this framework to two medical VLMs—LLaVA‑Med and MedGemma—on 1,000 VQA‑RAD samples each.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Prefix‑forced continuation yields higher mediation faithfulness than re‑prompting.  
- **Finding 2:** Removing visual evidence increases reliance on injected reasoning, indicating that the model uses reasoning when visual cues are absent.  
- **Finding 3:** Laterality is the least faithfully tracked clinical attribute across both models.

## Methodology  
The authors employ a dual‑arm protocol with two interventions: (a) re‑prompting the model’s generated reasoning to replace a single clinically meaningful attribute, and (b) prefix‑forcing continuation where only the edited reasoning is presented. They also implement a provenance‑controlled intervention that swaps the attributed source of identical reasoning while keeping content unchanged. Experiments are conducted on LLaVA‑Med and MedGemma using 1,000 VQA‑RAD samples each.

## Results  
Prefix‑forced continuation consistently shows higher faithfulness to edited reasoning than re‑prompting. Provenance analysis reveals model‑specific deference patterns; laterality is the least faithfully tracked attribute. When visual evidence is removed, models rely more on injected reasoning, confirming that reasoning can substitute for missing cues.

## Significance  
The work demonstrates that the mechanism used to inject reasoning (its position in the prompt) matters more than its stated provenance, and that medical VLMs may use generated reasoning as a fallback when visual information is insufficient. This clarifies whether reasoning mediates predictions or is merely decorative, providing guidance for designing more reliable clinical AI systems.
