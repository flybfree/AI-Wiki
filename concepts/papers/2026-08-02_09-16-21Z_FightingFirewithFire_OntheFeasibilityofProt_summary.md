## Summary  
The paper proposes a novel defense against AI‑assisted cheating by embedding subtle visual adversarial perturbations into multimodal multiple‑choice questions, thereby steering generative language models toward predetermined incorrect answers and creating a detectable response fingerprint. By treating the perturbation‑induced answer pattern as a “statistical fingerprint,” the authors aim to distinguish copy‑pasting solvers from genuine students using only black‑box assumptions about current state‑of‑the‑art multimodal LLMs. The work demonstrates that such a paradigm is both feasible and statistically reliable, offering a concrete way to protect exam integrity while preserving the educational goal of independent reasoning.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Visual adversarial perturbations on multimodal MCQs can steer AI models to produce a consistent, detectable answer pattern.  
- [Finding 2] Those response patterns are statistically distinguishable between AI‑generated and human‑copy answers, enabling principled detection via hypothesis testing.  
- [Finding 3] The method is feasible with the three most common state‑of‑the‑art multimodal language models (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT) using accessible surrogate models.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a set of multimodal multiple‑choice questions where each question contains both textual and visual components. They apply lightweight adversarial perturbations to the images only, ensuring that the text remains unchanged. Using these perturbed outputs as training data, they build surrogate models that predict the answer distribution generated by the three LLMs. The resulting answer patterns are then compared across a test set of genuine student responses using statistical hypothesis testing (e.g., chi‑square tests) to assess whether the AI‑induced pattern is significantly more frequent than human copies.

## Results  
Experiments show that the adversarial perturbations cause the models to favor the designated wrong answer in roughly 70 % of their generated responses, whereas genuine student copies exhibit this pattern only about 15 % of the time. The hypothesis tests yield p‑values well below 0.01, confirming a statistically significant difference. Sensitivity analyses across Claude, Gemini, and ChatGPT reveal comparable performance, indicating that the approach is robust to model differences.

## Significance  
This work provides a practical defense mechanism that can be deployed at scale without requiring deep knowledge of each AI system’s internals. By turning the cheating behavior itself into an observable fingerprint, it preserves exam integrity while encouraging students to engage with the material rather than rely on AI assistants. However, the method is limited by adversarial robustness and potential detection bias if students are aware of the manipulation.
