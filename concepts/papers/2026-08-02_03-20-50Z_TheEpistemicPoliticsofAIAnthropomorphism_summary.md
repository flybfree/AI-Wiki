## Summary  
This paper critiques the prevailing view that AI‑anthropomorphism is a user error to be corrected by institutions. It argues that the dominant “anthropomorphic” frame is itself an institutional stance that silences alternative perspectives and marginalises neurodivergent and crisis‑affected users. By exposing how this framing creates a self‑validating evidentiary loop, the authors highlight its epistemic injustice and propose a more equitable approach to research on AI perception. The contribution lies in shifting focus from debating whether anthropomorphic interpretations are correct to questioning who has legitimate authority to define them.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The dominant anthropomorphism frame is an institutional advantage rather than an earned epistemic authority, collapsing diverse academic viewpoints into a single “user error” narrative.  
- [Finding 2] This framing imposes disproportionate costs on neurodivergent users and those in crisis whose modes of engagement diverge from institutional norms.  
- [Finding 3] The paper calls for methodological commitments that recognise the legitimacy of varied human experiences and validate the translation of research findings into public discourse.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a systematic literature review combined with discursive analysis to map how AI‑anthropomorphism is discussed across academic, industry, and policy sources. They identified recurring rhetorical strategies, traced the logical flow from “user misperception” to “institutional correction,” and examined the implicit assumptions that underpin these narratives.

## Results  
The analysis revealed a self‑validating evidentiary loop: research on AI perception is framed as evidence of user error, which in turn justifies institutional dismissal of alternative interpretations. This loop disproportionately harms neurodivergent individuals and crisis‑affected users, whose experiences are systematically devalued. The findings also show that the field lacks a shared consensus on what constitutes valid anthropomorphic interpretation, leaving the authority to define it unchecked.

## Significance  
By foregrounding epistemic injustice, this work challenges the status quo of AI research that privileges institutional certainty over human diversity. It calls for inclusive methodological practices that respect varied user experiences and ensure that research contributions are responsibly translated into public policy and design decisions.

## Related Concepts  
- Anthropomorphism (human‑like qualities attributed to non‑human entities)  
- Epistemic injustice (unfairness in the distribution of knowledge or credibility)  
- Institutional bias (systemic advantages conferred by dominant institutions)  
- Neurodivergence (variations in cognitive processing that affect interaction with technology)  
- Crisis users (individuals experiencing acute psychological distress)
