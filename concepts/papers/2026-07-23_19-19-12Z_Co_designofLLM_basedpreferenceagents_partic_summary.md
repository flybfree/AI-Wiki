## Summary  
The paper investigates how co‑designing large language model (LLM) preference agents with the people they are meant to represent can create an “overtrust” effect, where participants feel that their agents accurately reflect them while systematic misalignment remains hidden. By examining a small qualitative study of 12 household‑energy users who built personal preference agents through surveys and interviews, the authors show that participant trust is high but independent validation reveals homogeneous, abstract agent outputs that diverge from human diversity. The contribution is to frame alignment as an enacted process rather than a static state and to warn that participatory design may mask structural problems at scale.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Participant‑driven co‑design fosters high trust but does not guarantee accurate representation of individual preferences.  
- **Finding 2:** Independent validation uncovers homogeneous, decisive, and abstract agent responses that differ markedly from the heterogeneous human sample.  
- **Finding 3:** The “overtrust engine” is a process where transparency and participation amplify trust while concealing misalignment.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a mixed‑methods approach: first, they administered a background survey to collect demographic and preference data; second, they held co‑design interviews guiding participants to build their own LLM agents for household energy choices; finally, they conducted a validation survey where the same participants evaluated how well the agents mirrored their original preferences. This triangulation allowed them to compare self‑perceived alignment with objective human responses.

## Results  
Participants consistently rated their agents as highly representative, indicating strong perceived alignment. However, when the same data were fed into an independent LLM validation task, the agent’s outputs were noticeably more uniform and abstract than the original survey responses, suggesting a loss of individual nuance. The gap between participant trust and objective alignment was quantified qualitatively through thematic analysis.

## Significance  
The findings highlight a critical risk in deploying participatory preference agents: while they can boost user acceptance and engagement, they may also propagate homogenized, less representative advice that could have unintended consequences for decision‑making at scale. Recognizing alignment as an ongoing process encourages designers to embed continuous validation mechanisms.
