## Summary  
This paper investigates how the availability of external tools reshapes human competence and reliance, treating the user‑tool‑task triad as a single dynamical system. The authors demonstrate that competence (what users retain) and reliance (what they outsource) co‑evolve in a bistable fashion: above a critical tool threshold competence collapses into a low‑dependence state, while below it remains stable. Their analysis shows that the collapse is irreversible unless a far lower availability level is reached, meaning historical practice—not current tool access—determines the lasting state of users. The work also reveals that when tools are opaque or too large, agency can transfer irreversibly to the instrument itself.

## Key Contributions  
- **Bistable competence‑reliance dynamics**: Users exhibit either a competent, low‑dependence state or a highly dependent, high‑dependence state depending on tool availability.  
- **Threshold dependence on competence and transparency**: The collapse threshold is jointly set by the user’s pre‑existing competence and the fraction of the tool that can be internally reconstructed (its transparency).  
- **Agency transfer to tools**: In uncertain tasks, opaque or overly complex tools can cause agency to shift irreversibly into an “agentic‑instrument” mode, making the human‑tool system inseparable.

## Methodology  
The authors model competence and reliance as coupled dynamical variables that co‑evolve with tool availability. They analyze this system theoretically, deriving conditions for bistability and collapse, then validate the predictions against empirical data from GPS/map navigation, arithmetic expertise, and large language model usage. Experiments compare users with identical present access but different histories to confirm that past practice fixes the state.

## Results  
Theoretical analysis predicts a critical tool‑availability point above which competence collapses toward a low dependent floor; below this point the system remains competent. Empirical tests across three domains confirm these predictions: GPS users who have long used maps retain competence, while novices become fully dependent; arithmetic experts maintain competence with calculators but lose it when tools are removed; language‑model users experience irreversible agency transfer to the model itself. The results show that historical practice, not current tool access, determines lasting competence.

## Significance  
These findings reframe how we design tools and deploy AI: they must preserve transparency to avoid agency loss and respect user competence levels. They also highlight the need for “tool‑resistant” education that builds internal competence rather than relying on external aids. The paper bridges cognitive science, human‑computer interaction, and artificial intelligence policy.
