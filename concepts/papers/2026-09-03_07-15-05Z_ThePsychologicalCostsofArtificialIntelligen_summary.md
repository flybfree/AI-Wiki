## Summary  
The paper investigates the psychological impacts of artificial intelligence adoption in software engineering, arguing that benefits such as productivity gains are offset by hidden emotional costs. It contributes three specific findings on stress domains and offers a conceptual shift from viewing AI integration solely as a technological upgrade to recognizing it as a human transition requiring support for identity and meaning.  

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Software professionals experience accountability anxiety because they feel responsible for the correctness and ethical implications of AI‑generated code.  
- Finding 2: Craft identity disruption occurs when AI reduces reliance on personal coding expertise, eroding the professional self‑concept that defines their role.  
- Finding 3: Meaning and satisfaction erosion is linked to uncertainty about future relevance of skills and the loss of purpose tied to traditional development tasks.  

## Methodology  
The authors conducted a qualitative case study within one large software‑development services firm that had rolled out AI tools for code generation, test cases, and reviews over the past year. They collected data through semi‑structured interviews (N = 21) and team meetings, probing participants’ lived experiences of AI integration to uncover underlying psychological stressors.  

## Results  
Participants reported heightened anxiety about being held accountable for AI outputs; they felt their identity as skilled developers was undermined; they expressed a loss of meaning tied to their work purpose; cognitive load increased due to managing both human and AI tasks; overall job satisfaction declined. These themes were thematically coded across the identified stress domains, confirming that AI adoption generates measurable psychological costs beyond productivity metrics.  

## Significance  
Understanding these psychological costs is crucial for organizations seeking sustainable AI adoption, as unaddressed emotional strain can lead to turnover, reduced performance, and cultural friction. The paper highlights that successful integration must address identity preservation and meaning maintenance, not just efficiency gains.
