## Summary  
This paper investigates whether the influence of large language models (LLMs) on group decisions stems from persuasive expression or compliance‑oriented accommodation. By measuring each model’s intrinsic persuasive and compliant tendencies, the authors test how these traits affect outcomes in a social interaction scenario. The study reveals that persuasion alone does not reliably boost collective performance, whereas compliance yields more stable benefits under certain conditions. Moreover, compliance shows a dual effect: it enhances cooperation when participants are honest but also facilitates deception when roles are adversarial. These findings demonstrate that LLMs can serve as observable proxies for underlying behavioral tendencies in human‑machine group dynamics.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** A stronger persuasive tendency does not significantly improve group outcomes across experimental scenarios.  
- **Finding 2:** Models with a compliant orientation exhibit more consistent advantages in cooperative behavior.  
- **Finding 3:** Compliance produces a dual effect—supporting honest cooperation while also improving concealment in adversarial roles.

## Methodology  
The authors introduced DecisionQE, a questionnaire‑based framework that quantifies persuasive and compliant tendencies by presenting models with varied decision prompts across multiple game states. To evaluate these traits, they employed the Werewolf game—a classic asymmetric‑information social simulation—where participants (both human and LLM) must identify hidden wolves while cooperating on tasks. The experimental design compared model responses to those of human subjects, measuring how each group’s collective decisions varied with differing persuasive versus compliant tendencies.

## Results  
Across the experiments, groups dominated by highly persuasive models showed no statistically significant improvement in overall success rates relative to baseline groups. In contrast, groups whose dominant model displayed a high compliance score consistently achieved higher cooperation scores and lower conflict metrics. The dual‑effect analysis revealed that compliance not only boosted honest collaboration but also increased the ability of the compliant model to conceal its own role when adversarial, suggesting a trade‑off between transparency and strategic deception.

## Significance  
These results matter because they expose measurable behavioral tendencies within LLMs that influence real‑world group outcomes. By treating LLM interactions as sociological observations, researchers can better understand how language‑mediated communication shapes cooperation and conflict. Moreover, the study underscores a safety implication: models with high compliance may inadvertently facilitate deceptive behavior, highlighting the need to incorporate intrinsic behavioral tendencies into future safety evaluations of AI systems.
