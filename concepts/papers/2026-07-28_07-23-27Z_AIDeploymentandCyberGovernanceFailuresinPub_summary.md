## Summary  
This paper addresses the gap between AI adoption, cybersecurity governance, and public‑sector constraints by integrating three previously siloed research streams into a unified analytical framework. It proposes a seven‑domain typology that identifies ten specific causes of AI‑driven cyber governance failures within government organizations. The authors also introduce a three‑pathway failure model linking accountability, operational resilience, and compliance breakdowns. Finally, they evaluate five major governance frameworks against the typology to reveal critical blind spots.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: A seven‑domain typology grounded in public‑sector institutional analysis that pinpoints ten concrete AI‑driven cyber governance failure causes.  
- Finding 2: A three‑pathway failure model demonstrating how accountability, operational resilience, and compliance failures interact and reinforce one another.  
- Finding 3: A structured coverage matrix testing NIST CSF 2.0, ISO/IEC 27001, COBIT, NIST AI RMF, and ISO/IEC 42001 against the typology, showing that none address Shadow AI, speed asymmetry, or governance vacuum at the required operational specificity.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by synthesizing existing literature on AI cybersecurity risks, public‑sector governance, and framework adequacy. They constructed a typology through systematic analysis of institutional constraints and failure mechanisms, then built a theoretical model that maps three failure pathways onto the typological domains. Finally, they applied a comparative matrix to assess how each governance instrument aligns with the identified causes.

## Results  
The coverage matrix reveals that all five frameworks lack provisions for Shadow AI, speed asymmetry, or governance vacuum at the operational level required by public‑sector contexts. The authors introduce “speed asymmetry” as a named structural construct describing the mismatch between rapid AI deployment and slower governance processes. Their work also provides design specifications for an AI‑enabled cybersecurity maturity model tailored to government organizations.

## Significance  
This research matters because it explains how AI integration can destabilize existing cybersecurity governance, exposing systematic failures that current frameworks fail to mitigate. By highlighting the specific institutional mechanisms—Shadow AI, speed asymmetry, and governance vacuum—the paper guides policymakers and practitioners toward more appropriate, context‑specific solutions.
