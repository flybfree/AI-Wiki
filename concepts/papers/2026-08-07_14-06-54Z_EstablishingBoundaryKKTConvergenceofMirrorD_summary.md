## Summary  
The paper establishes that mirror descent converges to a KKT point even when the feasible set has boundary limits, contrary to previous assumptions that such convergence fails at boundaries. It does so by introducing a metric‑flattening reparameterization S whose objective is defined on an extended space and admits a definable boundary extension. The authors prove this via a KL divergence argument applied to the reparameterized problem. Continuity of the inverse mapping S⁻¹ then guarantees that the original sequence converges to a KKT point of the constrained problem.  

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Proof of boundary KKT convergence for mirror descent via metric‑flattening reparameterization.  
- Finding 2: General framework linking objective, Legendre kernel, and feasible geometry through verifiable conditions.  
- Finding 3: Application to concrete examples (Shannon entropy, Fermi–Dirac entropy, power kernels) demonstrating the method’s versatility.  

## Methodology  
The authors approach by constructing a reparameterization S that maps feasible points into an unconstrained space where the objective becomes a Legendre‑type function. They verify verifiable conditions ensuring the kernel is monotone and the geometry of constraints is compatible with S. Using the KL divergence, they bound the difference between successive iterates in the extended space. Finally, they rely on continuity of S⁻¹ to transfer convergence back to the original feasible set.  

## Results  
The main theoretical result is that for any sequence generated by mirror descent with the reparameterized objective, S(x_k) converges in KL divergence to a stationary point of the Legendre kernel, which implies x_k converges to a KKT point after applying S⁻¹. The method works for nonconvex objectives such as Shannon entropy and Fermi–Dirac entropy, and also for power kernels. No additional assumptions beyond verifiable conditions are needed.  

## Significance  
This work resolves a longstanding gap in mirror descent theory by proving convergence at boundary points, enabling reliable optimization of problems where feasible sets are not closed or have singularities. It provides a theoretical foundation for using nonconvex methods in machine learning and physics where constraints often involve physical limits.
