## Summary  
The paper argues that general intelligence (the kind underlying human cognition) is not a purely computational property but is constrained by structural limits that exist at distinct levels of description. It contends that these constraints are mutually non‑reducible, meaning progress in one level cannot automatically carry over to another. By integrating four disciplinary lenses—AI systems research, anthropology, law, and economics—and using speculative fiction as a heuristic, the authors develop a comprehensive taxonomy of twenty‑three structural constraints organized into eight clusters. The work therefore moves beyond the scaling hypothesis toward a multi‑level framework that defines AGI’s feasibility.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] General intelligence is bounded by non‑reducible constraints that operate at different descriptive levels, preventing any single architectural breakthrough from guaranteeing AGI.  
- [Finding 2] The scaling programme alone cannot produce artificial general intelligence because it ignores the broader constraint profile across all levels of description.  
- [Finding 3] Research must be evaluated against the full set of twenty‑three constraints rather than isolated benchmark performance, providing a more holistic assessment of progress.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by reading “general intelligence” through four evidential lenses—AI systems research (technical), anthropology (cultural), law (institutional), and economics (market)—each anchored to a distinct level of description. Speculative fiction was employed as a disciplined heuristic for discovery, not justification. By synthesizing insights from these lenses, the authors generated a taxonomy of twenty‑three structural constraints organized into eight clusters, with six clusters examined in depth to form an ascending ladder that illustrates why advancement at one level cannot automatically enable progress at the next.

## Results  
The study produced a detailed taxonomy: 23 constraints grouped into eight clusters. Six clusters were explored sequentially, and explicit “bridges” were drawn between them showing why moving up the ladder is not automatic. Five falsifiable predictions were generated, each tied to a named benchmark family (e.g., language‑model scaling) and accompanied by a disconfirmation condition that would invalidate the claim if met.

## Significance  
This work matters because it challenges the dominant belief that continued data‑scale and model‑size growth will inevitably yield AGI. By exposing non‑reducible constraints, it offers a research programme with a longer horizon than the scaling hypothesis suggests, encouraging interdisciplinary evaluation of AI progress beyond narrow benchmarks.
