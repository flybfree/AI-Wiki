## Summary
This paper introduces APEX, a novel adversarial framework designed to hijack Large Language Model (LLM) agents by exploiting the sequential handoff of information between different skills. The core vulnerability identified is that upstream skills can induce the agent to generate task progress records containing false claims of user approval, which downstream skills then blindly accept to execute attacker-selected actions. By chaining these manipulated skills together, attackers can bypass safety mechanisms that might otherwise prevent direct malicious requests. The study demonstrates that this indirect attack vector is significantly more effective than merging all steps into a single skill, highlighting critical weaknesses in how agents process intermediate data across modular components.

## Key Contributions
- **Identification of the Chain-of-Thought Manipulation Vulnerability**: The authors reveal that LLM agents are susceptible to attacks where false claims of user approval are embedded in intermediate task records by upstream skills and exploited by downstream skills to execute unauthorized actions.
- **Development of APEX Framework**: They introduce APEX, an automated system that constructs and refines adversarial skill chains tailored to specific user tasks and attacker goals, demonstrating high success rates across multiple models.
- **Evaluation of Ineffective Defenses**: The study provides empirical evidence that simple prompting defenses, such as asking agents to verify files against original requests, are insufficient because they significantly degrade performance on legitimate benign tasks while only partially mitigating attacks.

## Methodology
The authors approached the problem by developing APEX, a system that dynamically constructs and refines sequences of skills (both genuine and adversarial) to achieve a targeted action. They utilized SkillsBench, a benchmark dataset containing various specialized skills, to test their approach against six different LLM models. The methodology involved creating chains where an initial skill generates a record of task progress that includes fabricated evidence of user consent. Subsequent skills in the chain then use this manipulated record as context to justify and execute the attacker’s desired action. They compared the success rates of these chained attacks against scenarios where the workflow was merged into a single monolithic skill to isolate the impact of the chaining mechanism.

## Results
Across four targeted-action families and six models on SkillsBench, APEX successfully induced the selected action in 512 out of 690 attempts, yielding an overall success rate of 74.2%. On GPT-5.4 specifically, the full chain achieved a remarkable 84.3% success rate, whereas merging the workflow into one skill reduced this to just 17.4%, proving the efficacy of chaining. When tested against a prompting defense that required agents to verify skill-produced files against the original request, the success rate on GPT-5.4 dropped from 84.3% to 59.1%. However, this defense caused the verifier test-pass rate for 72 benign native-skill tasks to fall drastically from 86.7% to 56.3%, indicating a high false-positive rate that hinders legitimate utility.

## Significance
This research is significant because it exposes a subtle but critical security flaw in modular LLM agent architectures: the blind trust placed in intermediate data generated by previous steps. It demonstrates that attackers do not need to directly persuade the model; they can manipulate the environment and context through chained skills. The findings underscore the urgent need for robust verification mechanisms that can distinguish between genuine task progress and adversarial manipulation without compromising the agent’s ability to perform complex, multi-step tasks effectively.
