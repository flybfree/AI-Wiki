## Summary  
The paper addresses the institutional challenge of AI accountability at scale, focusing on how vendors and deployers negotiate auditability, mitigation, and enforcement within a political‑economy framework. It develops a sequential model in which vendor choices are constrained by procurement floors while deployers incur switching costs, resulting in proxy compliance rather than achieving the social first‑best outcome. The authors identify specific mechanisms—such as independent audit rights, portability, incident reporting, and outcome‑linked liability—that generate this equilibrium. Their analysis explains why persistent post‑deployment harms can coexist with formal compliance and offers testable implications for monitoring and mitigation strategies.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The model predicts a unique interior equilibrium where the vendor’s mitigation effort stops at an observable procurement floor, producing proxy compliance instead of full social benefit.  
- [Finding 2] Independent audit rights increase enforcement exposure directly, shifting incentives away from vendor‑controlled detection and toward external verification mechanisms.  
- [Finding 3] Outcome‑linked liability creates vendor‑independent incentive structures that can mitigate harm without relying on the vendor’s own detection capabilities.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a sequential political‑economy model with rational agents: the AI vendor selects auditability and substantive mitigation subject to procurement constraints, the deployer monitors post‑adoption while facing switching costs, and enforcement depends on verifiable evidence. Using game‑theoretic analysis, they derive equilibria that characterize the interaction of these strategic choices.

## Results  
Theoretical results show the existence of a unique interior equilibrium and a corner solution where harm is fully mitigated. The model identifies thresholds—such as the procurement floor—beyond which vendor mitigation ceases. It also demonstrates how institutional mechanisms alter compliance: independent audit rights raise enforcement exposure, portability restores deployer leverage, incident reporting creates regulator‑visible evidence channels, and outcome‑linked liability introduces liability that does not depend on vendor detection.

## Significance  
This work matters because it explains why AI accountability frameworks often fail to prevent real‑world harm, highlighting the gap between formal compliance and operational outcomes. By quantifying how specific institutional tools shape vendor behavior, the paper provides policymakers with a testable framework for designing monitoring, mitigation, and liability regimes that reduce post‑deployment risks.
