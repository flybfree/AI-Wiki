## Summary  
The paper argues that general intelligence (the kind underlying human cognition) is not a single computational property but is bounded by structural constraints that exist at multiple levels of description, each of which cannot be reduced to the others. By integrating insights from AI research, anthropology, law, economics, and speculative fiction, the authors develop a taxonomy of twenty‑three constraints organized into eight clusters, showing that progress in one level does not automatically enable advancement at the next. This work reframes AGI research as a multi‑dimensional problem rather than a purely scaling challenge.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] General intelligence is constrained by structural limits that are distinct across levels of description and mutually non‑reducible, meaning they cannot be derived from one another.  
- [Finding 2] No single architectural advance or continuation of the scaling programme can produce artificial general intelligence; evaluation must consider the full constraint profile rather than isolated benchmarks.  
- [Finding 3] The methodology yields a taxonomy of twenty‑three constraints grouped into eight clusters, with six examined as an ascending ladder whose bridges demonstrate why advancement at one level cannot carry over to the next.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by reading general intelligence through four evidential lenses—AI systems research, anthropology, law, and economics—each anchored to a different descriptive level. Speculative fiction is used not for justification but as a disciplined heuristic to spark discovery. From these lenses they constructed a taxonomy of twenty‑three structural constraints organized into eight clusters, then examined six of them in depth, arranging them as an ascending ladder with explicit bridges that illustrate why progress at one level cannot be transferred to the next.

## Results  
The results consist of (i) a comprehensive taxonomy of twenty‑three constraints across eight clusters; (ii) an ordered ladder of six key constraints showing non‑transferable progression; and (iii) five falsifiable predictions. Each prediction is linked to a named benchmark family and includes a disconfirmation condition, converting the descriptive framework into a research programme that extends beyond the typical horizon implied by scaling arguments.

## Significance  
This work matters because it challenges the dominant belief that AGI will emerge from ever‑larger models or more data. By exposing non‑reducible constraints, it forces researchers to evaluate progress holistically, potentially steering resources away from pure scaling and toward solving deeper structural problems. The falsifiable predictions provide a concrete roadmap for future experimental work.
