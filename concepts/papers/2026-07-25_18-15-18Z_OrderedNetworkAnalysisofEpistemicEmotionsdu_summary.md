## Summary  
The paper investigates how epistemic emotions such as confusion and frustration evolve during in‑person collaborative problem solving (CPS) by analysing retrospective cued‑recall data. By applying ordered network analysis (ONA), the authors reveal systematic patterns of persistence and transition that are invisible to simple descriptive summaries. Their work bridges affective science with AI design, offering a principled framework for interpreting human emotional dynamics in group tasks. The contribution lies in showing that reporting methods reshape perceived connections among emotions and that these shifts correlate with group speed.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] ONA uncovers a stable epistemic core linking curiosity, optimism, and confusion across participants.  
- [Finding 2] Different reporting methods (self‑caught vs probe‑caught) emphasize distinct affective connections within the network.  
- [Finding 3] Faster groups show altered roles for confusion and disengagement, especially in relation to conflict.

## Methodology  
The authors recruited participants who solved a shared problem while an observer recorded their self‑reported emotional states after each step. Using retrospective cued‑recall, they collected lists of emotions experienced at various moments. Ordered network analysis was then applied: the temporal order of emotion mentions was encoded as directed edges in a weighted graph, and the strength of these edges reflected co‑occurrence frequency. This approach allowed systematic comparison between self‑caught and probe‑caught data sets.

## Results  
The ONA graphs revealed three main patterns: (1) curiosity consistently precedes optimism, which is followed by confusion; (2) probe‑caught reports emphasized a stronger link between optimism and disengagement than self‑caught reports did; (3) groups that solved the task faster displayed a pronounced transition from confusion to disengagement after conflict escalation. These results were not detectable through simple summary statistics.

## Significance  
Understanding how epistemic emotions persist and shift during collaboration is crucial for designing AI agents that can anticipate misunderstandings or maintain motivation in group settings. The ordered network framework provides an objective, data‑driven way to model emotional dynamics, informing both human‑centered interaction design and algorithmic support systems.
