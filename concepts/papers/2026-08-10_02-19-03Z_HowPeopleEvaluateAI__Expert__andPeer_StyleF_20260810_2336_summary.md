## Summary  
This study investigates how consumers evaluate three distinct styles of financial advice—generated by an AI assistant, delivered by a certified expert, and posted in an online community forum—while keeping the substantive content identical. The authors find that perceived expertise matters more than source labeling, and they show that disclosure functions as an interpretive frame whose accuracy interacts with message cues to shape trust and reliance. By separating attribution effects from style‑specific cues, the paper contributes a nuanced view of how people make sense of AI versus human advice in everyday financial decisions.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Expert advice is rated more favorably than AI advice on nine out of ten outcomes (effect size d = 0.20–0.47), even when source labels are absent, indicating a robust credibility gap that persists without explicit labeling.  
- [Finding 2] Correctly labeling the source adds little differentiation; however, mislabeling boosts ratings for AI advice on situational fit and overall quality (d ≈ +0.42 each) while diminishing the Expert advantage in situational fit (d ≈ ‑0.36).  
- [Finding 3] Descriptive analyses reveal that AI advice is most responsive to displayed attribution, whereas style‑based differences are most visible when an “AI” label is present, suggesting a feedback loop between labeling and evaluation.

## Methodology  
The authors conducted a preregistered vignette experiment with N = 285 participants. Each vignette contained identical financial facts, numerical values, recommendation direction, and reasoning, but the communication style varied: AI Financial Assistant (AI), Certified Financial Planner (Expert), or Online Community Forum (OC). Source attribution was manipulated through three conditions—correctly labeled, unlabeled, and mislabeled—to isolate the impact of attribution from message‑level cues. The design allowed systematic comparison across styles while controlling for substantive content.

## Results  
The main experimental results confirm that Expert advice outperforms AI advice on most outcomes (9/10) with medium effect sizes, even without labels. Mislabeling amplifies perceived fit and quality for AI advice but erodes the Expert advantage in situational fit. Descriptive checks show that participants’ evaluations of AI advice shift dramatically based on how it is attributed, whereas style differences are most pronounced when an “AI” label is displayed.

## Significance  
These findings matter because they challenge the assumption that simple disclosure neutralizes bias; instead, attribution frames can actively reshape trust and reliance. Understanding this dynamic helps designers of financial‑advice platforms create more transparent and ethically sound AI interactions.
