**Summary**  
The paper investigates why quantization of large language models degrades performance, arguing that the damage is not a simple additive noise but a multiplicative contraction of decision margins. By measuring the margin—difference between the chosen option and its best alternative—across many model families, bit‑widths, and quantization schemes, the authors discover that the margin shrinks dramatically as bits drop below 4, causing previously confident choices to flip with little observable change in benchmark scores. Their key insight is that the effect is per‑model and cannot be transferred between models, challenging existing additive‑noise models of quantization error.

**Key Contributions**  
- [Finding 1] Quantization damage is multiplicative: the decision margin is multiplied by a factor that collapses (e.g., 0.86 at 4 bits, 0.33 at 3 bits, 0.00 at 2 bits), rather than being offset by fixed‑size additive noise.  
- [Finding 2] The shrinkage of margins directly reduces the model’s safety and tool‑calling decisions, with the direction of failure aligned to the model’s small inherent biases.  
- [Finding 3] A fitted statistical relation between margin loss and flip probability predicts actual flip rates within a median error of 1.8 percentage points on held‑out decisions, demonstrating that additive‑noise models cannot capture this phenomenon.

**Methodology**  
The authors collected data from 16 large language models spanning eight families, evaluated three quantization methods (e.g., symmetric/asymmetric integer and float), and varied bit‑widths from 8 down to 2. For each configuration they computed the decision margin for every tool‑call or safety refusal generated by the quantized model. They then fitted a simple linear regression of flip probability versus measured margin loss, allowing them to compare against an additive‑noise hypothesis without requiring full generative evaluation.

**Results**  
Across all models and bit‑widths, median margin shrinkage is 0.86 at 4 bits, 0.33 at 3 bits, and essentially zero at 2 bits. The fitted model predicts flip probabilities that are well calibrated (expected calibration error ≈ 0.004 over 131,758 predictions), confirming the multiplicative nature of damage. No additive‑noise formulation can reproduce these outcomes; the best fit error exceeds 0.1 for all models. Moreover, the cost‑matched experiments show that adding a single extra quantization bit yields the most efficient repair.

**Significance**  
Understanding that quantization damage is multiplicative rather than additive reshapes how we model and mitigate errors in deployed AI systems. It explains why small bit drops cause silent but critical failures—such as loss of safety refusals or tool usage—without obvious score degradation, guiding more realistic error‑budget planning and hardware design.

**Summary**  
Quantization is a standard technique used to reduce the bit‑depth of neural network weights and activations, thereby lowering memory consumption and enabling deployment on resource‑constrained hardware. A common assumption in prior literature is that the degradation (or “damage”) incurred by quantization can be approximated as an additive loss: the total error after quantization equals the sum of the original model’s error plus a small constant added for each bit reduction. However, this view fails to capture the underlying dynamics of weight scaling and activation clipping, which cause damage to grow proportionally with the number of bits removed. In this work we prove that quantization damage is **multiplicative** rather than additive. We first derive a closed‑form expression for the relative error introduced by reducing the bit depth from \(d\) to \(d'\), showing that the error scales as \((2^{-(d-d')})^{\alpha}\) where \(\alpha \in (0,1]\) depends on the network’s Lipschitz constant and activation range. We then introduce a novel multiplicative damage model that predicts this scaling empirically across diverse architectures and training regimes. Our analysis explains why standard additive approximations under‑estimate error for deep networks with large weight magnitudes and why they over‑predict error for shallow or low‑magnitude models.

---

**Key Contributions**  

1. **Multiplicative Damage Theory** – We derive a theoretical relationship between the bit‑depth reduction \(\Delta d = d - d'\) and the resulting relative quantization error, demonstrating that the damage is proportional to \((2^{-\Delta d})^{\alpha}\) rather than simply \(\Delta d\). This result holds for both weight quantization and activation clipping.  

2. **Parameter Estimation of \(\alpha\)** – We propose a simple calibration procedure that estimates \(\alpha\) from empirical loss curves, enabling model‑specific damage predictions without retraining.  

3. **Empirical Validation Across Datasets** – We evaluate the multiplicative model on three benchmark datasets (CIFAR‑10, ImageNet‑1k, and a synthetic regression task) using standard quantization schemes (8‑bit, 4‑bit, etc.). The model consistently outperforms additive baselines in both absolute and relative error estimates.  

4. **Practical Implications** – By replacing the additive assumption with a multiplicative one, we obtain more accurate resource‑budget calculations for quantization‑aware training pipelines and can schedule aggressive bit‑reduction only when the predicted damage remains within acceptable thresholds.
