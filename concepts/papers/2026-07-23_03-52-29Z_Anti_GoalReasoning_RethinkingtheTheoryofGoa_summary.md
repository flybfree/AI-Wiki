## Summary  
The paper tackles the ambiguity that arises when non‑axiomatic logic (NAL) represents avoidance, showing that writing “avoid G” as ¬G conflates two distinct intentions—pursuing the negated event and avoiding a positive one—and can generate paradoxical reasoning. To resolve this, it introduces anti‑goal reasoning: a formal definition of anti‑goals and a mental operation called *prevent* that links avoidance to ordinary goal pursuit without treating ¬G as a goal itself. Four minimal case studies illustrate how the new rules correctly differentiate pursuit, passive avoidance, active prevention, and withholding action.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: A precise formal definition of anti‑goals distinct from simply negating a goal sentence.  
- Finding 2: The introduction of the *prevent* operation that connects anti‑goal reasoning to standard goal reasoning for active prevention.  
- Finding 3: Four minimal case studies that empirically verify the rules’ ability to distinguish pursuit, passive avoidance, active prevention, and inaction.

## Methodology  
The authors begin with NAL’s basic goal representation, identify where “avoid G” is interpreted as ¬G, and then extend the theory by defining anti‑goals as a separate class of goals. They introduce *prevent* as a mental operation that can actively block an event while preserving its desirability. Using these definitions, they analyze four simple scenarios—each representing one of the four action types—to derive explicit reasoning rules.

## Results  
The formal framework yields unambiguous predictions: when an agent has an anti‑goal to prevent G, it will execute a preventive action; when it merely avoids G without prevention, it will simply refrain from acting. The case studies confirm that active prevention is captured by *prevent* while passive avoidance remains ¬G with no action. Crucially, the paradoxical conversion of avoidance into pursuit disappears under this model.

## Significance  
By providing a clear theoretical model for anti‑goal reasoning in non‑axiomatic logic, the paper resolves longstanding ambiguities and offers a unified framework for adaptive systems that must both achieve and avoid events. This work is significant because it improves the reliability of goal‑driven decision making in domains where knowledge is incomplete and resources are limited.
