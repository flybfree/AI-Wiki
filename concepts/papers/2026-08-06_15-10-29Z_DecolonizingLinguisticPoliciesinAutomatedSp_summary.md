## Summary  
This paper argues that persistent failures of automatic speech recognition (ASR) for low‑resource, Indigenous, and non‑standard language varieties are not merely technical errors but implicit linguistic policies that reproduce colonial hierarchies. It introduces the Three Harms (Misrecognition, Misalignment, Mistrust), a seven‑layer situatedness model, and a participatory framework with a minimum audit protocol to achieve cross‑culturally competent speech AI.

## Key Contributions  
- Finding 1: Persistent failures in low‑resource languages reflect implicit linguistic policies that reproduce colonial hierarchies.  
- Finding 2: Introduces the Three Harms (3M) taxonomy—Misrecognition, Misalignment, Mistrust—and a seven‑layer situatedness model for linguistic diversity in ASR and voice interfaces.  
- Finding 3: Proposes a participatory framework with minimum audit protocol positioning affected communities as co‑designers, evaluators, and governance partners.

## Methodology  
The authors approach the problem by drawing on concepts of linguistic capital, raciolinguistic ideology, language policy research, and decolonial computing. They analyze how data, metrics, and model priors determine which voices become machine‑legible, synthesizing interdisciplinary scholarship to develop the 3M taxonomy and situatedness model rather than conducting new experiments.

## Results  
Theoretical: The 3M framework and seven‑layer model articulate how misrecognition, misalignment, mistrust arise from data scarcity, biased metrics, and exclusionary design. Practical: The participatory framework outlines concrete steps for co‑design, community evaluation, and audit protocols to ensure culturally competent ASR.

## Significance  
This matters because it challenges the assumption that technical fixes alone resolve inequities; by treating language policy as a colonial construct, the paper offers tools to democratize speech AI and improve access for marginalized groups.
