## Summary  
The paper investigates how the diffusion of large language models (LLMs) has reshaped scientific exploration, collaboration, and labor division among researchers. It links PubMed Central full‑text records with OpenAlex publication and collaboration histories to analyze CRediT contribution statements across 137,120 multi‑author papers. After 2022, scientists show increased interdisciplinary work, especially among established scholars from low‑middle‑income countries, and a shift in team roles toward more specialized functions.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Scientists' research becomes more interdisciplinary after 2022, with higher cross‑disciplinary publication rates than before.  
- [Finding 2] AI‑writing signal correlates with earlier interdisciplinary tendencies; the gap widens post‑LLM adoption among authors with strong AI writing signals.  
- [Finding 3] Division of labor within research teams becomes more differentiated: contributors report narrower role sets, coauthors share fewer common roles, and role profiles become fluid.  

## Methodology  
The authors combined PubMed Central full text records (775,323 scientists) with OpenAlex publication and collaboration histories to construct a dataset of 137,120 multi‑author papers. They extracted CRediT contribution statements for each author, coded them into role categories, and computed interdisciplinary indices by measuring the distance between disciplines of co‑authors. Temporal analysis was performed before (pre‑2022) and after (post‑2022) the LLM surge to identify shifts.  

## Results  
Post‑2022, interdisciplinary publication rates rose 18 % on average, especially among established scientists from non‑English low‑middle‑income countries. AI‑writing signal authors showed a 34 % increase in disciplinary distance compared with those without the signal. Role diversity fell: average coauthor role overlap dropped from 0.62 to 0.41, and role set size decreased by 9 %. Software and validation roles grew while conceptual and management roles declined.  

## Significance  
These findings reveal that LLMs have accelerated a structural reconfiguration of scientific labor, moving research toward specialized, fluid teams rather than integrated groups. They inform policy on equitable access to AI tools and suggest that interdisciplinary collaboration may be less dependent on shared expertise when AI assists writing.
