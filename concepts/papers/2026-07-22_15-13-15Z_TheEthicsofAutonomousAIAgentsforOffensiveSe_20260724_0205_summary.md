## Summary  
The paper investigates the ethical implications of employing autonomous AI agents in offensive security, arguing that these tools introduce three distinct dimensions of indeterminacy—non‑deterministic policy, open‑ended impact, and an indeterminate user population—that together enable the industrialization of offensive capability. By mapping how moral attribution becomes diffuse across users, developers, and third parties, the authors highlight a gap in existing cybersecurity and AI‑ethics frameworks that were not designed for this combination. Their contribution is both theoretical—identifying these three interrelated properties—and practical: they provide stratified recommendations to mitigate risks while preserving legitimate security research.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Autonomous agents exhibit indeterminacy along three independent dimensions: non‑deterministic policy, open‑ended impact, and an indeterminate user population.  
- [Finding 2] Moral attribution becomes diffuse among users, tool‑makers, and third parties, complicating responsibility assignment.  
- [Finding 3] The technology enables the industrialization of offensive capability and may democratize defensive practice through lowered skill requirements.  

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem by conducting a theoretical analysis that maps the interaction between dual‑use cybersecurity tools and AI ethics, employing stakeholder impact mapping to trace how responsibility is distributed across participants. They also examined existing frameworks for offensive security and AI governance to identify gaps and propose a set of stratified recommendations tailored to different deployment contexts.  

## Results  
The main results are theoretical rather than experimental: the authors formally articulate the three dimensions of indeterminacy, demonstrate that no current dual‑use framework addresses their combined effect, and present a taxonomy of stakeholder impacts that leads to concrete mitigation strategies. These findings provide a clear analytical lens for evaluating the ethical landscape of autonomous offensive security agents.  

## Significance  
This work matters because it underscores how emerging AI tools can reshape power dynamics in cybersecurity, potentially widening the gap between attackers and defenders while also lowering barriers to defensive practice. By exposing the diffusion of moral responsibility, the paper calls for new governance mechanisms that can balance innovation with accountability, thereby influencing policy, industry standards, and future research directions.
