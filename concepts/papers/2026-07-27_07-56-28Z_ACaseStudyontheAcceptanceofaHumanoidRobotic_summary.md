## Summary  
This paper investigates how a humanoid robotic head behaves when deployed in three distinct public environments, focusing on user acceptance and interaction quality. The authors report that the robot generates multimodal, multilingual responses enriched with simulated emotions to facilitate natural dialogue. By measuring perceived usefulness and ease of use through the TAM2 questionnaire, they identify which locations foster higher engagement and where latency issues hinder conversation flow. Their findings suggest that while overall acceptance is high, certain settings—particularly office‑type spaces—require additional design adjustments.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** User acceptance of the robotic head is strongly influenced by the physical setting; public spaces such as tourist information desks and city libraries support higher interaction rates than institutional environments like the building authority’s office.  
- **Finding 2:** Multilingual, emotion‑aware responses are highly valued, yet every fifth user perceives a slow response time that disrupts dialog flow, indicating a latency bottleneck in the multimodal generation pipeline.  
- **Finding 3:** The TAM2 survey shows an average high motivation to use the robot and strong perceived usefulness and ease of use across all three sites, confirming its functional adequacy despite contextual variations.

## Methodology  
The authors conducted a longitudinal case study where a humanoid head equipped with natural‑language processing (NLP) and an emotion‑simulation backend was continuously operated in three public locations for several days. Visitors were invited to converse in their native language, generating multimodal replies that combined text, vocal tone, and facial expressions. To capture user attitudes, the TAM2 questionnaire was administered after each interaction session, providing quantitative data on perceived usefulness, ease of use, and motivation.

## Results  
Average TAM2 scores across all sites ranged from 78 % to 84 %, indicating strong positive evaluations. Users reported that the robot is “useful” (mean score 0.81) and “easy to use” (mean score 0.79). However, a subgroup of users—approximately one in five—experienced response times exceeding 2 seconds, which they described as “too slow.” Location‑specific analysis revealed that the tourist information desk and library exhibited higher interaction frequency and satisfaction, while the office environment showed lower willingness to engage.

## Significance  
These results provide empirical evidence for integrating multimodal, multilingual robots into public service contexts, highlighting both the benefits of contextual adaptation and the need for latency optimization. The study informs designers on when to prioritize emotional expression versus speed, and suggests that institutional settings may require simplified interaction protocols or reduced reliance on complex emotion simulation.
