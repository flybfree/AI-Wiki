## Summary  
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) are reshaping Capture the Flag (CTF) competitions and raises concerns about fairness, ranking validity, and whether participation still yields genuine learning. By mapping the current human‑machine capability boundary across cryptography, web exploitation, and binary exploitation, the authors identify that easy and intermediate challenges are now reliably automated while narrower sub‑categories remain challenging. The study also uncovers a deeper source of community disagreement: an undeclared question about what a competition is for. To address this, the authors propose a four‑component safeguard framework—tiered divisions, LLM‑resistant challenge design, investigative telemetry, and a draft code of conduct—coupled with a decision tool that ties these measures to a competition’s declared purpose.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] LLMs can now solve easy and intermediate challenges in cryptography, web exploitation, and binary exploitation with minimal human input, while more specialized sub‑categories continue to resist automation.  
- [Finding 2] The community’s debate about AI permission is downstream of an underlying question concerning the fundamental purpose of a competition, which influences fairness judgments.  
- [Finding 3] A four‑component safeguard framework (tiered divisions, LLM‑resistant design, telemetry for investigation, draft code of conduct) and a decision tool that links safeguards to a declared competition purpose are proposed as practical responses.  

## Methodology  
The authors employed a mixed‑methods approach: they synthesized published benchmarks, including a recent government evaluation; conducted case studies across three challenge categories (cryptography, web exploitation, binary exploitation); observed public discussion channels where the community debates AI use; and performed semi‑structured interviews with experienced players and organizers. This triangulation allowed them to capture both quantitative capability trends and qualitative attitudes toward AI participation.  

## Results  
The analysis maps the human‑machine capability boundary by category, revealing that easy and intermediate challenges are now reliably automated while narrower sub‑categories remain unsolved. The authors also document community disagreement about AI use, which they attribute to an unaddressed question of competition purpose. Their framework identifies four safeguards—tiered divisions to separate skill levels, challenge design that resists LLM solutions, telemetry for investigative purposes only, and a draft code of conduct—and provides a decision tool that evaluates whether these safeguards align with the competition’s declared goal.  

## Significance  
This work matters because it challenges the assumption that CTF rankings reflect genuine skill when AI can solve many problems automatically, potentially eroding learning value and fairness. By extending the analysis beyond CTFs to any cybersecurity setting where results are taken as evidence of ability, the paper offers a broader perspective on AI impact in technical competitions. The proposed safeguards could help preserve educational integrity while acknowledging technological advances.
