## Summary  

Surprisal Theory, as it is commonly presented in contemporary Bayesian and information‑theoretic literature, claims that the epistemic value of a hypothesis can be measured by the amount of “surprise” (i.e., negative log‑likelihood) it generates when observed data are presented. The argument proceeds from a naïve identification between surprise and rationality: if a hypothesis is surprising, then it must be true; conversely, if a hypothesis is not surprising, it cannot be true. This line of reasoning is presented here as **tautological**—it merely restates the definition of surprisal without providing any independent rational grounding for why surprise should be taken as a normative epistemic criterion. The present analysis demonstrates that Surprisal Theory fails to satisfy the standard criteria of rationality (e.g., coherence, predictive power, and logical consistency) because its core premise is circular: it equates a descriptive statistical measure with a prescriptive normative rule without supplying any justification for that identification.

## Key Contributions  

1. **Explicit Identification of the Tautology** – The analysis isolates the precise point at which Surprisal Theory becomes tautological, showing that the inference “surprise ⇒ truth” is logically equivalent to “truth ⇒ surprise,” thereby collapsing into a circular definition.  
2. **Critique of Rational Grounding** – By contrasting Surprisal Theory with more robust epistemic frameworks (e.g., Bayesian decision theory and evidentialism), the contribution highlights where Surprisal Theory lacks an independent rational justification for linking statistical surprise to normative belief updating.  
3. **Methodological Clarification** – The work clarifies the assumptions underlying surprisal calculations (e.g., independence of data, normalised likelihoods) and demonstrates how violations of these assumptions can render the theory vacuous or misleading.  
4. **Implications for Bayesian Practice** – By exposing the theoretical flaw, the analysis urges practitioners to adopt alternative measures of hypothesis evaluation that are grounded in logical coherence rather than mere statistical surprise.

This equivalence eliminates any substantive content beyond a definitional loop.  

2. **Absence of Rational Justification** – A systematic survey of standard rational criteria (e.g., the principle of sufficient reason, coherence with prior evidence) reveals that none can be invoked to justify the leap from statistical surprise to normative belief endorsement.
