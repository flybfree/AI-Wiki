## Summary
This paper investigates the behavioral consequences of swapping Large Language Models (LLMs) within multi-session agent frameworks, a common strategy for balancing performance and inference costs. The authors identify a phenomenon termed "behavior transfer," where a recipient model inadvertently adopts reasoning and action patterns from the model that generated its conversation history. To mitigate this issue, the study proposes a novel technique called Direction- and Authorship-aware Summarization of History (DASH). This method aims to preserve desirable behaviors from stronger models while minimizing the negative impact of weaker models influencing stronger ones, thereby improving the stability and quality of multi-model orchestration.

## Key Contributions
- **Identification of Behavior Transfer:** The authors empirically demonstrate that when an LLM receives a raw conversation history generated by a different model, it tends to mimic the reasoning styles and action patterns of the previous model, regardless of its own inherent capabilities.
- **Mitigation via Summarization:** The study finds that summarizing conversation history significantly reduces the negative effects of behavior transfer compared to raw-history transfer, suggesting that abstraction acts as a buffer against behavioral contamination.
- **Proposal of DASH Framework:** The authors introduce DASH, a specialized summarization technique that accounts for the direction of the model swap (weak-to-strong vs. strong-to-weak) and the authorship of the history. This approach outperforms naive summarization and raw transfer in maintaining consistent agent behavior.

## Methodology
The researchers conducted controlled experiments using multi-session tasks to simulate real-world agent workflows where models are swapped to optimize cost or performance. They compared three primary approaches to history transfer: raw-history transfer, naive summarization, and their proposed DASH method. The experimental setup involved repeated model swaps to observe how different transfer methods affected the recipient model's reasoning consistency and task performance. By analyzing the outputs, they measured the extent to which the recipient model adopted the "habits" of the preceding model, focusing on reasoning patterns and action selection.

## Results
Experimental results indicate that raw-history transfer leads to the most significant behavior transfer, where weaker models can negatively influence stronger models, or stronger models can impose rigid patterns on weaker ones. Naive summarization reduces this effect but may lose critical contextual information. In contrast, DASH effectively preserves the beneficial reasoning patterns from stronger models while filtering out undesirable habits from weaker models. In repeated swap scenarios, DASH consistently outperformed both raw transfer and naive baselines in maintaining high-quality, consistent agent behavior, demonstrating its effectiveness in multi-model orchestration.

## Significance
This research is significant for the practical deployment of multi-model LLM agents, which are increasingly used to balance computational costs with performance. It highlights that model swapping is not merely a technical switch but a behavioral intervention that can degrade agent performance if not managed carefully. By providing a concrete method (DASH) to control behavior transfer, the paper offers a practical solution for developers to maintain agent consistency and reliability in complex, multi-session environments. It shifts the focus from simply selecting the best model to managing the behavioral continuity across model boundaries.
