## Summary  
The paper argues that many automated scientific‑discovery planners are limited by a myopic maximisation of immediate information gain, which can prevent them from selecting constructive experiments that provide future capabilities. It shows that within any fixed horizon an action that builds a capability may be observationally indistinguishable from a null action, causing the planner to ignore it and either never reach its goal or incur an unbounded approximation ratio. The authors formalise goal‑directed discovery as a stochastic shortest‑path problem in belief space and introduce a capability‑aware cost‑to‑go heuristic that incorporates both immediate information and future capability value.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Myopic planners cannot value constructive experiments because they yield no information within the horizon, leading to an unbounded approximation ratio or failure to reach the goal on specially constructed instances.  
- [Finding 2] The problem is modelled as a stochastic shortest‑path in belief space; the authors prove that for every lookahead depth there exists an instance where any myopic planner has an unbounded approximation ratio and another where it never reaches the goal.  
- [Finding 3] They introduce capability gating as a distinct reachability axis, separate from submodularity (curvature) and information order (adaptivity gaps), and propose CG‑Plan with a cost‑to‑go heuristic \(h = h_{\text{cap}} + h_{\exp}\).  

## Methodology  
The authors treat the discovery process as navigating a belief graph where each action updates the state. Constructive actions modify the downstream action graph by adding new capabilities, which can only be observed later. They prove a capability‑indistinguishability lemma: within any horizon \(d\), acquiring a capability is observationally equivalent to a null action, so myopic planners treat it as worthless. Using this framework they design CG‑Plan, an incremental replanner that computes a cost‑to‑go value combining immediate information gain and the potential future capabilities.  

## Results  
Theoretically, for any horizon \(d\) there exists an instance where every myopic information‑maximising planner has an unbounded approximation ratio to the optimal plan, and another instance where it never reaches the goal. Experimentally, CG‑Plan’s performance gap appears only when capability gating is active; the gap persists across fixed horizons and occurs specifically with near‑miss hypotheses generated by a data‑consistent proposer.  

## Significance  
Automated discovery systems risk suboptimal or non‑terminating planning if they rely solely on myopic scores, overlooking actions that enable future progress. By formalising capability gating as a reachability axis and providing CG‑Plan, the work offers a principled way to guide planners toward long‑term goals rather than short‑sighted information extraction.
