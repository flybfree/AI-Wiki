## Summary  
This paper investigates whether token probabilities generated by large language models (LLMs) can serve as reliable signals for estimating the correctness of mathematical answers, with a focus on calibration—aligning confidence estimates with actual accuracy. The authors explore both single-pass and multi-pass approaches to confidence estimation, finding that aggregating token probabilities across full sequences yields more informative and consistent signals than individual tokens. They evaluate two multi-pass strategies: self-verification via re-prompting (including an in-situ variant) and Monte Carlo Dropout for stochastic uncertainty quantification. The study also analyzes post-hoc calibration methods like Platt scaling and isotonic regression, which improve calibration but exhibit variable data efficiency and dataset-specific transfer issues.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Single-pass confidence estimation using token probabilities is often overconfident due to saturation in high-probability regions, limiting its reliability for calibration.  
- [Finding 2] Aggregating token probabilities across the full answer sequence reveals small but consistent differences between correct and incorrect generations, enabling more informative confidence estimates than single-token signals.  
- [Finding 3] Multi-pass methods such as self-verification and Monte Carlo Dropout can produce calibrated confidence estimates, while post-hoc calibration techniques like Platt scaling and isotonic regression reduce in-domain calibration error.

## Methodology  
The authors adopt a comparative empirical approach to assess confidence estimation for mathematical question answering. They generate answers using LLMs with varying confidence signals: single-pass methods use the final token probabilities from generation; multi-pass methods incorporate additional uncertainty estimates via re-prompting or stochastic forward passes (e.g., Monte Carlo Dropout). Post-hoc calibration is applied by transforming raw logits into calibrated probabilities using Platt scaling and isotonic regression. The study evaluates these methods across multiple datasets, measuring calibration error (e.g., Brier score) and accuracy trade-offs.

## Results  
The results show that single-token confidence signals are frequently overconfident due to saturation in token probability distributions. In contrast, sequence-aggregated token probabilities provide more balanced estimates with lower variance. Monte Carlo Dropout yields calibrated confidence by measuring output variation across stochastic forward passes, though it increases computational cost. Post-hoc calibration methods significantly reduce calibration error—up to 40% improvement on some datasets—but their effectiveness depends heavily on dataset difficulty and model architecture. Platt scaling is less data-efficient on harder tasks, while isotonic regression adapts better but requires more training time.

## Significance  
This work bridges the gap between raw token outputs and reliable confidence estimation in LLMs, offering practical pathways to improve decision-making in high-stakes applications like automated grading or medical diagnosis. By demonstrating that calibrated confidence is achievable through simple aggregation or stochastic sampling, the study supports more robust AI systems where uncertainty must be quantified accurately.
