## Summary  
This study investigates how blind and low‑vision (BLV) scientists as well as sighted researchers use artificial‑intelligence question‑answering tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini to explore multimodal scientific papers that contain figures, tables, and diagrams. By interviewing ten participants across diverse STEM fields, the authors uncover both existing practices—including reliance on static alternative text—and new accessibility workarounds enabled by AI QA. The research also documents how vague or incorrect AI outputs can lead users to abandon the workflow, highlighting a critical gap in current multimodal query systems. A valuable contribution is the creation of an 115‑item dataset that captures real‑world queries and model responses for authentic scientific documents.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Blind/low‑vision scientists primarily rely on static alternative text to access figures, while AI QA remains a niche but feasible practice.  
- [Finding 2] Vague or incomplete image descriptions, as well as incorrect AI responses, are strong predictors of users abandoning the AI workflow.  
- [Finding 3] The authors provide a curated dataset of 115 queries and corresponding AI‑generated answers for multimodal scientific papers.

## Methodology  
The researchers conducted semi‑structured interviews with five blind/low‑vision scientists and five sighted scientists from different STEM disciplines (e.g., physics, biology, computer science). Participants were asked to use both ChatGPT and Gemini to ask questions about visual content in their field’s literature. The interview protocol recorded how each participant navigated the AI tools, noted any accessibility workarounds, and captured feedback on response quality. A total of 115 distinct queries and model responses were collected and stored as a reproducible dataset.

## Results  
Interviews revealed that BLV users often supplement static alt text with interactive visual QA, whereas sighted users frequently skip the AI step altogether. The most common reason for disengagement was ambiguous or missing image metadata; when the AI could not generate a clear answer, participants switched back to manual reading. Both groups reported frustration when AI outputs were factually wrong, leading to abandonment of the tool. The dataset includes query‑answer pairs that illustrate these patterns and can be used to evaluate future multimodal QA systems.

## Significance  
Understanding how different abilities interact with AI‑driven visual exploration is essential for designing inclusive scientific information retrieval tools. By exposing the pitfalls of vague descriptions and inaccurate answers, this work guides developers toward more robust, accessible models that respect both technical quality and user needs across abilities.
