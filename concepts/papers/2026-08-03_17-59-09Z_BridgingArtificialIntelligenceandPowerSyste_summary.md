## Summary  
This paper addresses the growing demand for hands‑on AI tools that are grounded in power‑system engineering rather than generic black‑box models. By mapping core artificial‑intelligence concepts onto specific power‑system tasks, the authors deliver an open, executable module library that lowers entry barriers for students and practitioners. The framework includes deep neural network templates, a domain‑coupled CNN surrogate, and advanced modules such as DNN‑assisted optimization, deep reinforcement learning, and physics‑informed neural networks. These resources are packaged as Jupyter notebooks and distributed through an IEEE online course and PES webinar series.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A progressive difficulty ladder that aligns AI concepts with representative power‑system problems, enabling learners to progress from simple function approximation to complex control tasks.  
- [Finding 2] An open, executable module library (Jupyter notebooks) that runs locally or on Google Colab, providing reusable code for DNN, CNN, DRL, and PINN applications in power systems.  
- [Finding 3] A community‑driven validation study showing high engagement: a webinar attracted over 590 live attendees and the repository received more than 344 visits within two weeks.

## Methodology  
The authors first conducted a survey of researchers and practitioners to identify common barriers (92 % report at least one barrier) and strong interest in a power‑specific AI course (94 %). Using this feedback, they designed three progressive modules: (i) foundational DNN templates for load‑curve fitting; (ii) a CNN surrogate of the 5‑bus power flow; and (iii) advanced modules on DNN‑assisted optimization, deep reinforcement learning for battery storage control, and PINNs for the swing equation. All notebooks are released under an IEEE course and PES webinar platform.

## Results  
The webinar series generated strong interest, with 590 live attendees—among the ten most‑attended IEEE PES webinars—and over 344 repository visits within two weeks, indicating high adoption. The executable modules have been demonstrated to run without external dependencies and produce comparable results to state‑of‑the‑art methods on benchmark power‑system datasets.

## Significance  
This work bridges the gap between artificial intelligence research and power‑system education by providing a pedagogical pathway that respects engineering fundamentals while leveraging modern AI techniques. It offers educators reusable, low‑cost resources and encourages interdisciplinary collaboration, potentially accelerating the integration of AI into real‑world grid operations.
