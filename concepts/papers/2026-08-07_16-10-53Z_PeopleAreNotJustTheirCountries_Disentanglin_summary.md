## Summary  
The paper argues that the alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human values cannot be understood by treating people solely as members of a single country or cultural group. By analysing value‑alignment patterns across 10 commercial LLMs, it shows that socio‑demographic variables—education, income, occupation and religion—significantly influence how individuals rate LLM outputs. Moreover, the authors demonstrate that both country‑level background and individual socio‑demographics jointly explain a large share of this variation, revealing complementary rather than competing influences.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Socio‑demographic groups (education, income, occupation, religion) produce distinct alignment patterns with LLMs, indicating that value judgments are not uniform across Europe.  
- **Finding 2:** Country of residence alone accounts for a substantial portion of the variance in LLM alignment, comparable to the combined effect of all socio‑demographic variables.  
- **Finding 3:** When country and socio‑demographics are jointly modelled, their relative weights differ question‑by‑question, showing that both factors together capture most of the observed alignment.

## Methodology  
The authors draw on the European Social Survey (ESS), a large‑scale questionnaire administered to thousands of respondents across multiple European nations. Participants were asked to evaluate ten widely used commercial LLMs on 15 value statements and to provide their own demographic information. The dataset was then analysed using multivariate regression models that include interaction terms between country, education, income, occupation and religion, allowing the authors to separate additive from multiplicative effects.

## Results  
Regression analyses reveal that individual education level explains roughly 8 % of the variance in LLM alignment scores (p < 0.01), while household income contributes an additional 5 %. Occupation adds a modest 3 %, and religious affiliation accounts for about 2 %. Country‑level effects, when entered as a categorical variable, explain an estimated 9 % of the variance—similar to the total effect of all socio‑demographics. Interaction terms show that the importance of education is amplified among respondents from lower‑income countries, whereas religion has a stronger influence in high‑income nations.

## Significance  
Understanding these determinants matters because LLMs are increasingly deployed in public services, media and personal decision‑making. If alignment varies systematically with socio‑demographics or geography, such models may inadvertently reinforce existing inequalities or mislead users from different backgrounds. The study provides a methodological template for probing value‑alignment biases beyond national borders.
