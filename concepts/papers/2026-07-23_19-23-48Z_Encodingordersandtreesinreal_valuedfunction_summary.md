## Summary  
The paper develops function‑theoretic analogues of quantitative results on extracting order properties from large 2‑trees encoded as binary relations, improving the bounds that can be obtained from earlier work. It introduces two main theorems: Theorem 1.11 extracts a less restrictive threshold from a tree with significantly tighter constants than prior methods targeting more restrictive thresholds, and Theorem 1.14 provides a new proof of Daskalakis‑Golowich’s result on extracting “tight thresholds” while improving the quantitative analysis. Together these results resolve two open problems in combinatorial learning theory.

## Key Contributions  
- **Theorem 1.11** – Extracts a weaker threshold from a tree, yielding substantially better bounds than earlier approaches that required stronger threshold conditions.  
- **Theorem 1.14** – Gives a fresh proof of the tight‑threshold extraction result by Daskalakis and Golowich, improving constants and correcting an erroneous claim made by Jung et al.  
- **Resolution of open problems** – Provides at most double‑exponential bounds on dual sequential fat‑shattering and corrects the earlier proof that Jung, Kim, and Tewari claimed was flawed.

## Methodology  
The authors employ a function‑theoretic framework rooted in statistical learning theory. They consider real‑valued functions whose order properties are governed by thresholds, and they analyze 2‑trees encoded as binary relations using the notion of sequential fat‑shattering dimension. By comparing their combinatorial bounds with earlier results on stable functions, they derive improved quantitative regularity lemmas.

## Results  
Theorem 1.11 improves the bound for threshold extraction from a tree, allowing tighter regularity estimates for stable functions. Theorem 1.14 refines the tight‑threshold result, achieving at most double‑exponential dual sequential fat‑shattering and correcting the earlier erroneous proof of Jung et al. These advances give sharper theoretical guarantees.

## Significance  
The refined bounds strengthen the theoretical foundations of learning theory, offering algorithmic stability analyses that depend on more precise regularity estimates. Resolving these open problems also clarifies the relationship between tree‑based encodings and function thresholds, which is valuable for both combinatorial and statistical learning contexts.
