## Summary  
This paper investigates why human‑subject research is underutilized in AI safety and ethics despite its evident value for generating empirical evidence. By surveying 93 AI Safety & Ethics (AISE) researchers and conducting 17 in‑depth interviews, the authors uncover a complex set of epistemic, practical, and cultural barriers that limit the adoption of human studies. The study reveals an “epistemic fit” problem: many technical researchers view human methods as less valid or reliable compared to benchmark‑based approaches. The findings suggest that bridging these gaps requires deliberate alignment of research priorities, resources, and methodological standards across disciplinary subfields.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Human research is perceived as valuable for generating evidence but faces significant epistemic resistance from technical AI researchers who prioritize model‑centric benchmarks over empirical studies.  
- [Finding 2] Tangible resource constraints, including limited funding, data access, and methodological expertise, act as practical barriers that discourage human‑subject work.  
- [Finding 3] Institutional and infrastructural limitations—such as lack of standardized protocols for ethical approvals in AI research and fragmented collaboration across Technical, Sociotechnical, Governance, and Normative subfields—hinder the scalability of human studies.  

## Methodology  
The authors employed a mixed‑methods approach: first, they administered an online survey to 93 AISE researchers from four disciplinary backgrounds to quantify attitudes toward human research; second, they conducted semi‑structured interviews with 17 participants selected for thematic depth. The survey captured self‑reported confidence in the validity of human methods and perceived resource availability, while the interviews explored nuanced concerns about epistemic fit, methodological preferences, and systemic obstacles.  

## Results  
Survey results indicated that only 38 % of respondents felt human research had high evidential value, compared with 71 % who rated technical benchmarks as more reliable. Resource barriers were cited by 62 % of participants as a primary obstacle, with funding and data access being the most frequently mentioned issues. Interviews revealed that many researchers view human methods as “performative” when they lack rigorous design or transparent reporting, reinforcing concerns about epistemic fit.  

## Significance  
Understanding these barriers is crucial because AI safety decisions increasingly rely on empirical evidence; without robust human studies, policy and technical safeguards may remain speculative. The paper offers a roadmap for aligning research agendas across subfields to recognize the epistemic value of human data, thereby reducing performative “human‑washing” and fostering genuinely interdisciplinary AI safety work.
