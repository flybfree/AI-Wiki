## Summary  
This paper investigates how students from Canadian and South Korean universities perceive the ethicality and rule‑compliance of using generative AI (GenAI) in coding assignments, despite both institutions adopting identical policies. By employing a scenario‑based survey administered in Fall 2024, the authors compare cross‑cultural judgments of academic integrity and institutional policy. The study contributes to the literature by showing that cultural factors—not merely policy wording—drive these divergent perceptions. Its findings suggest that AI‑use guidelines must be culturally responsive to support responsible GenAI integration worldwide.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Canadian students are consistently more likely than Korean students to view GenAI use as unethical and against institutional policies, even though the policies are functionally identical.  
- [Finding 2] The proportion of AI‑generated code incorporated into assignments is the strongest predictor of ethical judgments across both cultural groups.  
- [Finding 3] Hofstede’s cultural dimensions—particularly power distance, individualism, and uncertainty avoidance—significantly shape students’ ethical reasoning about GenAI.

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem through a cross‑cultural experimental design: they created identical scenario prompts for Canadian and South Korean students, collected responses via an online survey in Fall 2024, and then applied statistical tests (Mann‑Whitney U tests and correlation coefficients) to compare groups. The scenarios varied only in the amount of AI‑generated code required, allowing the authors to isolate its influence on ethical judgments.

## Results  
Statistical analysis revealed significant differences across nearly all scenarios; Canadian respondents scored higher on perceived unethical use than Korean respondents (p < 0.01). Moreover, the correlation between the percentage of AI‑generated code and ethical scores was stronger in Canada (r = 0.68) than in Korea (r = 0.42), confirming that the amount of generated code is a key factor. The authors also found that higher power distance and lower individualism scores correlated with stricter ethical judgments, supporting the cultural‑dimension interpretation.

## Significance  
These results matter because they demonstrate that AI‑use policies cannot be one‑size‑fits‑all; cultural values affect how students interpret them. Policymakers must therefore design AI‑integration strategies that respect local conceptions of academic integrity while upholding core honesty principles, fostering equitable and responsible GenAI adoption across diverse educational contexts.
