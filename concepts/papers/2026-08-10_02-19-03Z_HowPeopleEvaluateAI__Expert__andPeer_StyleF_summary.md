## Summary  
This study investigates how consumers evaluate three distinct sources of financial advice—AI‑generated, expert‑certified, and peer‑produced—by holding the substantive content identical while varying only the communication style and source attribution. The experiment reveals that perceived expertise and trustworthiness are shaped not just by the label shown but also by subtle cues embedded in each message’s tone and reasoning. By separating attribution effects from content‑specific cues, the authors demonstrate that disclosure functions as an interpretive frame whose accuracy interacts with message cues to influence judgments of fit and quality. The contribution is a nuanced account of how trust formation in financial advice operates jointly across source identity and communication style.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** Expert‑style advice receives higher overall ratings than AI‑generated advice on nine out of ten outcome dimensions, with effect sizes ranging from d = 0.20 to 0.47, indicating a consistent but modest preference for human expertise.  
- **Finding 2:** When source labels are absent, the Expert advantage expands to eight outcomes (d up to 0.60), suggesting that perceived credibility drives evaluations even without explicit attribution.  
- **Finding 3:** Mislabeling the AI advice as expert amplifies its situational‑fit ratings by d = 0.42 and reduces the Expert’s advantage in fit by d = –0.36, highlighting how label accuracy modulates trust.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a preregistered vignette experiment with N = 285 participants. Each vignette contained identical financial facts, numerical values, recommendation direction, and logical reasoning, but the communication style varied: AI‑Financial Assistant (AI), Certified Financial Planner (Expert), or Online Community Forum (OC). Source attribution was manipulated through three conditions—correctly labeled, unlabeled, and mislabeled—allowing separation of attribution effects from content cues. Participants rated each advice on nine outcome dimensions including factual accuracy, recommendation direction, situational fit, and overall quality.

## Results  
Expert advice outperformed AI advice on 9/10 outcomes (d = 0.20–0.47) and remained superior without labels on 8/10 outcomes (up to d = 0.60). Correct labeling added little differentiation, while mislabeling increased ratings of AI advice for situational fit and overall quality (both d = 0.42). The Expert advantage in situational fit was attenuated by mislabeling (d = –0.36). Descriptive analyses confirmed that AI advice’s impact was most pronounced when the “AI” label was displayed, whereas style‑specific differences were most visible under an AI label.

## Significance  
These findings matter because they challenge the assumption that simply disclosing a source is neutral; instead, disclosure interacts with message cues to shape trust and reliance. Understanding this dynamic can inform design of financial‑advice platforms, regulatory guidance on labeling, and strategies for mitigating bias in automated decision support systems.
