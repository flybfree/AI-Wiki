## Summary  
The paper investigates how emotions affect multi‑agent negotiation outcomes for large language model agents, showing that emotional states dramatically influence acceptance rates and price negotiations. It contributes three findings: (1) angry buyers rarely reach agreement while happy buyers achieve a high deal rate but obtain worse prices; (2) the impact of emotion is role‑dependent—buyer emotions primarily drive acceptance/rejection whereas seller emotions shape concession dynamics; and (3) these emotional dynamics affect not only language but also termination behavior and price trajectories across multiple LLMs.  

## Key Contributions  
- Emotions strongly shape negotiation outcomes, with angry buyers achieving a 0.39 % deal rate compared to happy buyers at 28.91 %.  
- The effect is role‑dependent: buyer emotions mainly affect whether an agreement is accepted or rejected, while seller emotions primarily influence concession dynamics and price concessions.  
- These emotional effects alter language patterns, termination behavior, and the evolution of price trajectories across five widely used LLMs.  

## Methodology  
The authors constructed a controlled framework in which 350 real consumer products are negotiated under two budget conditions. Buyer and seller agents are independently assigned one of six emotional states (happy, sad, angry, fearful, neutral, excited) in all possible pairings, yielding 36 emotion‑pair settings. Five widely used LLMs are evaluated as the negotiating partners. The study measures deal rates, price differences, language content, termination events, and price trajectories to capture the full scope of emotional influence.  

## Results  
Across the 36 emotion‑pair scenarios and five LLMs, happy buyers reached agreement most often but obtained lower prices than fearful buyers, whose deals were more favorable. Angry buyers rarely agreed (0.39 % rate). Seller emotions modulated concession magnitude: angry sellers tended to concede less, while fearful sellers offered larger discounts. Price trajectories diverged according to emotional states, and termination occurred significantly more frequently when either party was highly negative.  

## Significance  
Understanding the role of emotions is crucial for designing robust LLM agents in commerce; ignoring affective cues leads to suboptimal negotiations, higher transaction costs, and potential frustration for users. The findings highlight that ethical AI design must account for emotional conditioning to improve fairness, efficiency, and user satisfaction in automated bargaining systems.
