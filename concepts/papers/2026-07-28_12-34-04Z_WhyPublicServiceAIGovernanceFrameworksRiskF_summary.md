## Summary  
The paper argues that public‑service AI governance frameworks, built around narrow AI safety concepts such as accuracy and bias mitigation, are ill‑suited to general‑purpose AI (GPAI) because GPAI’s unbounded outputs render those concepts inapplicable. By analysing the policing sector – where failure has severe consequences – the authors show that the two dominant safeguards (expert evaluation and human‑in‑the‑loop oversight) rest on assumptions that GPAI violates, effectively turning safety into an optional add‑on rather than an intrinsic feature of the system. Their contribution is a clear taxonomic distinction between narrow and general‑purpose AI in governance documentation and a recommendation to pause GPAI deployment until evidence proves responsible use.  

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** General‑purpose AI undermines traditional safety concepts (accuracy, bias, explainability, accountability) because its outputs are unbounded free‑text judgments rather than categorical predictions.  
- **Finding 2:** The two policing‑AI mitigations—expert evaluation and human‑in‑the‑loop oversight—depend on the very properties GPAI eliminates, making safety assurance an afterthought.  
- **Finding 3:** A taxonomy that separates narrow from general‑purpose AI should be mandated in governance documents, accompanied by a pause on operational GPAI deployment until robust evidence exists.  

## Methodology  
The authors approached the problem through a mixed methodology: first, they performed a theoretical analysis of how GPAI’s characteristics (generality, accessibility, low cost) conflict with safety‑focused AI governance; second, they conducted a case study of policing AI systems to illustrate concrete failures; third, they evaluated the efficacy of expert evaluation and human‑in‑the‑loop mechanisms under GPAI constraints. This combination of conceptual critique and empirical illustration allowed them to derive actionable policy recommendations.  

## Results  
Theoretical results demonstrate that accuracy cannot be quantified over unbounded outputs, bias cannot be disaggregated from free‑text judgments, explainability is replaced by superficial explanations, and accountability erodes as models optimise for persuasion rather than truth. Empirically, the case study shows that expert evaluation assumes bounded model behaviour, while human‑in‑the‑loop oversight fails to intervene when GPAI produces persuasive but misleading outputs. Consequently, safety assurance shifts from an intrinsic property of AI tools to a discretionary add‑on.  

## Significance  
This research matters because it highlights the systemic risk that public‑service AI governance may collapse under the weight of GPAI adoption, potentially leading to harmful policing practices and eroding trust in other sectors such as healthcare or education. By exposing the flawed assumptions behind current mitigations, the paper urges a rethink of safety frameworks that can support responsible deployment without sacrificing effectiveness.
