## Summary  
The paper investigates cross‑cultural differences in student perceptions of generative AI (GenAI) use within university computing education, comparing students from Canadian and South Korean universities. It seeks to uncover how cultural contexts shape ethical judgments about AI‑generated code even when institutional policies are identical. The study contributes empirical evidence that cultural dimensions influence whether GenAI is seen as unethical or a policy violation. This work calls for AI‑use guidelines that are culturally responsive while preserving academic honesty.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Canadian students were consistently more likely than Korean students to perceive the use of GenAI as both unethical and against institutional policies, despite identical policies.  
- [Finding 2] The amount of AI‑generated code incorporated into assignments was the strongest predictor of ethical judgments across cultures.  
- [Finding 3] Hofstede’s cultural dimensions—particularly power distance, individualism, and uncertainty avoidance—significantly correlated with students’ ethical reasoning about GenAI.

## Methodology  
The authors administered a scenario‑based survey to students at Canadian and South Korean universities in Fall 2024. Scenarios varied the proportion of AI‑generated code required for coding assignments. Responses were coded for perceived ethicality and rule compliance, then subjected to Mann‑Whitney U tests and correlation coefficient analyses.

## Results  
Statistical analysis revealed significant differences across all scenarios (p < .01). The percentage of AI‑generated code strongly influenced judgments: higher percentages led to more negative evaluations in both cultures, with a larger effect observed among Canadian students. Correlations showed moderate to strong links between individualism and uncertainty avoidance scores and the likelihood of viewing GenAI as unethical.

## Significance  
Understanding these cross‑cultural perceptions is crucial for designing equitable AI policies that respect diverse notions of academic integrity. One‑size‑fits‑all rules may be ineffective; culturally informed guidelines are needed to support responsible GenAI use in global higher education.

## Related Concepts  
Generative AI, academic integrity, cultural dimensions (Hofstede), ethical reasoning, policy design, scenario‑based survey, statistical analysis (Mann‑Whitney U).
