## Summary  
The paper critiques the notion of *Epistemia*—the illusion that a language model’s fluent output constitutes genuine knowledge—by arguing that this condition stems from a deeper socio‑technical split called *epistemic schizologia*. It proposes that large language models should be understood as *techno‑semiotic machines* that automate a phase of written semiosis, not autonomous epistemic agents. The authors therefore shift focus from the model alone to the full human practice surrounding its outputs.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Epistemic schizologia is identified as the socio‑technical cleavage between signs produced linguistically and signs embedded in socially mediated circuits of interpretation, evidence, criticism, verification, and responsibility.  
- [Finding 2] Large language models are reframed as *techno‑semiotic machines* that generate plausible linguistic configurations from the sedimented archive of human writing, thereby automating a specific stage of semiosis.  
- [Finding 3] A design programme is proposed that foregrounds inspectable genealogy, contestability, distributed responsibility, epistemic agency, and evaluation of hybrid human‑AI practices.

## Methodology  
The authors draw on Carlo Sini’s philosophy of practices concerning writing, signs, and technics. They analyze the LLM as a semiotic device operating within a practice that includes prompting, interpretation, verification, contestation, use, and consequential impact. This theoretical approach avoids empirical experiments, focusing instead on conceptual mapping of how LLMs function within human epistemic ecosystems.

## Results  
The primary result is a theoretical reframing: *Epistemia* is not an isolated model flaw but a symptom of epistemic schizologia amplified by algorithmic authority and eikotic closure. The secondary outcome is the articulation of a concrete design agenda that demands traceability, contestable outcomes, shared responsibility, and explicit epistemic agency in human‑AI collaborations.

## Significance  
Understanding LLMs as techno‑semiotic machines rather than epistemic beings changes how we assess AI reliability and ethical deployment. It highlights the need for institutional frameworks that preserve the distinction between linguistic production and responsible understanding, thereby mitigating misplaced confidence in machine‑generated knowledge.
