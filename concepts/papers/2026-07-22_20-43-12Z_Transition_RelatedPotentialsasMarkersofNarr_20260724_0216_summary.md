## Summary  
The paper investigates whether brief cinematic cuts generate a measurable EEG signature that reflects narrative processing, and it proposes a method to extract this “transition‑related potential” (TRP) directly from continuous recordings. By comparing coherent films with scene‑scrambled versions that contain matched sensory input after each cut, the authors show that the TRP is shaped by the surrounding narrative context. A compact deep neural network can recover the cut‑related EEG signature from group‑averaged continuous data and generalize across subjects and movies. This semi‑automated approach offers a new tool for probing how viewers understand film narratives in real time.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Transition‑related potentials (TRPs) exhibit ERP‑like temporal structure, indicating that cuts trigger significant information processing.  
- [Finding 2] The magnitude and timing of TRPs are systematically altered by narrative context—coherent films produce stronger responses than scene‑scrambled versions with matched post‑cut sensory input.  
- [Finding 3] A deep neural network trained on group‑averaged continuous EEG can recover the cut‑related EEG signature, reproducing the main context‑dependent effects observed in manually annotated cuts.

## Methodology  
Participants watched short films while their scalp was continuously recorded with EEG. Sharp cinematic transitions (cuts) were identified and used as anchors to extract transient potentials. The authors compared two experimental conditions: (a) coherent films where narrative flow is preserved, and (b) scene‑scrambled versions that retain the same visual input but disrupt narrative coherence after each cut. A deep neural network was trained on the continuous EEG traces to detect TRPs without requiring trial‑by‑trial labeling.

## Results  
Group‑averaged continuous recordings were fed into a compact DNN, which produced a detector for the cut‑related EEG signature. This detector generalized across different films and participant groups, generating TRP estimates that matched the main context‑dependent effects measured in manually annotated cuts. The reconstructed potentials displayed ERP‑like latency and amplitude profiles, confirming that narrative processing leaves a detectable trace even when trials are not isolated.

## Significance  
The work demonstrates that narrative context can be inferred from EEG without stopping recordings, providing a semi‑automated framework for analyzing how viewers process film narratives in naturalistic conditions. By adapting the method to other continuous stimuli (e.g., speech, music), researchers can probe brain responses more closely aligned with everyday experiences.
