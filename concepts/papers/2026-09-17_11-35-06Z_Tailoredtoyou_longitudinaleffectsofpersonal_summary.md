## Summary
This research paper investigates the longitudinal effects of personalizing Large Language Models (LLMs) on human behavior and perception, specifically focusing on how sustained interaction impacts users over time. While personalization is often framed as a tool for better user experience, this study explores the psychological consequences of these interactions, including effects on self-perception and interpersonal relationships. The authors compare three different conditions—non-personalized baseline, memory-based personalization (history-driven), and survey-based personalization (pre-study data)—to determine how specific types of customization influence human-AI dynamics.

## Key Contributions
- **Distinction between Exposure and Personalization:** The study identifies that many changes in human-AI interaction over time are driven by repeated exposure to the AI rather than the specific personalization techniques employed.
- **Impact of Memory-based Personalization:** Participants interacting with models conditioned on prior conversational history showed increased self-disclosure and perceived the AI as less "creepy" compared to other groups.
- **Risks of Survey-based Personalization:** Conversely, participants in the survey-based condition reported higher levels of regret regarding the personal information they shared with the AI, highlighting a potential psychological friction point in specific types of data collection.

## Methodology
The researchers recruited 992 participants to engage in daily "advice-seeking" interactions with language models over a period of five days. The study employed a controlled experimental design comparing three distinct conditions:
1.  **Non-personalized baseline:** A standard interaction without personalized context.
2.  **Memory-based personalization:** The model was conditioned on the user's prior conversational history to adapt its responses dynamically.
- **Survey-based personalization:** The model was conditioned on specific information collected through a pre-study intake survey.

The researchers measured changes in attitudes toward AI, levels of self-disclosure, and feelings of regret or "creepiness" to assess the psychological impact of these interactions over time.

## Results
The findings reveal that while repeated interaction generally alters how humans engage with AI, the *method* of personalization significantly dictates the user's emotional response. In the memory-based condition, users became more comfortable and open, suggesting that a model that "remembers" them can foster a sense of rapport or familiarity. However, the survey-based approach led to higher regret, suggesting that explicit data collection—even if used for personalization—might trigger privacy concerns or feelings of vulnerability in the user. These results suggest that not all forms of personalization are equal; some may build trust while others may create psychological discomfort.

## Significance
This research is significant because it moves the conversation beyond technical accuracy and toward the human-centric implications of AI deployment. By highlighting how different types of personalization affect user psychology, the study provides a framework for "responsible design." It warns developers that simply making an AI more personalized might inadvertently lead to higher levels of regret or psychological discomfort depending on how that data is collected and utilized. The findings suggest that designers must balance personalization with user agency and emotional safety.
