## Summary  
This paper investigates how a TPACK‑guided framework can shape the integration of generative AI into a master‑level requirements engineering (RE) assignment focused on quality analysis. By embedding a multi‑agent AI tool within the task, the study empirically examines how students’ use of the system aligns with RE content, influences their understanding of user‑story quality criteria, and shapes perceptions of AI’s benefits and limitations. The mixed‑methods approach combines quantitative analysis of 72 student submissions (N = 100) with qualitative reflections on AI trust and usability.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Students employed the AI tool selectively—primarily as a support for analysis and evaluation rather than for automated generation, indicating a pedagogical alignment between AI affordances and RE learning objectives.  
- [Finding 2] Improvements in understanding were most pronounced for structurally concrete quality dimensions such as value articulation and testability, while negotiability showed mixed effects, revealing nuanced impacts of AI scaffolding on different aspects of requirements quality.  
- [Finding 3] Students reported conditional trust, active refinement of their ideas using AI feedback, and heightened awareness of quality criteria, alongside moderate usability challenges that highlight the need for balanced design.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a TPACK‑guided empirical study combining quantitative analysis of student submissions with qualitative interviews. A multi‑agent generative AI tool was integrated into a master RE assignment where students produced user stories and evaluated their quality using predefined criteria. The mixed‑methods design allowed the researchers to capture both objective patterns in the data (e.g., frequency of AI use) and subjective insights (e.g., trust levels). Data were collected from 100 students, with 72 submissions analyzed for textual evidence.

## Results  
Quantitative analysis revealed that AI usage was highest during the evaluation phase, suggesting strategic reliance on the system. Qualitative feedback indicated conditional trust: students felt confident using AI when it clarified concrete quality aspects but remained skeptical about its role in abstract or negotiable criteria. The study also noted increased awareness of value articulation and testability among participants, while usability challenges—such as latency and output ambiguity—moderated overall satisfaction.

## Significance  
The findings demonstrate that TPACK‑based scaffolding can effectively align AI capabilities with RE pedagogy, fostering responsible integration in software engineering education. By clarifying where AI adds value and where it may overstep, the study provides concrete design guidance for educators seeking to embed generative AI into complex subject areas without compromising learning outcomes.
