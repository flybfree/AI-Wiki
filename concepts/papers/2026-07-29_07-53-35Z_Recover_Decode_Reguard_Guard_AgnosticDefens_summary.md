## Summary  
The paper addresses the “decode gap” in guard‑agnostic defenses for vision‑language models, where harmful requests are re‑encoded as set theory, code, or images of text and slip past surface‑based safety classifiers. It proposes a recover‑and‑decode amplifier that translates these encoded payloads back into plain language before they reach the guard, thereby allowing any off‑the‑shelf classifier to block them. The authors evaluate this amplifier against an ensemble of eleven attacks using a best‑of‑suite metric, revealing a safety‑utility ceiling for non‑iterative recovery defenses across five guards and two VLMs. Their work also introduces a modular “reguard” layer that can partially mitigate residual risk but at the cost of high over‑refusal rates.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] An empirical safety‑utility ceiling is observed for non‑iterative recovery defenses, which perform poorly across five guards and two target VLMs.  
- [Finding 2] The amplifier only partially closes the attack gap; even with a guard plus the amplifier, attack success remains around 63–65 % in many cases.  
- [Finding 3] A modular reguard layer can lower over‑refusal to 81–92 % for well‑calibrated guards but cannot simultaneously achieve low attack success and low over‑refusal.

## Methodology  
The authors construct a guard‑agnostic amplifier that first extracts the visual content of an input image, then decodes any encoded textual payload (e.g., set theory, code) into its original plain‑language form. This decoded request is fed to the safety classifier before it reaches the model. To assess robustness, they employ an ensemble of eleven attacks generated by AutoAttack’s best‑of‑suite approach, treating a behavior as broken if any single attack succeeds. The study maps performance across five different guard configurations and two VLM families, focusing on representation‑shifting attacks that preserve legible payloads rather than pixel‑level or embedding‑space manipulations.

## Results  
Guard alone blocks roughly 70 % of plain‑language requests but fails on many encoded variants. Adding the amplifier improves performance only in four out of ten guard‑target pairs, still leaving attack success between 63–65 %. The ensemble of eleven attacks breaks 89–91 % of behaviors, indicating a high overall vulnerability. For well‑calibrated guards, over‑refusal rises to 81–92 % when the reguard layer is applied, while the best guard plus amplifier still permits 63–65 % attack success. No configuration yields both low attack success and low over‑refusal; the pipeline cannot simultaneously achieve high safety and low false‑positive rates.

## Significance  
These findings expose a fundamental limitation: guard‑agnostic recovery defenses, while conceptually appealing, do not universally close the decode gap and can introduce undesirable over‑refusal. The empirical ceiling highlights the need for more nuanced, multi‑layered strategies that balance safety and utility. By introducing an ensemble evaluation metric and a modular reguard layer, the authors provide a clearer picture of where recovery‑based VLM defenses work and where they fail, guiding future research toward more robust, deployable safety mechanisms.
