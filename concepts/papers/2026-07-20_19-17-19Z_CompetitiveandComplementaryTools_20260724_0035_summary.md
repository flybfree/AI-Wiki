## Summary  
The paper proposes a dynamical‑systems model that treats an agent, its tool, and the task it performs as a single evolving system. It demonstrates that competence (what the user retains) and reliance (what is outsourced) co‑evolve in a bistable fashion: above a critical level of tool availability the competent state collapses into a low‑dependence floor where the user completely outsources work. The authors argue that historical practice, not just current tool access, determines which lasting state an individual occupies.  

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Competence and reliance co‑evolve in a bistable system; above a critical tool availability the competent state is destroyed and competence collapses to a low dependent floor.  
- [Finding 2] The collapse threshold depends jointly on the user’s prior competence and the tool’s transparency, i.e., the fraction of its working that can be reconstructed by the user; history of practice fixes the lasting state.  
- [Finding 3] When an uncertain goal is faced, a large‑scale tool can cause agency to transfer irreversibly, turning the human‑agent into an agentic‑instrument.  

## Methodology  
The authors model the interaction as a dynamical system where competence and reliance are variables that evolve together. They analyze the system mathematically to locate critical thresholds and simulate how varying levels of tool availability and transparency affect outcomes. The theoretical framework is then tested empirically across multiple domains—GPS and map use, arithmetic expertise, and large language models—to verify predictions.  

## Results  
The model predicts a bistable outcome: users with high competence can maintain independent performance up to a critical tool‑availability threshold; beyond that they lose competence and become fully dependent. Lowering availability does not reverse the collapse until a far lower threshold is reached, confirming that historical practice locks in the state. Experiments across GPS/map tasks, arithmetic calculations, and language‑model interactions confirm these theoretical predictions, showing that agency can transfer to the tool when its model exceeds internalizable complexity.  

## Significance  
These findings reframe how tools should be designed: they must respect user competence and provide sufficient transparency to avoid irreversible outsourcing of agency. They also guide the deployment of artificial intelligence by highlighting risks of agentic‑instrument formation. Moreover, the paper outlines what a “tool‑resistant education” might require—teaching users to preserve competence despite tool availability.
