## Summary  
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can perform mentalization—the cognitive ability to infer others’ beliefs and intentions—by applying computational modeling techniques to economic games that require strategic reasoning. The study compares the performance of LLMs from different providers and sizes against human participants, using a prompting strategy designed to elicit deeper reasoning. Results reveal distinct patterns in how LLMs engage in mentalizing, with some models adapting their cognitive depth dynamically based on opponent sophistication. These findings highlight both the potential and limitations of current AI systems in modeling human-like social cognition.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The study demonstrates that certain large language models exhibit clear computational signatures of mentalization across two economic games, suggesting they can infer others’ intentions and beliefs to guide their actions.  
- [Finding 2] Strategic prompting significantly improves LLM performance by inducing more sophisticated reasoning, indicating that external cues can enhance cognitive depth in AI systems.  
- [Finding 3] GPT-5 agents outperformed human participants by flexibly adjusting the recursive depth of their reasoning to match increasingly complex opponents, showcasing superior adaptive mentalization.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a comparative experimental design using two economic games with embedded cognitive modeling components that require players to infer others’ beliefs and intentions. Individual LLM agents from four model families—DeepSeek, GPT-4.1, GPT-5, and Gemini 2.0 Flash—were tested across multiple rounds against human opponents of varying strategic sophistication. The same games were also played by human participants (N = 251) to serve as a benchmark. A novel prompting strategy was introduced to elicit deeper reasoning in LLMs, allowing researchers to measure the extent and quality of mentalization through computational modeling.

## Results  
Across both tasks, LLMs showed measurable behavioral and computational indicators of mentalization that varied by model provider and size. Strategic prompting led to improved performance, particularly in GPT-5, which demonstrated dynamic adjustment of reasoning depth. However, the benefit was not uniform—some models plateaued or underperformed compared to humans. Notably, GPT-5 agents achieved higher accuracy and more complex decision-making than human participants, suggesting that certain LLMs can surpass human mentalization capabilities when prompted appropriately.

## Significance  
This research provides a formal framework for assessing the cognitive depth of AI systems using computational modeling, enabling objective comparisons between human and machine performance. It challenges the assumption that current LLMs merely simulate social reasoning superficially; instead, it reveals that some models possess genuine adaptive mentalizing abilities under specific conditions. The findings have implications for AI safety, ethical design, and the development of more robust social intelligence in machines.

## Related Concepts  
- Mentalization: The capacity to infer others’ beliefs and intentions.  
- Theory-of-mind tasks: Cognitive exercises requiring understanding of another’s mental state.  
- Computational modeling: A method for simulating cognitive processes using mathematical representations.  
- Large language models (LLMs): AI systems trained on vast text data capable of generating human-like responses.  
- Strategic prompting: A technique to guide AI reasoning by providing higher-level instructions or context.
