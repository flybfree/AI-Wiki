## Summary
This paper provides a comprehensive synthesis of recent literature regarding the phenomenon of anthropomorphism in Large Language Models (LLMs) and broader Artificial Intelligence systems. The authors aim to clarify why humans tend to attribute human-like characteristics to machines, examining both the psychological drivers and the critical role of linguistic framing in shaping these perceptions. By analyzing existing theoretical frameworks, the study highlights how language influences our understanding of AI capabilities and societal roles. Ultimately, the work seeks to establish a more grounded scientific approach to describing AI that minimizes misleading human-centric comparisons.

## Key Contributions
- The authors introduce a novel conceptual taxonomy that categorizes twenty-one specific concerns associated with AI anthropomorphism into five distinct analytical domains: epistemic, affective, human agency, normative, and societal/institutional risks.
- They demonstrate the profound impact of linguistic framing on anthropomorphic tendencies, arguing that the language used to describe AI systems directly influences public perception, trust levels, and the ethical boundaries assigned to these technologies.
- The paper proposes a multi-faceted mitigation strategy that integrates interventions in system design, communication protocols, educational curricula, and governance frameworks to reduce the negative consequences of anthropomorphizing non-human entities.

## Methodology
The authors employed a systematic literature review approach, synthesizing findings from recent interdisciplinary studies spanning computer science, psychology, linguistics, ethics, and sociology. Rather than conducting new empirical experiments, they critically analyzed existing theoretical models and case studies to identify patterns in how anthropomorphism manifests across different AI applications. This qualitative synthesis allowed them to map the complex relationships between user interactions, linguistic cues, and resulting behavioral or societal outcomes.

## Results
The primary result is the development of a structured taxonomy that organizes the scattered literature on AI risks into coherent categories. The analysis reveals that epistemic risks involve misunderstandings of AI limitations, while affective risks pertain to inappropriate emotional bonding. Furthermore, the study identifies that human agency risks arise when users abdicate decision-making power to AI systems due to perceived human-like autonomy. Normative and societal risks include the reinforcement of biases and the erosion of social norms regarding responsibility and accountability.

## Significance
This research is significant because it moves beyond technical discussions of AI safety to address the cognitive and sociolinguistic dimensions of human-AI interaction. By providing a clear framework for identifying and mitigating anthropomorphic risks, it offers valuable guidance for policymakers, developers, and educators. The paper underscores the urgent need for precise terminology in AI discourse to ensure that societal integration of these technologies is based on accurate understandings rather than illusory human-like traits.
