## Summary  
This paper argues that the common AI narrative of “general intelligence,” “hallucination” as cognitive failure, and “sentience” are category mistakes: they project human communicative practices onto language models. It introduces the concept of asymmetric communication, where model outputs enter further exchanges without bearing normative commitments or entitlements. The framework shows that correctness is enforced solely by the receiver, accountability rests with humans, and any output’s practical standing depends entirely on human uptake.

## Key Contributions  
- Correctness in LLM‑human interaction is exclusively a function of the receiver’s judgment.  
- Accountability for model outputs remains the responsibility of human participants alone.  
- The practical significance of an LLM’s response hinges solely on how humans take up and act upon it.

## Methodology  
The authors employ a philosophical analysis, drawing on Wittgensteinian meaning‑enactment, Luhmannian communication theory, Esposito’s notion of algorithmic contingency, and Brandom’s normative scorekeeping to map the structure of human‑LLM exchanges as a language game.

## Results  
Through this theoretical mapping, the paper demonstrates that the three defining conditions of asymmetry hold regardless of model capability; they are structural necessities rather than manifestations of machine agency. Consequently, concepts such as alignment are reclassified as institutional constraint engineering, not goal synchronization between agents.

## Significance  
By locating responsibility and normative evaluation on human institutions, the framework offers a new lens for AI governance: alignment is engineered through constraints imposed by societies, not achieved via autonomous model alignment, preserving human accountability in the loop.

## Related Concepts
