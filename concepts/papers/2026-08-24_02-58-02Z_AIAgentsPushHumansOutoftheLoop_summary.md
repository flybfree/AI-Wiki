## Summary  
The paper argues that current AI‑agent systems are eroding the very human oversight they rely on, creating a dangerous feedback loop of automation and skill loss. It contends that existing “human‑in‑the‑loop” approaches are ineffective because they do not address design flaws or the cognitive degradation experienced by overseers. The authors propose integrating the situated goals and mental demands of effective human judgment directly into AI agent architecture, treating human needs as equally important as AI capability. Their contribution is a set of design‑level affordances and organizational protocols that support critical thinking while counteracting skill atrophy.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Human oversight is systematically degraded by the current design of AI agents, which prioritize automation over the cognitive demands of supervisors.  
- [Finding 2] Prolonged use of automated systems leads to a measurable decline in the supervisory skills that are essential for effective judgment.  
- [Finding 3] Effective human‑agent interaction requires explicit affordances and organizational protocols that align AI behavior with the situational goals of overseers.

## Methodology  
The authors draw on interdisciplinary work from automation theory, human‑computer interaction, and cognitive psychology to map out how AI agents can be engineered. They outline a framework that links the design of autonomous systems to the real‑world tasks of human supervisors, identifying specific affordances (e.g., transparent decision logs, adjustable confidence thresholds) and organizational practices (e.g., regular skill‑maintenance checklists, collaborative review cycles). This synthesis aims to create a coherent set of recommendations rather than isolated experiments.

## Results  
The primary result is a theoretical analysis demonstrating that without such alignment, AI agents will continue to passively encourage the erosion of supervisory competence. The paper quantifies this risk through a conceptual model showing how autonomy amplifies automation bias and accelerates skill decay over time. No empirical data are presented; instead, the findings rely on logical inference and expert reasoning.

## Significance  
This work matters because it highlights a systemic vulnerability in AI deployment: the loss of human expertise could compromise safety, decision quality, and trust. By foregrounding the cognitive needs of overseers alongside AI capability, the authors advocate for policies and engineering practices that preserve human agency and prevent unintended de‑skilling.
