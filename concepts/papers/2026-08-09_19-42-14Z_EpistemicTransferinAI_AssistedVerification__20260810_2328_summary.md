## Summary  
This paper investigates how AI‑assisted verification influences a user’s later, unaided ability to judge new claims—a phenomenon the authors call “epistemic transfer.”  The work distinguishes epistemic transfer from related phenomena such as correction effects or trust and proposes two quantitative measures: the Epistemic Transfer Effect (ETE) and the Tool‑Removal Cost (TRC).  By combining these metrics, the authors create a diagnostic framework that separates cases of genuine skill building, tool advantage, de‑skilling, or verification on loan.  The paper therefore moves beyond evaluating AI tools in real time to assessing what remains of human judgment after the tool is removed.

## Key Contributions  
- **Finding 1:** A formal distinction between epistemic transfer and other outcomes like correction effects, trust, reliance, and team performance.  
- **Finding 2:** Introduction of two simple metrics—ETE (delayed unassisted performance) and TRC (immediate drop when the tool is taken away)—to quantify epistemic transfer.  
- **Finding 3:** A comprehensive evaluation protocol that operationalizes these concepts in online or field experiments, enabling systematic measurement across conditions.

## Methodology  
The authors design an experiment with four AI conditions—answer‑first and evidence‑first paired with active practice versus no practice—and a control group of participants who never used the tool.  After exposure, participants are tested on held‑out claims without any AI assistance.  The study records both immediate performance (TRC) and delayed performance (ETE), collects behavioral data, and conducts analyses at participant and item levels to isolate epistemic transfer.

## Results  
Results show that ETE is positive only when active practice was combined with the evidence‑first AI condition, indicating genuine skill enhancement.  In contrast, TRC is consistently negative across all conditions, confirming an immediate performance drop when the tool is removed.  The diagnostic space revealed by these metrics separates cases of capability building (positive ETE) from de‑skilling or verification on loan (negative ETE with high TRC).

## Significance  
Understanding epistemic transfer matters because AI tools are often deployed in contexts where independent judgment is required, yet current evaluations ignore what remains after tool use.  By measuring both immediate loss and later gain, the framework helps researchers and designers assess whether an AI truly builds human expertise or merely provides a temporary boost.
