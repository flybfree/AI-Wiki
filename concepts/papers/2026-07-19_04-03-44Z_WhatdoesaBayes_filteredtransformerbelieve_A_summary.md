## Summary  
The paper investigates what a Bayes‑filtered transformer (BFT) actually believes about the latent task that generated its training data, moving beyond simple next‑token predictions to recover an implicit prior and posterior in latent space. By employing predictive Monte Carlo (PMC), the authors provide a general interpretability tool that approximates these distributions using only autoregressive token generation. The study demonstrates that BFTs retain interpretable beliefs across several stylized task families, revealing phenomena previously observed at the output level but now visible directly in the model’s internal latent representation. This work bridges theory and practice by offering a principled method to interrogate BFT behavior without relying on fragile reference‑posterior comparisons.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] Predictive Monte Carlo (PMC) can approximate the implicit prior and posterior over the latent task of a Bayes‑filtered transformer using only next‑token generation.  
- [Finding 2] The learned BFT’s predictions correspond to an approximation of the Bayesian posterior predictive distribution, but the exact prior and posterior are not uniquely determined by this output alone.  
- [Finding 3] PMC recovers interpretable latent task distributions across 0‑Markov and 1‑Markov exchangeable tasks, confirming that BFTs encode meaningful beliefs rather than merely memorizing surface patterns.

## Methodology  
The authors define a Bayes‑filtered transformer as a model trained on sequences generated by first sampling a latent task from a prior and then drawing observations conditionally on that task. Training proceeds via autoregressive log loss, yielding next‑token predictions that in the idealized limit equal the posterior predictive distribution (PPD). To answer what prior and posterior the BFT internalizes, they introduce PMC: repeatedly generate tokens under the model’s own next‑token distribution while maintaining a running estimate of the latent task’s probability mass function. This Monte Carlo trace approximates both the prior and posterior in latent space without requiring access to reference algorithms or explicit posterior calculations.

## Results  
Across three stylized task families—0‑Markov, 1‑Markov, and exchangeable sequences—the PMC estimates recover the true latent task distributions with moderate accuracy. The recovered posteriors align qualitatively with the original generative priors, confirming that BFTs encode interpretable beliefs. Moreover, when compared to reference posterior predictions from alternative algorithms, the PMC‑derived posteriors often differ, highlighting that multiple posterior means can arise from distinct latent task models. These findings validate that BFT behavior is not merely a surface approximation but reflects underlying probabilistic structures.

## Significance  
Understanding what a BFT believes about its latent task improves model interpretability and aids debugging in generative AI systems. By providing a direct, data‑driven estimate of the prior and posterior without external references, PMC offers a scalable tool for researchers and practitioners to assess model confidence, detect overfitting, and design better training objectives.
