## Summary  
The paper tackles the problem of evaluating factual completeness in long‑form open‑ended generation, a dimension that current methods largely ignore. Existing pipelines focus on precision—detecting incorrect claims—but they do not measure whether an answer contains all relevant information. The authors introduce a two‑level meta‑rubric framework that first encodes the organization and importance of required content, then converts this into a flat set of binary, machine‑gradable rubrics for reliable LLM scoring. They instantiate this framework as **Gamut**, a benchmark that tests factual completeness across modalities.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] A two‑level meta‑rubric representation separates high‑level content organization from low‑level binary checks, enabling systematic evaluation of open‑ended generation.  
- [Finding 2] Gamut is a modality‑agnostic benchmark comprising 1,813 questions grounded in wearable imagery across ten diverse domains, each paired with evidence‑backed rubrics verified by expert annotators.  
- [Finding 3] The benchmark demonstrates genuine difficulty for frontier models (best score 58.7 % from Gemini 3.1 Pro), is highly discriminative, and yields stable scores regardless of the human judge’s choice.  

## Methodology  
The authors first construct a structured meta‑rubric that captures how facts are organized and their relative importance in a complete answer. This high‑level rubric is mechanically decomposed into a collection of independent binary criteria—each representing “is fact X present?” or “are facts Y and Z related?”—which can be scored automatically by an LLM judge. The framework is deliberately modality‑agnostic, so the same meta‑rubric works for both image‑based and text‑only tasks. The dataset was generated by expert annotators to ensure that each question’s rubric reflects the full set of facts a correct response should contain.  

## Results  
Evaluating 14 frontier and open‑weight models on Gamut, the highest achieved score is 58.7 % (Gemini 3.1 Pro). The results show strong model differentiation: lower scores correspond to missing or inaccurate information. Crucially, the benchmark’s performance is robust to variations in human judge interpretation, indicating that the meta‑rubric provides a reliable, machine‑evaluable metric for factual completeness.  

## Significance  
By addressing the “missing half” of factuality—coverage rather than just correctness—the paper offers a comprehensive evaluation tool that encourages models to produce truly complete answers. The two‑level meta‑rubric and Gamut benchmark provide a common language across modalities, facilitating fair comparison and guiding future research toward more holistic factual generation.
