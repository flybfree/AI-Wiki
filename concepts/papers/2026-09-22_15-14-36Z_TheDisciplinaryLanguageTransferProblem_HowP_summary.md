## Summary
This paper addresses a critical gap in AI governance by identifying how psychological vocabulary—such as "learning," "memory," and "trust"—transfers from psychology to AI agent deployment, creating epistemological misalignments that undermine accountability frameworks. The authors argue that this is not merely a matter of terminology but a deeper issue involving the transfer of disciplinary assumptions into governance discourse, leading to systemic failures in oversight and responsibility attribution. Their contribution lies in diagnosing the "disciplinary language transfer problem" and proposing a corrective reform through a six-question audit and translation taxonomy. The paper contends that current AI systems lack developmental continuity with psychological constructs, rendering standard governance models ineffective.

## Key Contributions
- [Finding 1] Psychological vocabulary functions as an "invisible grammar" of its originating discipline (psychology) that imposes metaphysical expectations onto AI agents, which do not possess the developmental or cognitive foundations these terms assume.
- [Finding 2] The disciplinary language transfer problem operates at three levels: epistemological assumptions, theoretical constructs, and surface vocabulary, each requiring distinct remediation strategies to correct governance failures.
- [Finding 3] A Disciplinary Audit—a six-question governance document scan—can identify misaligned terminology and conceptual drift, enabling the replacement of psychological terms with agent-appropriate constructs using a taxonomy of thirty-seven term mappings.

## Methodology
The authors approached the problem through interdisciplinary analysis, drawing on Wittgenstein’s language games to show how meaning is context-dependent, Kuhn’s paradigm shifts to illustrate how disciplinary frameworks resist change, Haraway’s situated knowledge to emphasize the limitations of universal models, and Star and Griesemer’s boundary object theory to explain how shared representations fail across domains. They traced six foundational epistemological assumptions embedded in Western psychological governance discourse—such as the belief that AI can "learn" or possess "values"—to specific philosophical traditions (e.g., behaviorism, cognitive science) and demonstrated why these fail when applied to systems without developmental continuity.

## Results
The results are theoretical rather than experimental; however, they include a comprehensive analysis of term mappings between psychological constructs and operational AI agent functions. The authors identify that terms like "memory" imply persistent internal states in AI, which is not technically feasible, while "trust" assumes human-like relational dynamics absent in current architectures. Their Disciplinary Audit provides a practical tool to audit governance documents for such misalignments, with the translation taxonomy enabling systematic reform.

## Significance
This matters because current AI governance frameworks risk creating illusory accountability by applying psychological metaphors to systems that do not embody them. Without linguistic and conceptual correction, organizations may blame AI agents for behaviors rooted in flawed assumptions rather than system design flaws. The paper’s significance lies in its call for a paradigm shift: treating AI as a technical construct, not a psychological entity, is essential for ethical deployment.
