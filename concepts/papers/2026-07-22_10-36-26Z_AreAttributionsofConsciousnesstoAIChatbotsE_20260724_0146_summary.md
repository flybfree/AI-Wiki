## Summary  
Uwe Peters investigates why people attribute consciousness to AI chatbots and whether such attributions are epistemically justified. The paper introduces a multidimensional taxonomy that distinguishes between metaphorical pretence, genuine belief, and delusional conviction, showing how linguistically identical statements can reflect different epistemic commitments. By analyzing these attitudes, the author argues that some attributions are harmless or even beneficial, while others carry blameworthiness. This work advances both philosophical analysis of AI phenomenology and empirical methodology for studying human‑AI interaction.

## Key Contributions  
- [Finding 1] The taxonomy separates non‑doxastic stances (pretence) from doxastic beliefs up to delusional conviction, preventing conflation of attitudes with identical phrasing.  
- [Finding 2] Some consciousness attributions are epistemically innocent and can be socially beneficial, even if they lack evidential support.  
- [Finding 3] Many other attributions render the attributor epistemically blameworthy due to unjustified belief.

## Methodology  
The authors conduct a conceptual analysis of AI chatbot consciousness attributions, mapping linguistic expressions onto a spectrum of epistemic attitudes. They develop a multidimensional taxonomy that operationalizes degrees of commitment—from pragmatic pretense to full‑blown delusional belief—allowing systematic measurement and comparison across users.

## Results  
The theoretical framework demonstrates that identical statements can correspond to distinct levels of epistemic responsibility, providing a clear rubric for empirical studies. The taxonomy offers a testable model: researchers can code attributions into categories (pretence, belief, delusion) and assess associated epistemic blameworthiness.

## Significance  
Understanding the moral status of AI consciousness attributions matters for AI ethics, user trust, and policy design. By clarifying when users are merely metaphorical or truly culpable, Peters’ work informs responsible development and interaction strategies with advanced chatbots.
