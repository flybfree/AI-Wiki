## Summary  
The article revisits a 1979 paper that argued formal program verification is unlikely to succeed because it treats proofs as the final product rather than a social process of communication. It outlines three classic objections—proofs are merely a means, specifications are informal and hard to pin down, and verification is impractical—and then asks whether recent AI‑driven software development has invalidated these concerns. The piece suggests that while AI has made verification tools faster and more visible, the underlying structural problems remain largely unaddressed.

## Key Takeaways  
- Formal verification is not a replacement for mathematics; it must be seen as a communication tool whose value lies in community acceptance, not in the proof itself.  
- Real‑world requirements are inherently informal, making precise specifications and proofs difficult to achieve without ongoing dialogue between engineers and stakeholders.  
- AI’s rapid adoption of formal methods may amplify hype but does not resolve the fundamental challenges identified by the 1979 paper.

## Context  
The surge in searches for “formal verification” and “Lean” over the past two years reflects a broader trend: AI coding assistants are producing code at unprecedented speed, prompting engineers to seek additional assurance mechanisms. Projects like Signal Shot aim to verify entire systems end‑to‑end, illustrating how industry is integrating formal methods into mainstream development pipelines.

## Implications  
For the field and industry, the article warns against treating verification as a silver bullet that will automatically guarantee correctness. It underscores that realistic progress depends on improving specification clarity, fostering collaborative proof production, and managing expectations—rather than chasing hype driven by AI‑generated code.
