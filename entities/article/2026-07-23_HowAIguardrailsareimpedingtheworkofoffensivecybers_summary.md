## Summary  
The article explains how AI safety guardrails, imposed by companies like Anthropic and OpenAI, are unintentionally blocking legitimate offensive cybersecurity research that seeks to discover and exploit software vulnerabilities before malicious actors can use them. Government export controls on Anthropic’s Mythos model further restrict access, while the dual‑use nature of these tools—both defensive “fix this code” prompts and offensive probing techniques—creates a conflict between safety policies and security progress.

## Key Takeaways  
- Guardrails designed to prevent malicious use also impede researchers’ ability to test and validate vulnerabilities.  
- Export restrictions on advanced AI models limit the availability of powerful tools for both defense and offense.  
- The same AI capabilities serve dual purposes, making it impossible to separate their offensive and defensive functions without sacrificing one.

## Context  
The broader AI landscape is moving toward tighter safety controls as large language models become more capable. Companies market “vetted” access programs that promise fewer restrictions for cybersecurity professionals, reflecting an industry effort to balance innovation with risk mitigation. However, this gatekeeping is not unique; it reflects a growing tension between corporate liability concerns and the need for open collaboration in security research.

## Implications  
If offensive researchers cannot freely probe systems using AI tools, critical vulnerabilities may remain undiscovered longer than intended, allowing attackers to exploit them first. This slows overall system hardening, undermines trust in AI‑assisted security solutions, and could exacerbate an arms race where defensive measures become counterproductive. The field must therefore find mechanisms that preserve safety while enabling responsible use of AI for security testing.
