## Summary
This article discusses a three-month randomized controlled trial involving patent attorneys that investigates the long-term impact of AI assistance on professional skill development. While the study confirms that AI tools consistently improve immediate work quality and drafting scores, it reveals a critical divergence in how these tools affect learning based on seniority. Senior lawyers demonstrated enhanced independent judgment after using AI, whereas junior lawyers showed no average skill gain, with their performance outcomes splitting into higher and lower scores rather than uniformly improving.

## Key Takeaways
- **AI Enhances Immediate Output but Not Uniformly for Learning:** The study found that AI assistance raised drafting scores by 0.34 standard deviations for all participants, confirming its effectiveness as a productivity booster. However, this immediate gain does not translate into equal long-term skill acquisition for all experience levels.
- **Seniority Determines the Impact on Judgment:** Senior lawyers who used AI for 90 days demonstrated stronger independent judgment compared to those who did not, suggesting that experienced professionals can leverage AI to refine and deepen their existing expertise. In contrast, junior lawyers did not show an average improvement in independent skills, indicating that AI may not effectively substitute for the foundational learning processes required for novices.
- **The "Exoskeleton" Effect and Skill Polarization:** For junior workers, AI usage resulted in a polarization of outcomes, where some performed better while others performed worse, without a net average gain. This highlights the risk that AI can act as a temporary crutch that fails to persist as a skill enhancer once the tool is removed, potentially hindering the development of core competencies.

## Context
This research addresses a growing tension in the AI industry regarding the dual role of generative AI as both a productivity tool and a potential disruptor of human expertise development. Previous studies have shown mixed results: while some fields like radiology and legal education suggest AI can enhance training, others in software engineering and consulting show that AI boosts fade once the tool is removed. This study is significant because it combines sustained AI usage in real-world professional settings with blinded, expert-graded evaluations of unassisted performance, providing a more rigorous framework for understanding how AI interacts with human learning curves over time.

## Implications
The findings have profound implications for how organizations integrate AI into professional workflows, particularly in expertise-intensive fields like law, medicine, and engineering. It suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to AI deployment is flawed; while AI can safely augment senior professionals, its use for junior staff requires careful management to prevent skill erosion or dependency. Companies must design training programs that ensure AI tools are embedded in workflows that promote active learning rather than passive reliance. Furthermore, this research underscores the need for AI developers to create tools that not only boost output but also support the pedagogical processes necessary for building independent judgment, ensuring that AI serves as a scaffold for growth rather than a replacement for foundational skill acquisition.
