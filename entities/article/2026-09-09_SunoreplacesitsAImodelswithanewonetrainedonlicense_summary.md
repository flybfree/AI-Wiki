## Summary  
Suno has announced the launch of its new v6 model family, which it claims is trained exclusively on licensed music data from major labels such as Warner Music Group and BMG, a move intended to address mounting copyright lawsuits. The company settled with several record companies last year and released three versions—base, wild, and mini—while phasing out older models, adding features like prompt‑driven editing and multimodal inputs, and introducing watermarking to manage ecosystem challenges.

## Key Takeaways  
- Suno v6 is built on licensed data only, directly responding to ongoing copyright disputes and legal settlements.  
- The new model suite offers advanced capabilities: users can edit specific song sections via prompts, use text, images or video as references, and remix tracks with separate instrument stems.  
- Suno plans additional revenue streams through a licensing program for artists who opt‑in, alongside watermarking to curb fraudulent streaming and distribution abuse.

## Context  
The AI music generation sector has become a focal point of copyright litigation, with multiple record labels suing startups like Suno for using unlicensed training data. As generative models proliferate, the industry grapples with balancing innovation against intellectual‑property rights, prompting companies to seek legal safeguards and partnerships with rights holders.

## Implications  
This shift could set a precedent for regulated AI training in creative industries, encouraging broader adoption of licensed datasets while reducing litigation risk. However, it also raises questions about fair compensation for artists, the enforceability of watermarks, and whether such models truly respect copyright boundaries—issues that will shape future policy and market dynamics.
