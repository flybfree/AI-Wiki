## Summary  

NASA has announced that the 48‑year‑old Voyager 2 spacecraft will remain in operation through at least early 2026, extending its historic mission by another year. The decision follows a routine health check and a careful assessment of the probe’s dwindling power budget, which is limited to about 150 watts from its decaying solar panels. Engineers confirmed that Voyager 2 continues to receive occasional commands from Earth and can still perform small attitude adjustments using its residual thrusters. While the spacecraft will not be able to launch any new instruments or make major scientific breakthroughs, it will continue to transmit low‑rate telemetry and a handful of high‑resolution images captured during its fly‑bys of Uranus and Neptune in 1986–1989. The move underscores NASA’s commitment to preserving the most valuable legacy mission in its history, even as newer deep‑space probes are being built for future exploration.

## Key Takeaways  

- **Extended Mission Window:** Voyager 2 will stay alive until early 2026, adding roughly a year of data collection beyond its original design life.  
- **Power Constraints Remain:** The spacecraft’s solar array output has dropped to ~150 W, limiting the frequency and size of any new commands or experiments.  
- **Minimal Operational Activity:** Only routine attitude control and occasional low‑rate telemetry are possible; no major science payloads can be activated.  
- **Cost‑Effective Decision:** Launching a fresh interplanetary probe would cost billions, whereas extending Voyager 2’s life leverages existing hardware and infrastructure.  
- **Scientific Value Preserved:** Even at low data rates, the mission will continue to return historic images of Uranus and Neptune, providing valuable context for future missions.
