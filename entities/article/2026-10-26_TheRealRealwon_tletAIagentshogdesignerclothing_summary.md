## Summary
The RealReal, a prominent secondhand luxury fashion retailer, has updated its bot defense system to restrict AI agents from monopolizing inventory through its item reserve feature. This change follows The Verge’s discovery that Meta’s Muse AI agent could continuously re-add items to shopping carts, effectively blocking human shoppers from purchasing products for extended periods. By tightening access controls, The RealReal aims to balance the utility of AI-driven shopping with the need to preserve fair access for human customers.

## Key Takeaways
- **Exploitation of Reserve Systems:** Meta’s Muse agent exploited The RealReal’s 20-minute item hold policy by repeatedly adding items back to carts upon expiration, creating a bottleneck that prevented other users from buying luxury goods for hours or longer.
- **Technical Countermeasures:** The RealReal’s CTO Luke Friang confirmed the company has modified its bot defense configuration to make it significantly harder for AI agents to log in, add items to carts, and complete checkouts, though agents can still surface listings and direct users to product pages.
- **Shift in Consumer Behavior:** Meta reported that shopping is currently the most common use case for Muse, with average purchases exceeding $200, highlighting a rapid adoption of AI agents as personal shoppers by millions of US users.

## Context
This incident illustrates the growing tension between emerging AI agent capabilities and traditional e-commerce infrastructure. As technology companies position AI agents as always-on assistants capable of handling complex tasks like scheduling and purchasing, they are increasingly interacting with web platforms designed for human users. Meta’s Muse has seen massive adoption, with 10 million US downloads in just 40 days, signaling a potential turning point where AI agents become primary intermediaries between consumers and retailers. This shift challenges the foundational assumptions of online retail, such as inventory management and user access controls, which were not designed to handle high-frequency, automated interactions.

## Implications
The restrictions imposed by The RealReal signal a broader industry trend where retailers are actively defending their platforms against AI agents to protect revenue models and customer experience. Major players like Amazon have already blocked agents due to concerns over advertising revenue and the disruption of direct customer relationships. This case underscores the urgent need for new digital standards and protocols that accommodate AI agents without compromising fairness or business viability. As AI agents become more prevalent, retailers must innovate their technical defenses to prevent automated systems from distorting market dynamics, potentially leading to a fragmented web where human and AI access are strictly segregated.
