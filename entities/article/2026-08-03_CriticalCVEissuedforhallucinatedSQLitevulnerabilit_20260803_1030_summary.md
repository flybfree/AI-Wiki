## Summary
JFrog Security researchers have exposed a wave of fabricated Critical CVEs targeting SQLite, revealing them to be "LLM slop" generated by artificial intelligence rather than genuine security flaws. Initial automated systems from the NVD and CISA incorrectly assigned critical severity scores to these non-existent vulnerabilities before manual verification dismantled their validity. The investigation highlights significant failures in current vulnerability intake processes that rely too heavily on unverified, AI-generated data sources.

## Key Takeaways
- **Fabricated Evidence:** The advisories cited code functions and line numbers that do not exist in the specified SQLite versions, and proof-of-concept payloads failed to trigger any crashes or memory errors during rigorous testing.
- **AI Generation Indicators:** Content analysis tools like Gptzero flagged the advisories as AI-generated, and combining them triggered warnings typical of synthetic text, confirming they were likely mass-produced by large language models.
- **Metadata Inconsistencies:** The CVE entries contained contradictory metadata, such as incorrect Common Platform Enumeration (CPE) pins and references to unrelated logic, leading Red Hat to downgrade the severity of one key CVE from Critical to High.

## Context
This incident underscores a growing crisis in cybersecurity known as "LLM slop," where generative AI models produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect technical content at scale. As organizations increasingly automate vulnerability tracking and scoring, there is a dangerous reliance on metadata that lacks human oversight. The rapid propagation of these fake CVEs through major databases like NVD illustrates how quickly misinformation can infiltrate critical infrastructure security ecosystems when verification mechanisms are insufficient.

## Implications
The proliferation of AI-generated vulnerabilities poses a severe risk to software supply chain integrity and developer trust. Security teams may waste valuable resources patching non-existent issues or, conversely, overlook real threats due to noise saturation. This event necessitates stricter validation protocols for CVE assignments, emphasizing the need for human-in-the-loop verification before critical severity scores are applied. It also serves as a warning for the industry to develop robust detection methods for synthetic technical content to prevent the erosion of confidence in official vulnerability advisories and maintain the reliability of global security standards.
