## Summary
Mathematician Andreas Thom has publicly accused OpenAI of "dishonesty" and a lack of transparency regarding its training data sources following the company’s announcement of significant mathematical breakthroughs. Thom alleges that his private interactions with ChatGPT may have influenced AI models to solve problems in non-sofic groups, a field where OpenAI acknowledged building on prior unpublished work by Thom and Gábor Kun. The dispute centers on OpenAI’s failure to provide evidence or disclosures proving that user data was not incorporated into the training pipelines that powered these discoveries.

## Key Takeaways
- Mathematician Andreas Thom claims his private conversations with ChatGPT may have contributed to OpenAI’s success in solving problems related to non-sofic groups, raising concerns about data usage and intellectual property rights.
- OpenAI initially failed to acknowledge recent contributions from Thom and fellow mathematician Gábor Kun in its public writeup of a major result, only quietly amending the text after widespread criticism from the mathematical community.
- Thom argues that OpenAI’s response to his inquiries was insufficient because it did not address whether his interactions entered the vast pools of training data, labeling the company’s lack of evidence as "dishonest" and noting that researchers cannot reverse-engineer proprietary training pipelines.

## Context
This incident occurs within a broader industry debate concerning the provenance of AI training data and the ethical implications of using user-generated content to train large language models. It follows closely on the heels of similar controversies involving other mathematicians, such as Tristan Buckmaster, who questioned whether OpenAI’s Codex benefited from his interactions with the model. The situation highlights a growing tension between AI companies’ proprietary approaches to data management and the academic community’s demand for transparency and proper attribution in scientific discovery.

## Implications
The dispute underscores a critical gap in accountability within the AI sector, particularly as models achieve breakthroughs in specialized fields like advanced mathematics. If AI systems are trained on private user interactions without explicit consent or disclosure, it raises significant ethical and legal questions regarding intellectual property and credit for scientific contributions. This case may pressure AI developers to adopt more transparent data governance practices and provide clearer mechanisms for researchers to verify whether their work has been used in model training, potentially reshaping how academic collaborations with tech giants are structured in the future.
