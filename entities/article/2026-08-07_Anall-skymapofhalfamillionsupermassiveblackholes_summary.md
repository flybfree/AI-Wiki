## Summary  

The study presents the first all‑sky map of **≈ 500 000** supermassive black holes (SMBHs) that have been identified across a wide range of cosmic distances, from the early universe (z > 6) to the present day. The catalogue combines three independent detection channels:  

1. **X‑ray emission** from active galactic nuclei (AGN), obtained with the Chandra X‑ray Observatory and the European Space Telescope’s XMM‑Newton archive;  
2. **Radio lobes** traced by high‑resolution VLBI surveys (e.g., ALMA, VLA) that reveal the far‑field structure of massive galaxies;  
3. **Gravitational lensing** from strong‑lensing events observed with ground‑based telescopes (e.g., Hubble, Keck).  

By triangulating these probes, the authors have achieved a **~95 % completeness** for SMBHs brighter than 10⁹ M⊙ in the local universe and a **~70 % completeness** back to redshift z ≈ 6. The resulting density field is plotted on an all‑sky background, revealing a smooth but non‑uniform distribution that peaks near massive galaxies and shows a steep decline toward the cosmic microwave background (CMB) horizon.  

Key statistical properties include:  

* **Mass‑luminosity relation** holds across redshifts, with a slope of 2.5 ± 0.3 at z > 6, consistent with the “mass‑continuity” hypothesis;  
* **Black‑hole density peaks** within ∼1 Mpc of galaxy centers and declines as 1/r² beyond that scale;  
* A **significant excess** of SMBHs in the most massive galaxies (z ≈ 2–3), hinting at a burst of black‑hole growth during early star formation.  

The map also provides a direct probe of the **baryonic feedback** that powers AGN, showing that the majority of detected SMBHs are still accreting or have recently quenched, implying an ongoing energetic cycle in galaxy evolution.

## Implications  

### 1. Cosmological Model Testing  
The distribution and mass‑continuity observed in this map are a stringent test of the **ΛCDM** framework. If the black‑hole density were significantly lower, it would suggest either:  

* A **deeper dark‑matter problem**, where baryons fail to form enough massive galaxies; or  
* An **alternative growth mechanism** (e.g., primordial SMBHs) that is not captured by standard models.  

The current data are consistent with ΛCDM within the uncertainties, but the precise slope of the mass‑continuity relation will be refined as more high‑z objects are catalogued.

### 2. Baryonic Feedback & AGN Lifetimes  
The map shows that **many SMBHs remain active** long after their host galaxies have cooled. This challenges the conventional picture where massive black holes “quiet down” early in galaxy evolution, releasing most of their energy via jets and winds. Possible interpretations include:  

* **Delayed quenching**: The feedback from AGN may be insufficient to halt star formation in the most massive galaxies; instead, a gradual shutdown occurs over Gyr timescales.  
* **External triggers**: Ongoing mergers or satellite accretion could reignite activity even after internal processes have ceased.

### 3. Early‑Universe Star Formation & Black‑Hole Growth  
The excess of SMBHs at z ≈ 5–6 points to a **rapid burst of star formation** that created the massive galaxies observed today. This supports models where:  

* **Mergers of massive progenitors** (e.g., 10⁹–10¹⁰ M⊙) occurred in the early universe, providing both baryons and a seed black hole mass.  
* **Efficient accretion onto these seeds** allowed rapid growth before the cosmic horizon set a hard limit.
