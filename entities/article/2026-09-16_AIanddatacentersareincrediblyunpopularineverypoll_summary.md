## Summary
A recent joint poll by The New York Times and Siena University reveals that AI data centers are deeply unpopular among American likely voters, with 61% opposing their construction. Despite this widespread opposition, neither major political party holds a significant advantage in handling the issue, as voters generally trust their own party to manage it better than the other.

## Key Takeaways
- **High Opposition Rates:** A majority of respondents (61%) opposed the building of new AI data centers, while only 14% strongly supported them. This sentiment was particularly strong among Kamala Harris voters and non-voters, who showed opposition rates of 74% and 67%, respectively.
- **Primary Motivations:** The leading reasons for opposition included environmental concerns regarding water usage (32%), negative impacts on local communities (21%), and general distrust or dislike of AI technology (18%). A significant portion of opponents (38%) preferred a total ban rather than just limits on construction.
- **Low Priority Status:** Despite the high level of opposition, AI and data centers ranked low as a primary deciding factor for midterm elections. For most demographics, less than 1% cited this issue as their top concern, with younger voters (ages 18–29) being the only group where it registered at 3%.

## Context
This polling data aligns with earlier surveys from NBC News and Gallup, which also indicated that public sentiment toward AI infrastructure is increasingly negative. The results reflect a growing "AI backlash" in political discourse, where concerns over resource consumption, environmental degradation, and community disruption are becoming central to public opinion. This trend highlights a disconnect between the rapid technological expansion of AI infrastructure and the general public's comfort level with its physical footprint and societal impact.

## Implications
The findings suggest that AI companies and policymakers face significant political headwinds in expanding data center capacity. While the issue may not currently dominate election campaigns, the strong opposition based on environmental and community concerns indicates a fragile social license for expansion. Industry stakeholders must anticipate stricter regulatory environments and potential local resistance to new construction projects. Furthermore, the lack of a clear partisan edge implies that this is a cross-cutting cultural and economic issue rather than a strictly ideological one, requiring nuanced communication strategies from both tech leaders and government officials to mitigate public backlash.
