## Summary  
A growing number of U.S. restaurants are adopting a tip‑free model by raising menu prices and eliminating tips altogether, aiming to pay staff higher, more predictable wages—particularly the kitchen workers who traditionally earn low base salaries while waiting for guest tips. The practice is framed as a way to reduce reliance on the generosity of strangers and to address inequities in how tipping income is distributed among employees.

## Key Takeaways  
- Restaurants such as La Cigale in San Francisco now charge $140 per person (up from lower prices) and state “What you see is what you pay,” removing tips entirely.  
- Tipping creates a two‑tier income system: front‑of‑house staff can earn far more than kitchen workers, who often work minimum wage during non‑service periods.  
- Customer acceptance varies; some venues like Talulla in Massachusetts have reverted to tipping after price hikes prove unsustainable in certain seasons.

## Context  
The shift reflects a broader industry trend away from the tip‑based compensation model that dominates U.S. service restaurants, influencing labor economics and consumer expectations. While not directly AI‑driven, the decision involves algorithmic pricing strategies and data on staff earnings to balance cost and revenue.

## Implications  
Adopting tip‑free structures could set a precedent for more equitable pay across service sectors, prompting other businesses to reconsider how variable income affects morale and retention. It also raises questions about consumer willingness to pay higher prices when the “tip” is no longer an option, potentially reshaping market dynamics in hospitality and beyond.
