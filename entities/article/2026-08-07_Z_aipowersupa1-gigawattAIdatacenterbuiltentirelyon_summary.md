## Summary  
Z.ai has completed a 1‑gigawatt AI data center that runs exclusively on Chinese‑made chips, using them to train its GLM model family and now operating multiple clusters of roughly 10 000 chips each. The facility’s gigawatt power draw is comparable to running 750 000 homes, but the lower performance‑per‑watt of domestic accelerators means it delivers less usable training compute than a similar‑size Nvidia system. This move underscores China’s push to decouple AI training from Western hardware.  

## Key Takeaways  
- Z.ai’s facility uses only domestically produced chips, eliminating reliance on Nvidia silicon and aligning with the U.S. Commerce Department’s restriction list.  
- The 1 GW power consumption translates to roughly 750 000 homes but yields less training compute because Chinese accelerators such as Huawei Ascend are less efficient than Nvidia Blackwell parts.  
- Domestic chip supply constraints—including HBM shortages and SMIC node utilization above 93%—limit how many accelerators can be manufactured to sustain the gigawatt demand, highlighting a production bottleneck.  

## Context  
China is planning a nationwide AI data‑center grid of about 2 trillion yuan over five years, with at least 80 % of underlying technology sourced domestically. The plan faces challenges: SMIC’s most advanced 7nm‑class N+2 node is already near full utilization, and limited HBM supply caps the number of Ascend accelerators that can be built. These bottlenecks mirror broader industry concerns about domestic chip production capacity versus global demand.  

## Implications  
The gigawatt Chinese data center illustrates a trade‑off: local manufacturing can reduce dependence on foreign hardware but may sacrifice performance and efficiency, potentially affecting AI competitiveness. It also accelerates the race for alternative silicon standards and could reshape global supply chains as firms weigh reliability against cost and geopolitical risk.
