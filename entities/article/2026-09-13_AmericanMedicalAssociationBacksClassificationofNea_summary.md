## Summary
The American Medical Association has formally endorsed classifying nearsightedness as a disease, aligning with global health initiatives to improve access to treatments and insurance coverage for children. This policy shift aims to address the rising prevalence of myopia by promoting evidence-based interventions that slow its progression and reduce long-term vision risks.

## Key Takeaways
- The AAO resolution supports classifying nearsightedness as a disease, including by CMS, to ensure insurance coverage for treatments that slow myopia progression in children.
- High myopia carries significant health risks, including retinal detachment, glaucoma, cataracts, and myopic maculopathy, necessitating proactive management strategies.
- FDA-approved interventions such as special eyeglasses, multifocal contact lenses, and orthokeratology are recognized for their ability to delay onset and slow myopia progression in children aged 6–12.

## Context
This development reflects a broader global effort led by the American Academy of Ophthalmology since 2021, which has highlighted nearsightedness as a growing public health crisis. With myopia prevalence nearly doubling in the U.S. over the past 50 years and projections indicating half the world’s population may be affected by 2050, there is urgent need for coordinated medical and educational responses to mitigate long-term consequences.

## Implications
By reclassifying nearsightedness as a disease, the AAO underscores its importance in pediatric healthcare and public policy. This shift encourages expanded insurance coverage, standardized screening protocols, and greater research funding into myopia management technologies. For the AI and health tech sector, this recognition opens opportunities for developing predictive analytics tools, personalized treatment algorithms, and digital monitoring systems to support early intervention and long-term vision care optimization.
