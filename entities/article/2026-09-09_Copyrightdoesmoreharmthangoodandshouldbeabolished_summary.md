## Summary  
The article argues that copyright, a legal framework intended to protect intellectual property, actually creates more societal harm than benefit. It contends that the system stifles creativity, concentrates economic advantages among a few, and imposes substantial costs on society, suggesting that abolishing copyright in favor of simpler contract‑based protection would be preferable.

## Key Takeaways  
- Copyright laws hinder genuine creativity by granting exclusive rights that discourage sharing and remixing.  
- The perceived benefits of copyright are questionable; the economic gains often outweigh the social costs.  
- Contract law alone is sufficient to protect intellectual property, making a full abolition of copyright feasible.

## Context  
In the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence, copyright plays a pivotal role in shaping how data can be used for training models. Many AI systems rely on datasets that contain copyrighted material, raising legal and ethical questions about ownership, consent, and fair use. The broader industry discourse often debates whether current copyright regimes support open innovation or merely entrench proprietary control.

## Implications  
If copyright is abolished as proposed, AI developers could more freely incorporate diverse data sources, fostering a culture of openness that accelerates research and reduces legal risk. However, this shift also challenges the existing economic incentives for creators and may require new contractual mechanisms to ensure fair compensation without relying on statutory copyright protection.
