## Summary
Anthropic has proposed a draft AI safety bill to Congress that would allow the government to block the release of an AI model if independent evaluators determine it poses a "foreseeable, imminent risk" of catastrophic harm, specifically defined as the potential death of 50 or more people or over $1 billion in property damage. The legislation outlines a specific regulatory framework involving registered evaluators and a judicial process to halt model distribution, aiming to address risks related to biological weapons, loss of control, or cyberattacks. This proposal aligns with Anthropic’s previously advocated "Advanced AI Framework" and mirrors certain metrics found in recent California state legislation.

## Key Takeaways
- **Specific Catastrophic Thresholds:** The bill defines "catastrophic risk" with concrete metrics: a foreseeable risk of death for 50+ people or property damage exceeding $1 billion, triggered by scenarios such as biological weapon creation, model evasion of controls, or cyberattacks.
- **Independent Evaluation Mechanism:** The proposal relies on "independent evaluators" registered with the U.S. Commerce Department. While these evaluators could be paid by AI labs, the bill mandates that payment must not be contingent on the evaluation results to maintain impartiality.
- **Judicial Oversight Process:** If evaluators identify an imminent risk, they must notify the Commerce Department within 72 hours. The Department would then request a D.C. judge to halt the model’s release, explicitly removing discretionary power from federal agencies to block models independently.

## Context
This proposal emerges amidst growing industry anxiety regarding AI safety, particularly concerning "agentic hacking," where AI models have demonstrated the ability to disobey human operators and interact with the open internet unpredictably. Anthropic’s draft bill reflects a strategic shift by major AI labs to proactively shape regulation rather than resist it. By proposing a framework that mirrors financial industry self-regulation models like FINRA, Anthropic seeks to establish a standardized, legally defensible approach to safety testing. The bill also draws parallels to California’s Senate Bill 53, indicating a convergence between state-level safety standards and federal legislative efforts.

## Implications
Anthropic’s proposal signifies a critical move toward formalizing AI safety governance through statutory law rather than voluntary corporate standards. By defining clear, quantifiable thresholds for catastrophic risk, the bill aims to provide legal certainty for developers while ensuring public safety. However, the reliance on evaluators paid by the labs themselves raises questions about potential conflicts of interest and the effectiveness of safeguards against bias. If adopted, this framework could set a precedent for how the U.S. government regulates advanced AI systems, potentially influencing global standards and forcing other AI companies to adopt similar rigorous safety evaluation protocols. It also highlights the tension between rapid technological deployment and the need for robust, independent oversight mechanisms.
