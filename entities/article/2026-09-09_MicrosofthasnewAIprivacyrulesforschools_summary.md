## Summary  
Microsoft has entered into a legally binding agreement with the American Federation of Teachers (AFT) and its New York City affiliate to impose strict AI‑privacy standards in U.S. schools. The pact obligates Microsoft not to train any models on student or educator data, bans student‑facing AI companions, mandates human review for high‑risk decisions, limits the amount of data collected, and requires plain‑language disclosures to families. The terms can be added to existing district contracts without renegotiation, giving schools a practical path to adopt safeguards ahead of anticipated bans.

## Key Takeaways
- Microsoft pledges not to train AI models on student or educator data, prohibits AI companions, and requires human review for “high‑risk” decisions.  
- Schools can incorporate the agreement into current contracts without full renegotiation, allowing immediate compliance.  
- The AFT frames the rules as “iron‑clad” because federal action is absent, positioning them as a necessary industry response.

## Context  
The agreement follows one‑year bans on student‑facing AI tools in New York City and Los Angeles districts, which prompted broader policy scrutiny of AI deployment in education. The AFT’s stance reflects a growing coalition of educators and unions that view unchecked AI as a privacy risk, while tech firms like Microsoft are responding to public pressure with contractual safeguards. Additionally, the AFT has launched a $23 million AI training hub for teachers funded by Anthropic, Microsoft, and OpenAI, illustrating an effort to balance innovation with oversight.

## Implications  
These rules set a precedent that corporate AI practices in schools must be contractually enforceable rather than left to voluntary compliance, potentially reshaping how ed‑tech vendors design data‑handling policies. By limiting data usage and mandating human oversight, the agreement may reduce long‑term liability for schools and encourage more transparent AI tools. However, critics argue that contractual terms alone cannot replace comprehensive federal regulation, leaving a loophole for future misuse of student data.
