## Summary
The article explores a critique by Father Paolo Benanti, a priest and advisor to the Catholic Church on AI, regarding the governance of artificial intelligence development. Benanti argues that the "doomsday" rhetoric surrounding superintelligence—often championed by the leaders of major AI labs—serves as a distraction from the need for democratic regulation. He warns that allowing a small group of private companies to dictate safety standards creates a "cartel-like" environment that excludes public oversight and prevents a balanced, transparent debate on how to constrain these technologies.

## Key Takeaways
- **The Danger of Private Governance:** Benanti argues that ethics in AI should not be determined solely by corporate boards of trustees; instead, he advocates for democratic regulation where safety standards are debated and established in the public square.
- **"Cartel" Behavior:** The article highlights a concern that major labs may use "fear-based" safety warnings to position themselves as the only entities capable of managing AI risks, thereby securing a monopoly on influence and preventing outside oversight.
- **Conflict of Interest:** The text notes that while companies like OpenAI and Anthropic call for safety measures (such as third-party evaluations or bans on recursive self-improvement), these calls are complicated by massive financial incentives, including the pursuit of public markets and geopolitical competition between the US and China.
- **The Danger of Deification:** Benanti criticizes the tendency to describe AI as a "divinity" or something beyond human understanding, arguing that this mystical framing obscures the reality of the technology—which is based on mathematics—and hinders rational, objective discourse about control mechanisms.

## Context
This discussion emerges during a period of intense "AI safety" alarmism, where researchers and CEOs have warned of existential risks posed by superintelligent systems. Simultaneously, the industry is facing immense pressure from geopolitical rivals (specifically China) and investors to accelerate development speeds. This creates a paradox where companies publicly advocate for "slowdowns" while privately competing to achieve the most advanced capabilities as quickly as possible.

## Implications
These insights suggest that the primary challenge for AI governance may not just be technical, but structural. If safety standards are dictated by the same entities that profit from rapid development, there is a risk of "regulatory capture," where rules are designed to favor incumbents rather than public safety. For the field, this means that achieving "safe" AI requires moving beyond corporate-led self-regulation toward a framework where democratic oversight ensures that human control remains the priority over market dominance.
