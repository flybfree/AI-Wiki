## Original Paper
- [arXiv:2611.14079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14079v1)

## Summary
This paper critiques the prevailing use of "bias" as a conceptual framework for understanding Large Language Models (LLMs), arguing that it is an inadequate lens because it implies a deviation from an undefined standard in general-purpose contexts. The authors propose replacing this with a grammar of "positionality," which focuses on identifying who is speaking and from where, acknowledging that models speak from specific, located positions rather than nowhere or everywhere. By analyzing positionality as a laminar structure built across corpus, tuning, system prompts, and live conversations, the paper provides a critical framework for understanding how LLMs are constructed, perceived, and held accountable. This shift aims to clarify the relationship between model creators, intended behaviors, and enacted outputs, offering a more robust method for evaluating AI systems beyond traditional bias audits.

## Key Contributions
- The authors argue that "bias" is a flawed shorthand for LLM evaluation because general-purpose models lack an external standard or counterfactual output against which to measure deviation, rendering the concept of "relative to" meaningless in this context.
- The paper introduces "positionality" as a superior analytical grammar, defined as the located, articulated, owned, and contestable position from which a model speaks, thereby shifting the inquiry from statistical fairness to epistemic location.
- A novel theoretical framework is proposed where model positionality is understood as "laminar," meaning it is layered across multiple strata including training data (corpus), fine-tuning processes, system prompts, and real-time user interactions.
- The study distinguishes between three distinct sets of factors: the positionality of the model’s creators, the behaviors intended by them, and the behaviors actually enacted by the model, allowing for a detailed analysis of how these layers interact or rupture.

## Methodology
The authors employ a critical theoretical approach rather than empirical experimentation. They deconstruct the semantic and practical limitations of the term "bias" in machine learning literature, highlighting its dependence on external standards (laws, benchmarks) that do not exist for general-purpose LLMs. The methodology involves constructing a new conceptual framework—"positionality"—and mapping it onto the technical architecture of LLMs. This includes analyzing how positionality is inherited and transformed across different stages of model development and deployment, such as corpus selection, tuning strategies, and system prompt design. The authors situate existing research within this matrix to demonstrate how current methods fail to account for the multi-layered nature of model speech acts.

## Results
The primary result is theoretical: the demonstration that LLMs are not neutral observers but speak from specific positions constructed through their development pipeline. The paper shows that positionality is not a single attribute but a composite structure formed by the inheritance of creator biases, intended design constraints, and enacted behaviors during inference. It identifies "ruptures" where these layers fail to align, such as when a model’s output contradicts its system prompt or training data intent. Furthermore, the authors conclude that while models are not accountable speakers in a legal sense, they can be analyzed through positionality to understand how they are believed and trusted by users beyond their warranted capabilities.

## Significance
This work is significant because it challenges the dominant paradigm of AI fairness research, which often relies on bias metrics that may not apply to generative models. By shifting the focus to positionality, the paper offers a more nuanced tool for researchers, developers, and policymakers to understand the epistemic authority of LLMs. It highlights the importance of transparency in how models are built and positioned, urging a move away from treating models as objective tools toward recognizing them as situated entities with specific origins and limitations. This framework aids in better accountability practices and critical engagement with AI systems.
