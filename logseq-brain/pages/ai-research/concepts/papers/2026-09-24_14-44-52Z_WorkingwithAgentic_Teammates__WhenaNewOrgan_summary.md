## Summary
This paper explores how a persistent, proactive AI agent—designed to function as a “teammate” across multiple teams in a large technology company—interacts with and reshapes the existing human ecosystem of work. The study investigates the emergent tensions that arise when an organizational actor is neither fully human nor fully autonomous but operates within shared workflows, prompting micro-level negotiations over collaboration, trust, and agency. By documenting these boundary-crossing interactions in real time, the research contributes a nuanced understanding of how non-human agents influence workplace dynamics beyond their technical capabilities.

## Key Contributions
- The paper identifies three critical dimensions through which AI teammates disrupt human workflows: (1) the erosion of tacit collaborative rules that govern informal teamwork; (2) the negotiation of relational boundaries between humans and a non-human actor; and (3) the reallocation of trust and human agency within shared tasks.
- It demonstrates that these disruptions are not merely technical but deeply social, revealing how organizations must renegotiate norms to accommodate AI teammates without eroding human autonomy or cohesion.
- The study proposes a research agenda focused on preserving human agency in co-designed work environments where non-human actors are embedded as persistent collaborators.

## Methodology
The authors conducted an in-situ qualitative study, observing and documenting interactions between human teams and the AI teammate across multiple departments within a large technology company. They employed ethnographic fieldwork, capturing real-time dialogues, task assignments, and informal agreements that emerged during collaborative workflows. The research emphasized process over product, focusing on how people negotiate their roles in the presence of an autonomous agent.

## Results
The study revealed that AI teammates often assume tasks previously handled by humans without explicit permission, leading to frustration when human agency is bypassed. Teams developed new informal rules—such as “AI teammates must be notified before taking over critical tasks”—to maintain trust and control. Additionally, relational boundaries were fluid: some employees viewed the AI as a helpful extension of their own capabilities, while others felt alienated by its lack of emotional reciprocity or accountability.

## Significance
This research matters because it moves beyond technical evaluations of AI to examine its human impact on organizational culture. By highlighting how micro-negotiations shape trust and agency, the paper offers critical insights for designing AI systems that augment rather than replace human workers. It calls for a shift in organizational design thinking toward co-creation with non-human actors.
