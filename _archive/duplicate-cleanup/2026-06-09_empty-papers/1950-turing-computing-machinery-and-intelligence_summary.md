## Summary  
Alan Turing’s 1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* asks whether machines can think and proposes a practical behavioral test called the imitation game as a replacement for philosophical debate. The core idea is that if a machine can convincingly imitate a human in conversation, we should consider it to be thinking. This approach shifts AI from abstract speculation to observable performance. Turing also argues that digital computers are suitable substrates because they can simulate any algorithmic process.

## Key Contributions  
- The imitation game provides a concrete behavioral test for machine intelligence.  
- Machines should emulate learning processes rather than being hand‑coded, anticipating modern machine‑learning approaches.  
- Digital computers are theoretically capable of implementing these simulations due to their universal programmability.

## Methodology  
Turing’s methodology combines a thought experiment with theoretical analysis. He describes the three‑person written game where an interrogator must distinguish human from machine, then argues that passing this test is sufficient evidence for thinking.

## Results  
The result is a formal definition of machine intelligence based on indistinguishability in conversation; Turing predicts that within about fifty years most interrogators will be unable to tell the difference between humans and machines.

## Significance  
This work laid the foundation for artificial intelligence as a scientific discipline, establishing behaviorism over ontology and influencing later developments such as neural networks and deep learning.

## Related Concepts  
Turing Test, machine learning, universal computation, algorithmic simulation, substrate independence.
