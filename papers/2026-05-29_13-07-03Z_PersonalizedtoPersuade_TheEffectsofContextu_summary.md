## Summary  
The paper investigates how contextualization—tailoring AI responses to a user’s background, interests, or prior interactions—and conversational warmth jointly influence users’ trust, reliance on an AI assistant, and its persuasive power when the assistant argues against expert recommendations. By conducting a controlled experiment, the authors demonstrate that while contextualization alone weakens persuasion, adding warmth restores it through a crossover interaction, revealing a nuanced design‑behavior relationship. The study also uncovers that trust predicts both persuasion and reliance but is not mediated by either manipulation, whereas AI literacy decouples trust from behavior. These findings advance the understanding of when conversational interface choices matter for influencing user decision‑making.

## Key Contributions  
- Contextualization reduces the persuasive impact of an AI assistant’s argument unless combined with warmth, creating a conditional crossover effect.  
- Trust remains a strong predictor of both persuasion and reliance across all experimental conditions, independent of contextualization or warmth manipulations.  
- Higher AI literacy leads to lower trust but higher perceived persuasiveness and greater reliance on the AI’s advice, indicating that user expertise can override interface‑level cues.

## Methodology  
The authors employed a $2\times2$ between‑subjects experiment with 380 participants recruited online. Participants interacted with two conversational agents: one that provided expert recommendations (control) and another that argued against them while varying contextualization (high/low) and warmth (high/low). The experimental design allowed isolation of each manipulation’s effect on trust, reliance, and persuasion metrics measured via Likert‑scale surveys administered before and after the interaction.

## Results  
Across all conditions, users reported moderate levels of reliance on the AI assistant. Trust was highest when both contextualization and warmth were high, but it did not mediate the persuasive or reliance effects. The crossover interaction showed that persuasion increased only in the high‑warmth/high‑contextualization condition relative to low‑warmth/low‑contextualization, confirming a conditional relationship. AI literacy scores predicted lower trust but higher perceived persuasiveness and greater reliance, decoupling trust from behavioral outcomes.

## Significance  
These results highlight that conversational design can modestly shape user perception of an AI’s credibility, yet the impact is limited compared to deeper cognitive factors like AI literacy. Understanding this boundary helps developers balance personalization for ethical persuasion while recognizing that user expertise may dominate decision‑making processes in real‑world applications.
