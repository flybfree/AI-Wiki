On August 6th, the Minneapolis Air Route Traffic Control Center
lost radar and communications for around two hours
. The outage disrupted more than
1,100 flights
across the center’s 330,000 square mile, nine-state airspace sector.
Two days earlier, on August 4th, President Donald Trump departed the White House inside his Marine One military helicopter. As Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) protocol requires, the pilots tried to contact Reagan National Airport to tell them to halt commercial traffic during the flight. But the air traffic controllers never heard the transmission and cleared a different airplane for takeoff — which came closer than the minimum safe distance allowed. (When this story first broke in
The Wall Street Journal
,
the White House dismissed it, noting that Trump was never in “harm’s way.”)
The outages were surprising, given the administration’s stated ambitions to rebuild the nation’s air traffic control system. Last February, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy invited
major Trump donor
Elon Musk
and his Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE)
to overhaul the FAA. On X, Musk
promised
“rapid safety upgrades to the air traffic control system.”
But Musk ended up as more wrecking ball than problem solver. His agency did not help the FAA buy or implement any major system upgrades.
He wrongly accused Verizon
of running the FAA’s communications system, while also failing to
turn Starlink into the agency’s telecom provider of choice
. Its signature achievement was the termination of
400 FAA employees
, including
maintenance technicians
at 18 different air traffic control centers.
Musk ended up as more wrecking ball than problem solver.
Meanwhile, the “rapid safety upgrades” Musk promised never materialized. The last few years have seen
equipment failures
in
Dallas
, Denver, and Houston. Unexplained fumes in
Atlanta
,
Newark
, and the
Potomac
air traffic control center. Chronic
staffing shortages
in Boston, New York, Los Angeles, Orlando, and Philadelphia.
The nation’s unstable air traffic control system made 2025 the worst year for flight delays in over a decade, according to the
US Public Interest Research Group
. One in four flights was delayed, diverted, or canceled for a total of almost 1.7 million disruptions. Tarmac delays, where airplanes are held on the ground but passengers must stay on board, were up 64 percent compared to 2024. And an estimated 2.4 million checked bags were lost or damaged on domestic flights alone.
In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act authorized
$12.5 billion in supplemental funding
for a complete overhaul of the FAA’s air traffic control system. Of that total, roughly $5 billion is dedicated for software upgrades. And since Elon Musk’s DOGE fell short of its promises, the administration has invited Peter Thiel, another
Republican megadonor
, to step in at the FAA. His company Palantir is deeply integrated with the FAA’s systems, making it a prime contender for many of those contracts, despite no significant prior experience with air traffic control.
According to air traffic controllers — several of whom spoke to
The Verge
on condition of anonymity so as not to jeopardize their jobs — these moves will do little to alleviate the issues on the ground (and in the air). They say that the FAA’s most urgent needs remain on the personnel side.
None of the $12.5 billion in new FAA funding authorized by the One Big Beautiful Bill goes toward personnel. Instead, it seems destined for Palantir’s pockets.
In Trump’s second term, Palantir, the analytics company that Thiel cofounded and currently chairs, has rapidly extended itself across the entire federal government like a giant, data-sucking squid.
It has operated as a fringe government contractor since 2008, beginning by building databases for the
Department of Defense and the FBI
and spending the next decade and a half building up its defense business.
It surpassed $1 billion in federal contracts for the first time in 2025
. In 2026 it will surpass $1.5 billion. Palantir is now one of the 100 largest government contractors in the US.
You’ve probably heard about some of its projects.
It built the Maven AI-powered targeting system
that made
its wartime debut earlier this year in Iran
. It designed
a mapping system that can generate
targets for ICE agents in real time. It
developed a search engine
for the Department of Health and Human Services that allowed DOGE employees to identify and cancel more than
$780 million worth of grants
linked to DEI initiatives. It is even working on a “
mega-database
” of US citizens and their financial information for the IRS.
Legislators and watchdog groups have raised concerns around
data privacy
,
poor
accuracy
, and/or the
legality
of every program mentioned above. But Palantir’s reach keeps growing.
The FAA started using Palantir’s
Foundry, a data integration and analytics system
, in 2024 during the Biden administration. All of the agency’s data and much of its tech stack now runs through Palantir systems.
In 2025 it won a
no-bid contract
to make an AI-powered runway collision avoidance tool. And another
no-bid contract
to add AI to the FAA’s grants system, which pays for up to
95 percent of air traffic control improvements
at small airports. This year the FAA
opened bidding
for “an ontology-driven Foundry Operating System” to drive AI integration across the agency. The
Ontology data integration schema
and the
Foundry operating system
are proprietary Palantir technologies. Of course the company won that contract too.
All of the agency’s data and much of its tech stack now runs through Palantir systems
Palantir was even selected as one of three finalists to bid on the FAA’s $875 million
SMART national airspace system
contract. SMART is an AI-powered tool that will help controllers manage traffic flows and predict disruptions before they disrupt operations. Once again, Palantir’s integration with FAA systems was considered to be far more important than experience in the industry.
Palantir ultimately lost the competition to a Boston-based startup called
Air Space Intelligence
, whose executive team includes four Palantir alumni: VP of operations Dan Rickert, head of US operations Steph Starsia, growth lead Connor Ryan, and chief of staff Tate Krasner.
(Air Space Intelligence responded to
The Verge
’s request for comment by referring us to the FAA’s media relations team.)
Meanwhile, real-time concerns are piling up — and FAA leaders have rushed to blame the agency’s ongoing problems on its personnel. On August 7th, thunderstorms in the Northeast caused around
9,000 flight delays and 2,000 cancellations
. In a post on X, Duffy implied that the main reason for delays was “controllers calling out sick.”
His post outraged air traffic controllers around the country. “These facilities have been understaffed for decades,” said one. “If two controllers can’t make it to work for some reason, that’s 40-60 percent less staff. These crazy numbers make it seem like so much more than it is.”
As we reported in February 2025
, the nation’s air traffic control system was already unstable even before Musk’s intervention. Almost 90 percent of the nation’s air traffic control centers were
chronically understaffed
, and the overwhelming majority of technology in those centers was classified as “unsustainable” or “potentially unsustainable” by the
FAA
. But while the tech is set for an upgrade, staffing remains a huge — and underfunded — issue.
Shut out of the One Big Beautiful Bill, the agency must instead draw on its
normal 2026 budget for staffing
, which includes only $239 million in new funds to support controller hiring initiatives.
One such initiative was the “Level Up Your Career” initiative that encouraged gamers to apply to
become air traffic controllers
. According to Duffy, the campaign yielded
2,000 applications
, which he described as a “HISTORIC” success. Still, it’s a long road ahead for these candidates:
Thirty percent of candidates wash out during training
, and it takes up to three years to become a fully certified controller.
To be clear, any investment in America’s aging air traffic system is a good thing.
No administration since Ronald Reagan’s
has launched as many projects as the Trump administration has done since 2025. Even longtime observers in the field of aviation safety are impressed.
“There’s a lot going on,” said Hassan Shahidi, president and CEO of the Flight Safety Foundation. “Replacing copper wires across the country. Refurbishing and replacing radars. This administration certainly has focused on implementation and getting things done.”
But Palantir’s software fixes are years away from deployment. Its AI-based tools are still completely unproven. And front-line controllers have yet to see much benefit.
One air traffic controller had not even heard of any of the initiatives
The Verge
asked about. “The only new technology we have gotten was a new screen that runs off of the same old hardware,” he said. “So that says a lot.”
“The only new technology we have gotten was a new screen that runs off of the same old hardware.”
— Anonymous air traffic controller
Despite the controller shortfalls, the agency actually thinks it can operate with fewer of them. In May it released a revised “full staffing target” that calls for only
12,563 active controllers
, down from its original target of 14,633. It cites AI and better scheduling practices for the change in headcount.
That’s tough to believe when many facilities already schedule people for 10 hours a day, six days a week, multiple weeks in a row.
“It is more attractive politically for them to award large contracts to companies … rather than focus on the workforce itself,” said one 20-year veteran of the FAA. “I would rather see our pay and staffing issues adequately addressed first.”
The schedule can be so punishing that burnout and exhaustion are common. Controllers risk medical suspension or even the loss of their job if they seek professional help for anxiety or depression. The suicide rate for air traffic controllers is
eight times higher than the national average
. One controller who spoke to
The Verge
knew of two colleagues who have died by suicide this year. (The FAA did not respond to a request for comment.)
No wonder the air traffic control system remains in crisis even after the deadliest year in commercial aviation since 2009.
