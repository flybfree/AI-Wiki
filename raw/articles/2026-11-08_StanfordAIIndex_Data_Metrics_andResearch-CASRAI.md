The
Stanford AI Index
is an independent annual initiative led by the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI) that tracks, collates, and visualizes longitudinal data on the state of artificial intelligence. Published annually as the
AI Index Report
, it provides researchers, policymakers, and institutional leaders with an empirical, multi-dimensional assessment of AI progress across technical capability, economic investment, research output, and public policy.
As the velocity of machine learning innovation accelerates, distinguishing verified technical breakthroughs from industry hype requires rigorous, unbiased measurement. The Stanford AI Index fills this critical gap in scholarly and public evaluation by aggregating data from academic publishers, private research laboratories, government agencies, and international standards bodies into open-access datasets and structured thematic chapters.
What It Is
First launched in 2017 under the One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100) and later integrated into Stanford HAI, the AI Index Report is widely recognized as one of the world’s most authoritative compendiums of AI metrics. Rather than presenting subjective predictions or promotional narratives, the project monitors concrete, measurable indicators: benchmark performance curves, hardware and computational expenditure, scholarly publication volumes, patent filings, corporate capital allocation, educational pipeline shifts, and national legislative enactments.
The 2024 edition represents the project’s seventh annual report, spanning 502 pages across nine distinct analytical chapters. For researchers operating within
machine learning
and the broader scope of
artificial intelligence
, the index provides vital baseline statistics for literature reviews, research grant justifications, and research strategy assessments.
Who Runs It and What It Covers
The Stanford AI Index is managed by an independent, interdisciplinary steering committee of academic scholars, computer scientists, economists, and policy researchers affiliated with Stanford HAI, co-founded by Ray Perrault and Jack Clark and managed by research director Nestor Maslej. The initiative collaborates with a global consortium of data partners, including the Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET) at Georgetown University, Quid, Lightcast, LinkedIn, McKinsey, and the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS).
The report’s analytical framework is organized into nine exhaustive chapters:
Research and Development:
Analyzes global publication volumes across journals, conference proceedings, and preprint repositories like arXiv, tracking institutional contributions by country and sector. In 2023, the report identified 149 foundation models released globally, with industry producing 51 notable models compared to academia’s 15, reflecting a significant shift in computational concentration. Notably, 65.7% of foundation models released in 2023 were open source, compared to 44.4% in 2022.
Technical Performance:
Benchmarks model performance against standard evaluations, including ImageNet, MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding), GSM8K (grade-school mathematics), HumanEval (code synthesis), and competition-level math. The report tracks benchmark saturation, noting where AI matches human performance and where complex reasoning remains unsolved.
Responsible AI:
Measures safety evaluations, algorithmic bias, hallucination rates, toxicity, copyright litigation, and the environmental footprint of training runs. The 2024 report emphasizes that standardized, reproducible evaluations for foundation model safety remain severely fragmented.
Economy:
Details private equity investment, venture capital flows, corporate adoption rates, and hiring metrics derived from Lightcast job-posting analytics. While overall private AI investment dipped slightly from peak pandemic surges, funding specifically directed at generative AI skyrocketed to $25.2 billion in 2023, an almost ninefold increase over 2022.
Science and Medicine:
A newly expanded domain tracking how AI models accelerate empirical discovery in genomics, protein folding, material synthesis (such as GNoME), and medical diagnostics.
Education:
Documents computer science undergraduate enrollments, specialized AI doctoral conferrals, and post-graduation industry versus academia placement ratios.
Policy and Governance:
Quantifies global legislative activity, executive orders, and regulatory enforcement. In the United States, AI-related federal regulations grew from just 1 in 2016 to 25 in 2023, aligning with international compliance benchmarks like the
EU AI Act and ISO standards
.
Diversity:
Examines demographic representation among CS faculty, doctoral candidates, and industry researchers.
Public Opinion:
Synthesizes multinational polling data (including Ipsos surveys across 31 countries) measuring public sentiment, trust, and perceived economic vulnerability.
How to Search and Use the AI Index
Researchers can access, query, and reuse the data generated by the Stanford AI Index through several official digital interfaces provided by Stanford HAI. To extract quantitative evidence for research papers, funding applications, or institutional reports, follow these five steps:
Navigate to the HAI AI Index Portal:
Visit the official hub at
https://aiindex.stanford.edu/
or
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/
to view the latest edition’s executive summary, interactive highlights, and individual chapter download links.
Download Thematic Chapter PDFs:
If examining a specific domain (such as Technical Performance or Responsible AI), download the isolated chapter document to review detailed methodological notes, sample sizes, and attribution footnotes without parsing the entire 500-page file.
Access the Public Raw Data Repository:
Open the official public data folder linked directly on the AI Index download page (hosted via Google Drive and synchronized on GitHub). The repository contains clean, machine-readable datasets in
.xlsx
and
.csv
formats for virtually every figure and chart published in the report.
Query the Global AI Vibrancy Tool:
Use the interactive web-based
Global AI Vibrancy Tool
on the AI Index website to compare national capabilities across 29 discrete indicators (such as AI patent counts, research publications, talent concentration, and private investment), adjusting indicator weights to model comparative national profiles.
Verify Source Licensing and Attribution:
All text, charts, and underlying data tables are released under an open Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY-ND or CC BY) license. Cite the specific report edition, chapter title, and primary data partner (e.g., CSET or Lightcast) when integrating charts into academic publications.
Strengths and Limits
To use the Stanford AI Index effectively in scholarly research, analysts must appreciate both its methodological strengths and structural limitations:
Empirical Rigor:
Unlike commercial analyst reports that conceal their methodologies behind paywalls, the AI Index provides transparent data provenance, clear sampling parameters, and direct access to underlying tabular datasets.
Cross-Sector Breadth:
By bridging technical benchmark logs, economic venture records, and legislative texts, the index enables cross-domain analyses that isolated computer science or economics papers cannot achieve.
Annual Publication Lag:
Because the report synthesizes full calendar-year records, metrics published in the spring reflect data through the preceding December. In fast-evolving domains like frontier model training runs, immediate commercial developments may outpace the annual publication cycle.
Commercial Estimation Constraints:
Frontier AI labs increasingly treat training hardware configurations, dataset mixtures, and compute budgets as proprietary trade secrets. Consequently, training costs (such as the estimated $78 million for GPT-4 and $191 million for Gemini Ultra) rely on rigorous hardware estimation models (such as Epoch AI analyses) rather than direct vendor disclosures.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the release cadence of the Stanford AI Index Report?
The Stanford AI Index Report is published annually during the spring (typically in April), covering comprehensive full-year indicators compiled throughout the preceding calendar year.
Can researchers freely download the raw data from the AI Index?
Yes. Stanford HAI provides open, unrestricted access to the complete set of raw data tables in Excel and CSV formats hosted on public cloud repositories, allowing researchers to reproduce visualizations or conduct secondary econometric analyses.
How does the AI Index evaluate foundation model training costs?
Because major commercial developers rarely disclose exact balance sheets, the report draws on computational modeling conducted in partnership with research organizations like Epoch AI, estimating training FLOPs (floating-point operations), hardware cluster configurations, duration, and prevailing electricity and GPU rental rates.
Does the report cover international AI regulations?
Yes. The Policy and Governance chapter systematically tracks legislative mentions, parliamentary debates, and formal enacted laws across dozens of countries, cataloging regulatory shifts including the European Union AI Act and executive directives in North America and Asia.
