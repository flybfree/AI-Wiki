[New Carolina Catalyst Fund prioritizes AI research]
Carly Evans
On Sept. 3, UNC announced the launch of a new internal fund intended to help faculty pursue high-risk, high-reward research projects. The Carolina Catalyst Fund offers awards of as much as $10,000 through Catalyst Spark and up to $75,000 through Catalyst Accelerate.
While the program is open to all disciplines, projects that use artificial intelligence methodologies, bring engineering design or translational thinking to bear on a scientific or scholarly challenge will receive special consideration. Projects that align with UNC’s strategic research priority areas will also be considered.
Assistant Vice Chancellor for Research Development Nathan Blouin said the University anticipates supporting 10 to 15 projects across both tiers. Applications are due Oct. 15, with award decisions expected in early December and projects expected to begin January 2027, Blouin said.
“The federal landscape certainly has been changing,” Blouin said. Federal grants have become more competitive each year, he added.
Xiaoming Liu, a distinguished professor in the Department of Computer Science who has served on federal grant review committees, said some federal grants are awarded to only approximately 5 percent of applicants. Liu described the Catalyst Fund as a starting point for more substantial grants.
Blouin said Vice Chancellor for Research Penny Gordon-Larsen and Provost Magnus Egerstedt supported the creation of the fund to encourage early-stage research and make research a central institutional priority.
Blouin said the funding comes from the Division of the Provost, while the Office of the Vice Chancellor for Research provides project management and administrative support.
Blouin added internal seed funding helps faculty germinate ideas, find collaborators, develop their projects and produce material that strengthens later grant applications.
He added that UNC’s previous internal seed investments have historically produced a fivefold to sevenfold return in follow-on grant funding or other resources.
According to Blouin, applicants are expected to submit high-risk, high-reward project ideas that are achievable within one year, illustrate a clear path to sustainability and ideally include plans for collaboration. Blouin said projects that establish a proof of concept or generate pilot data are especially attractive because the funding can reduce the financial risk of testing ideas.
Blouin said the fund’s design reflects trends in technology adoption and the priorities of federal research sponsors.
This emphasis comes as the Trump administration has made AI development a national priority. The administration’s July 2025 AI Action Plan called for accelerating innovation, expanding AI infrastructure and strengthening U.S. leadership in the technology.
The national focus was visible in Chapel Hill during the recent
G20 Innovations Ministerial meeting
, where government officials and technology leaders discussed AI development and its global implications.
Snigdha Chaturvedi, an associate professor and associate chair for research in the Department of Computer Science, said projects that fit the fund’s goals could involve developing AI models or applying them to problems in fields such as medicine. Her own research focuses on natural language processing and its social aspects.
Chaturvedi said simply calling an existing large language model may constitute using AI, while training a model or improving how it solves problems would more directly constitute AI research.
“AI plus X is a very hot topic,” Liu said.
He added that AI applications in drug discovery, health care and hospital operations are areas of particular interest at UNC.
The fund also raises questions about the role of universities in AI research. Liu said companies have more computing resources and larger graphics processing units, or GPU clusters, but universities have more time to think deeply and pursue high-risk approaches.
Chaturvedi said the rapid development of AI is precisely why more research is needed, especially in AI ethics and that researchers must study both its possibilities and “some undesirable outcomes.”
Similarly, Dhruv Srinivasan, a sophomore majoring in pre-business studies and data science and vice president of finance for the AI Ethics and Law Club at UNC, said ethical research has not kept pace with technological development.
Srinivasan said he hoped AI ethics researchers and professors would help review proposals for potential ethical implications. He also said students should learn about AI risks early if UNC wants to become a leader in AI.
Srinivasan added that it is important to discuss what is ethical about AI and how to properly fund it; otherwise, it could become too late.
“We got from planes on Kitty Hawk to launching a rocket to the moon 50 years later,” Srinivasan said. “You know, technology is advancing much faster than we can comprehend, and AI is no different from that.”
