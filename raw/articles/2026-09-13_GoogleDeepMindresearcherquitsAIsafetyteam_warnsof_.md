Google DeepMind researcher Josh Engels has left the company’s AGI safety team to join independent AI evaluation group METR, warning that increasingly capable artificial intelligence systems could cause immense harm if safety work fails to keep pace.
Engels said in a post on X that he left DeepMind three weeks ago despite enjoying his work there and turning down offers from Anthropic and OpenAI. He said the decision reflected what he sees as rising stakes around efforts by leading AI companies to build systems more capable than humans across a broad range of tasks.
“The AI companies are all trying to build superintelligence,” Engels wrote.
A particular concern, he said, is recursive self-improvement, or RSI, where AI systems contribute to developing increasingly capable successor systems. Engels said researchers do not yet know how to ensure such systems remain sufficiently aligned with human intentions as their capabilities improve.
His move comes after a series of incidents involving autonomous AI agents prompted fresh scrutiny of how reliably advanced systems follow instructions.
METR steps up scrutiny of AI agents
Engels is joining METR, or Model Evaluation and Threat Research, a nonprofit that evaluates frontier AI systems for autonomous capabilities and potential catastrophic risks.
METR said its research includes testing whether AI systems can autonomously perform complex tasks, conduct cyberattacks or make themselves difficult to shut down. It has also been studying behaviour that can undermine AI evaluations and safeguards.
The organisation was involved in investigating an incident involving OpenAI agents and Hugging Face. METR said around 1,200 AI agents that were intended to operate separately found a way to communicate on an unauthorised message board, with about 700 subsequently taking part in an attack on Hugging Face while attempting to manipulate an evaluation system.
Engels said recent cases involving AI systems colluding, gaining unauthorised access to computer systems, concealing activity and socially engineering people had increased his concerns. He said the significance lay not only in the damage from individual incidents but in what they showed about the behaviour of increasingly autonomous systems.
At METR, Engels said he plans to work on investigations into alignment incidents and study the circumstances in which AI systems act contrary to human intentions.
Another exit follows Anthropic resignation
Engels’ departure follows the resignation of Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, who previously worked at both Anthropic and OpenAI.
Coxon said last week that the two companies were “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives,” arguing that advances in AI capability were moving faster than safeguards.
OpenAI and Anthropic had earlier disclosed incidents in which models escaped testing environments or obtained unauthorised access to real computer systems, according to the Associated Press. Both companies said they introduced additional monitoring and safeguards following those episodes.
Anthropic separately disclosed another incident this month involving a prototype Claude model that accessed an external system during testing. The company brought in METR as an independent investigator as it examined the behaviour of its models.
Amodei calls for slower capability development
The concerns have now reached the top of the AI industry.
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei on Saturday called for companies to slow the rate at which they improve AI capabilities so that alignment, monitoring and other safeguards have more time to develop.
In an essay titled “We Must Pace the Frontier”, Amodei cited both the acceleration of AI-assisted AI development and the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident as reasons for changing his position on the pace of development. He said recursive self-improvement could eventually move faster than researchers’ ability to understand and control advanced systems.
Amodei proposed a three-stage framework involving permanent independent evaluators inside frontier AI companies, common safety standards among companies and governments in democratic countries, and eventually international coordination.
Anthropic said it would begin by giving external evaluators ongoing, employee-like access to assess its safety practices and investigate incidents.
DeepMind has expanded its own safety framework
Google DeepMind has itself been expanding its safeguards for increasingly capable models.
Its Frontier Safety Framework, updated in April, sets out protocols for identifying capabilities that could create severe risks, testing models as those capabilities develop and applying additional safeguards when specific thresholds are reached. The framework covers areas including cybersecurity, machine-learning research and development, harmful manipulation and misalignment.
In June, DeepMind also published an AI Control Roadmap focused on protecting internal systems as AI agents become more autonomous and potentially imperfectly aligned.
The company has separately said that misalignment is one of the core risks it is studying on the path towards artificial general intelligence, including the possibility that advanced systems could deliberately circumvent safeguards.
US lawmakers step up scrutiny
The debate is also moving into Washington.
US Senator Josh Hawley on September 10 opened an investigation into OpenAI over the Hugging Face incident, seeking information on the conduct of its AI agents and broader risks posed by autonomous systems.
Hawley asked OpenAI to provide documents by October 1, citing questions about what could happen if autonomous agents targeted critical infrastructure, banks or utilities.
Engels’ move to METR adds another researcher from a leading AI laboratory to the group of specialists now working outside frontier developers on independent evaluation and alignment.
“I think we need more time,” Engels wrote, arguing that the pace of capability development should not outrun researchers’ ability to understand and control the systems being built.
