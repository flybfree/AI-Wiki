## Summary
This paper aims to clarify how linguistic meaning is treated in different scientific and philosophical approaches, focusing on formal semantics, grounded semantics, and distributional semantics. It then contrasts these theories with the way transformer‑based language models encode meaning during training. The analysis shows that while human language acquisition relies heavily on structured rules and experiential grounding, transformers learn patterns from massive corpora without explicit semantic scaffolding.

## Key Takeaways
- Formal semantics treats meaning as a composition of syntactic structures according to strict logical rules, which transformer models do not replicate because they lack rule‑based inference.  
- Grounded semantics emphasizes the role of real‑world experience and usage in shaping understanding; transformers rely solely on statistical co‑occurrence rather than personal or contextual grounding.  
- Distributional semantics links meaning to patterns in data distributions, a method that aligns with transformer training but overlooks deeper conceptual representations humans use.

## Context
The rapid adoption of large language models has sparked debate about whether they truly grasp language semantics or merely mimic it through statistical regularities. This paper situates the discussion within ongoing research on AI interpretability and human‑like cognition, highlighting gaps between model behavior and linguistic theory.

## Implications
For practitioners, recognizing these differences can guide more responsible deployment of language models by setting realistic expectations about their semantic capabilities. For researchers, the work underscores the need for hybrid approaches that combine formal rigor with distributional learning to better emulate human comprehension.
