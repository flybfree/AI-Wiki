## Summary
This paper describes a mixed‑stakeholder workshop that examined 13 AI policing use cases for racial bias risks and found broad openness to adoption except recidivism risk assessment, highlighting three core questions guiding ethical evaluation: does the tool work, will it deliver genuine benefit, and will that benefit extend to everyone.

## Key Takeaways
- Participants rejected recidivism risk assessment mainly because of its premise rather than implementation flaws.
- Racial equity was foregrounded but did not narrow the set of concerns; bias remained a central issue.
- The deliberation emphasized practical efficacy, universal benefit, and equitable outcomes as primary decision criteria.

## Context
AI deployment in policing raises ethical scrutiny due to potential discrimination; this study adds a rare empirical view of community‑involved risk assessment that moves beyond theoretical frameworks toward actionable insights for inclusive design, illustrating how mixed stakeholder dialogue can surface latent risks.

## Implications
Policers and policymakers should embed equity questions early, recognizing that technical performance alone is insufficient; integrating community perspectives can reshape risk‑benefit analyses without sacrificing openness to AI tools, fostering more just and effective policing practices.
