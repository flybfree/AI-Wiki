## Summary
This narrative review examines the ethical and privacy risks emerging from large language models integrated into geospatial artificial intelligence systems. The authors identify eight critical challenges, including spatially structured bias amplification, passive location inference, hallucinated spatial facts, and regulatory gaps, while proposing a governance-aware architecture to map these risks to enforceable controls across the data lifecycle. Despite conceptual advancements, the review highlights a significant lack of field-tested evaluations and outlines a research agenda focused on empirical validation and workforce development.

## Key Takeaways
- LLM-enabled GeoAI introduces unique spatial privacy threats, such as passive location inference from mobility traces and uncertainty compounding across multimodal geospatial inputs, which standard AI ethics frameworks fail to adequately address.
- The review catalogs eight recurring governance issues spanning data provenance, algorithmic bias amplified by spatial autocorrelation and the modifiable areal unit problem, LLM hallucinations, explainability deficits, policy gaps, and workforce development needs.
- A proposed governance-aware architecture maps each identified risk to auditable artifacts and enforceable controls throughout the geospatial data lifecycle, demonstrated through a flood-response routing scenario, though current solutions remain largely conceptual without robust empirical validation.

## Context
As large language models increasingly power autonomous geographic information systems, the intersection of generative AI and spatial analysis creates novel governance challenges that traditional technology ethics frameworks overlook. This paper situates itself within the rapidly evolving GeoAI landscape, addressing how spatial data characteristics fundamentally alter risk profiles compared to standard natural language processing applications. By synthesizing existing literature on spatial mechanisms and LLM vulnerabilities, it provides a foundational framework for aligning geospatial AI development with ethical and regulatory standards.

## Implications
Practitioners and policymakers must recognize that spatial data introduces structural risks like scale effects and autocorrelation that can amplify biases and privacy violations in ways general AI does not predict. The proposed governance architecture offers a practical roadmap for embedding enforceable controls and audit trails into autonomous GIS workflows, though immediate adoption requires further empirical testing. Ultimately, bridging the gap between conceptual frameworks and field-tested evaluations will be critical for developing spatially aware interpretability tools and training workforces capable of managing these emerging risks.
