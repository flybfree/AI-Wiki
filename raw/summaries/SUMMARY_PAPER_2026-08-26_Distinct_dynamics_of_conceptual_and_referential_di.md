## Summary
The paper investigates how conceptual and referential disruptions in short narratives affect human self‑paced reading and the processing of large language models. It finds that humans experience a strong, localized cost for conceptual disruption that peaks early and then fades, whereas referential disruption yields weaker, more gradual effects modulated by sentence boundaries. In contrast, language models show immediate disruption signals but differ in representation dynamics: referential distortion causes larger initial displacement while both follow power‑law decay.

## Key Takeaways
- Conceptual disruptions create a pronounced, early processing cost that diminishes quickly after the distorted word, indicating tightly coupled integration of meaning.
- Referential disruptions produce weaker, slower‑decaying effects and are influenced by sentence boundaries, reflecting broader discourse maintenance.
- Language models generate immediate surprisal patterns mirroring humans for conceptual disruption but show distinct representational displacement, especially for referential distortion.

## Context
This work bridges psycholinguistics and large language model research, highlighting that meaning processing is not uniform across human cognition and AI architectures. By isolating conceptual versus referential components, the study clarifies how different semantic layers are encoded in neural representations.

## Implications
For developers of generative models, recognizing these distinct dynamics can inform more efficient training strategies and better alignment with human comprehension. Practitioners may leverage the localized cost of conceptual errors to design interventions that reduce misinterpretation in dialogue systems.
