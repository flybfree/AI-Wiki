## Summary
The paper proposes a normative framework to define when reliance on generative AI is epistemically warranted rather than merely behaviorally induced, arguing that existing accuracy and fairness metrics are insufficient for high‑stakes contexts. It identifies three jointly necessary conditions—epistemic humility, epistemic access, and resistance to epistemic injustice—as the core of trustworthiness.

## Key Takeaways
- Epistemic humility demands that AI systems explicitly acknowledge their competence limits, which is essential because overconfident outputs can mislead users in critical reasoning tasks.
- Epistemic access requires mechanisms that let users inspect, question, and contest AI outputs within the relevant context, ensuring they remain active participants rather than passive recipients.
- Resistance to epistemic injustice obliges systems to treat users as legitimate knowledge holders, preventing marginalisation of their expertise and experience.

## Context
Generative AI is now embedded in legal, medical, and hiring workflows where decisions have real consequences. Traditional safety assessments focus on output correctness or fairness alone, overlooking the epistemic dynamics that shape user belief formation.

## Implications
Designing systems around epistemically warranted reliance will shift evaluation priorities from pure performance to how responsibly users can trust and integrate AI insights. Practitioners must embed humility cues, transparent contestability tools, and inclusive design practices to mitigate hidden harms in high‑stakes environments.
