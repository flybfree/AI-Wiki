## Summary
The paper investigates how frequency and surprisal predict lexical acquisition in Spanish across development. It finds that cumulative exposure (frequency) strongly predicts age of acquisition while surprisal adds little to this prediction. In adult reading, surprisal remains a robust predictor of fixation duration beyond frequency.

## Key Takeaways
- Frequency, reflecting cumulative exposure, is the primary driver of when children acquire individual Spanish nouns, with a strong negative correlation between frequency and age of acquisition.
- Surprisal, representing contextual predictability, contributes little to early lexical acquisition but remains significant for adult reading performance.
- The association between surprisal and behavior is stronger in reading than in acquisition, indicating different roles across the language trajectory.

## Context
This study bridges developmental linguistics with natural language processing by applying frequency and surprisal metrics from child-directed speech to model age of acquisition. It also uses advanced language models to assess how surprise influences adult eye movements, highlighting a gap between linguistic theory and computational modeling.

## Implications
For AI researchers, the findings caution against using surprisal alone as a proxy for human learning processes. Practitioners should consider cumulative exposure when designing language acquisition datasets or training objectives. The results also suggest that frequency remains a more reliable indicator of early lexical representation formation than surprise.
