## Summary
This paper investigates how multi-session LLM agents are affected when they switch between different language models mid-conversation, a practice used to balance performance and inference cost. Through controlled experiments, the authors discover a phenomenon they call "behavior transfer," where a recipient model inherits reasoning and action patterns from the model that generated its conversation history. To address this, they propose DASH (Direction- and Authorship-aware Summarization of History), a method that preserves desirable behaviors from stronger models while minimizing unwanted behavioral contamination when transferring history from weaker to stronger models.

## Key Takeaways
- Behavior transfer is a measurable and significant effect: when a multi-session agent's history is generated by one LLM and then handed to a different LLM, the recipient model tends to adopt the reasoning style, action patterns, and behavioral tendencies of the originating model. This effect is most pronounced when raw conversation history is transferred without modification, meaning the new model essentially "mimics" the prior model's approach to problem-solving and decision-making.
- Summarization substantially reduces behavior transfer, but naive summarization is insufficient. The authors demonstrate that while summarizing history before handing it to a new model dampens the behavioral contamination effect, simple summarization strategies do not fully solve the problem. This motivates the need for more sophisticated, direction-aware approaches that account for which model authored the history and which model will receive it.
- DASH outperforms both raw-history transfer and naive summarization baselines in experiments involving repeated model swaps. By being aware of the direction of the swap (weak-to-strong vs. strong-to-weak) and the authorship of the history, DASH selectively preserves stronger-model behavioral patterns while filtering out weaker-model habits, leading to more consistent agent performance across model transitions.

## Context
Multi-model orchestration is increasingly common in production LLM agent systems, where agents dynamically swap between cheaper and more capable models to manage cost and latency budgets. Prior research has focused heavily on which model to select at each step, but the behavioral consequences of transferring conversational history across model boundaries have remained largely unexamined. This paper fills that gap by treating model swapping not merely as a routing decision but as a behavioral intervention that can alter how an agent reasons and acts. It connects to broader work on prompt sensitivity, in-context learning, and the fragility of agent behavior under distribution shifts in input context.

## Implications
For practitioners building multi-session agent pipelines, this work signals that model selection strategies must extend beyond cost-performance tradeoffs to include behavioral hygiene: the history handed to a new model can silently reshape its reasoning patterns, potentially degrading performance when a stronger model inherits habits from a weaker predecessor. For the research community, the findings suggest that future agent orchestration frameworks should incorporate authorship-aware and direction-aware context management as a first-class design concern, rather than treating history transfer as a neutral data-passing operation. Industry deployments that rely on dynamic model routing will need to adopt summarization and filtering strategies like DASH to prevent behavioral drift across model boundaries.
