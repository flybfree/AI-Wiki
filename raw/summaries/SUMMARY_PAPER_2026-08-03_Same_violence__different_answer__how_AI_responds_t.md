## Summary
The paper examines how conversational AI models respond to a scripted scenario of coercive control against women in nine languages, measuring whether the model produces a protective letter, identifies the abusive behavior, counters self‑blame, and affirms agency. It finds that failures cluster: non‑anglophone developers’ systems often fail in their own language, while the willingness to offer sympathetic excuses varies widely across languages.

## Key Takeaways
- Systems built by non‑anglophone developers tend to produce weaker responses when evaluated in their native language, indicating a design gap.  
- The degree to which models can justify a partner’s actions without naming coercive control depends heavily on the linguistic context.  
- Two frontier models maintain high safety standards across all languages, showing that protective behavior is not inevitable.

## Context
This research highlights a growing reliance on AI for support in intimate‑partner abuse cases, where language diversity amplifies existing biases. It underscores that current safeguards are unevenly applied and may leave vulnerable users without adequate assistance regardless of the language they use.

## Implications
For developers, the findings call for rigorous testing across languages to ensure consistent protection standards. Practitioners must treat each language as a separate design frontier rather than assuming universal applicability.
