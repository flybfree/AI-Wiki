## Summary
The paper investigates epistemic transfer—the ability of users to maintain verification skills after using an AI tool—by distinguishing it from other effects and introducing metrics ETE and TRC. It proposes a protocol combining answer-first and evidence-first AI conditions with active-practice controls, delayed tests on held-out claims, behavioral measures, and analyses at participant and item levels.

## Key Takeaways
- The Epistemic Transfer Effect (ETE) quantifies the drop in unassisted performance after tool use compared to a no-tool baseline.  
- Tool-Removal Cost (TRC) captures the immediate decline in accuracy when the AI is taken away, revealing short‑term reliance.  
- Combining ETE and TRC creates a diagnostic space that separates capability building, tool advantage, epistemic inertness, and verification on loan.

## Context
Current AI assistance research focuses on real‑time performance while the tool is active, overlooking how tools shape independent judgment later. This work addresses that gap by measuring post‑assistance learning outcomes in naturalistic settings.

## Implications
For developers, the protocol offers a standardized way to evaluate whether an AI truly builds skills or merely provides temporary help. Practitioners can use ETE and TRC to decide which interventions are worth deploying when independent verification matters.
