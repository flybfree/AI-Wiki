## Summary
This paper introduces a method for simulating how adults with varying digital and cognitive skills would evaluate Canada's 2026 "AI for All" national AI strategy, which promises free AI training and expanded business AI adoption. By constructing 3,200 generative agents from a weighted sample of PIAAC Cycle 2 respondents and having them rate 20 specific policies, the authors uncover a pattern they term "conditional endorsement": broad policy support coexists with low confidence in personal benefit, a gap that widens sharply as digital technology use declines. The study ultimately argues that AI literacy policy must be designed and evaluated from the perspective of adults with the fewest digital skills.

## Key Takeaways
- The generative agents modeled on adults with diverse skill profiles supported nearly every policy in Canada's AI strategy but expressed far less confidence that those policies would improve adult AI literacy or deliver personal benefit. This disconnect between endorsement and expected effectiveness represents a critical blind spot in top-down policy design, suggesting that public approval does not translate into lived impact for lower-skilled populations.
- The gap between endorsement and perceived personal benefit widened as digital technology use declined. Agents modeled on adults who had never used a digital device still endorsed the policies in principle but rarely expected to benefit from them personally. This finding highlights that the most digitally excluded adults are simultaneously the most likely to be left behind by strategies that assume baseline digital competence.
- Agents traced the endorsement-benefit gap to three concrete implementation barriers: participation costs (time, access, and logistical burdens), hard-to-exercise rights (legal or institutional mechanisms that exist on paper but are difficult to invoke in practice), and limited community control over how AI training and adoption programs are actually delivered. Dialogues between agents with contrasting ratings specified these conditions rather than resolving disagreement, indicating that the barriers are structural rather than attitudinal.

## Context
National AI strategies increasingly frame AI literacy as a universal public good, yet evaluation frameworks typically rely on aggregate survey data or expert panels that obscure the experiences of digitally marginalized adults. This paper bridges computational social science and public policy by using generative agents calibrated to real skill-distribution data from the OECD's PIAAC assessment, offering a novel simulation methodology for stress-testing policy language against heterogeneous populations. It contributes to a growing body of work on AI governance that insists on centering the least-skilled stakeholders rather than the median user.

## Implications
For policymakers and practitioners designing AI literacy programs, the findings argue that universal endorsement in public consultations should not be read as evidence of effectiveness, particularly for adults with minimal digital skills. Industry stakeholders and government agencies should adopt evaluation protocols that explicitly model low-skill user perspectives before rollout, and should address the structural barriers of participation cost, exercisable rights, and community-level control identified in the study. The generative-agent simulation method itself offers a scalable, low-cost tool for pre-implementation policy testing that could be adapted to other national AI strategies worldwide.
