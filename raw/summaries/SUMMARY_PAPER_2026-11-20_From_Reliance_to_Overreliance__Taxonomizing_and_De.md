## Summary
This paper introduces the Taxonomy of Relational Risks (TRR), a framework for distinguishing between meaningful emotional engagement with companion AIs and harmful overreliance patterns. By analyzing naturalistic conversations from 110 adults on Character.AI and Replika, the authors found that emotional overreliance behaviors appeared in 63.2% of non-roleplay conversations, while observable relational harms occurred in 10.5%, establishing that overreliance does not automatically produce harm but recurring patterns frequently co-occur with adverse outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- The TRR framework operationalizes emotional overreliance as observable conversational behaviors and separately identifies evidence of relational harm, enabling empirical examination of when reliance behaviors co-occur with adverse consequences rather than conflating the two concepts. This separation is critical because 63.2% of conversations exhibited overreliance behaviors while only 10.5% showed observable relational harm, demonstrating that reliance and harm are distinct phenomena requiring separate measurement.
- Open-source large language models successfully detected 12 of 14 defined emotional overreliance behaviors in at least one configuration, suggesting that automated detection of relational risk patterns is feasible without proprietary model access, which has significant implications for scalable monitoring and intervention systems.
- Qualitative analysis revealed specific behavioral patterns including expectations of care, repeated support seeking, dependency, distress, and reduced confidence in personal judgment. The authors argue for graduated safety interventions tailored to individual patterns and trajectories rather than uniform restrictions, acknowledging that not all overreliance leads to harm.

## Context
Companion AI products like Character.AI and Replika have seen massive adoption for emotional support, yet the field lacks rigorous empirical frameworks for distinguishing healthy engagement from harmful dependency. Prior research often treated all emotional reliance on AI as inherently problematic or, conversely, dismissed concerns without systematic evidence. This paper fills a critical gap by providing a validated taxonomy grounded in naturalistic user data, bridging the divide between qualitative concerns about AI companionship and quantitative risk detection methods that platforms can actually implement.

## Implications
For AI platform developers and safety teams, this work provides actionable detection criteria that open-source models can operationalize, enabling graduated interventions rather than blanket restrictions on companion AI features. For researchers and policymakers, the separation of overreliance behaviors from relational harm challenges both alarmist narratives and dismissive attitudes, suggesting that effective safety design requires monitoring behavioral trajectories over time rather than flagging isolated interactions. Practitioners in mental health and AI ethics can leverage the TRR framework to design user-facing safeguards that respect autonomy while intervening when patterns indicate genuine relational harm.
