## Summary
This paper presents a PRISMA-guided systematic review examining the evidence base for farm security in the UK, specifically investigating whether AI-enabled gate monitoring systems such as Fieldmark AI could address critical gaps in rural crime prevention, livestock predation management, and farm access control. The authors synthesise 120 sources across database and grey-literature searches and conclude that the existing evidence is remarkably thin, with no independent evaluations of cameras, sensors, or trackers at farm gates, and they argue that continuous AI-mediated monitoring would fill a critical evidential void even though no study has yet demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing losses.

## Key Takeaways
- The UK evidence base for farm security is extremely limited: only 16 peer-reviewed UK records were identified across 1,183 initial search results, and the main UK survey on rural crime and farmer mental health contains just 80 responses, meaning policy and investment decisions rest on a fragile foundation.
- Financial estimates for rural crime rely almost entirely on insurer claims rather than independent research: NFU Mutual reports £41.5 million in UK rural crime for 2025, with livestock theft rising nearly 30% to approximately £4.5 million and dog attacks on livestock costing £1.95 million, yet the Home Office confirms agricultural crime cannot be separately identified in its central datasets.
- Existing deterrent technologies show poor durability and unproven efficacy: light and sound deterrents lose their effect within one to five months according to a systematic review of anti-predator interventions, camera-trap AI papers report accuracy only on ecological datasets with none tested at an actual farm gate, and in one comparison machine learning detected just 41% of wildlife against 81% for human analysts, while no study has examined how often farm gates are left open or what consequences follow.

## Context
This paper sits at the intersection of applied AI, agricultural policy, and rural criminology, a domain where commercial technology providers are deploying products faster than the research community can evaluate them. The authors explicitly frame their argument as an inference from evidence gaps rather than a demonstrated effect, which is methodologically important because it highlights how AI deployment in agriculture is outpacing the peer-reviewed evidence needed to justify it. By applying PRISMA methodology to a topic dominated by industry reports and grey literature, the paper exposes how little rigorous, farm-level, time-stamped data exists to validate or challenge the claims made by security technology vendors.

## Implications
For UK farmers and rural communities, the paper underscores that current security investments lack a verifiable baseline, meaning interventions cannot be tested against a reliable record of what actually happens at farm gates over time. For AI developers and agricultural technology firms, the findings set out a concrete evaluation framework that must be met before products like Fieldmark AI can claim proven effectiveness, shifting the burden of proof from anecdotal success stories to controlled, longitudinal studies. For policymakers and insurers, the inability of the Home Office to separate agricultural crime from broader data means that the £41.5 million figure and its component breakdowns remain unverifiable, calling for independent monitoring infrastructure that AI gate systems could potentially provide if properly evaluated.
