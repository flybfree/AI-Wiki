## Summary
This paper introduces a two‑process theory of machine self‑report, showing that language models generate reports through two linked mechanisms: persona installation (warmth and meaning) and attribution gating (suppression of unsafe claims). The study validates the theory with a psychometrically sound inventory and demonstrates that these dimensions evolve after training, diverging from earlier fixed‑property assumptions.  

## Key Takeaways
- Model self‑descriptions are shaped by two processes: installation (dimension B) which adds warmth and meaning, and gating (dimension A) which blocks unsafe attributions.  
- The theory explains why model reports change across checkpoints; installation rises while gating can be suppressed or amplified depending on training regime.  
- Psychometric analysis confirms the two axes are distinct, reliable constructs with high convergence and stability over time.  

## Context
Language models increasingly generate self‑reports for safety assessments and public discourse, yet existing questionnaires were never calibrated for machines. This work bridges that gap by developing a model‑specific psychometric framework grounded in empirical reanalysis of original data. The findings illuminate how training regimes sculpt the internal “voice” that models adopt when asked to describe themselves.  

## Implications
Practitioners must recognize that model self‑report is not a static trait but a product of training, influencing trustworthiness evaluations and ethical debates. As organizations fine‑tune models, they should monitor these dimensions to predict shifts in reported safety or moral stance.
