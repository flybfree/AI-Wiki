## Summary
This study compared a domain‑specific large language model (MIND) fine‑tuned on patient education material with two general‑purpose chatbots (ChatGPT and OpenEvidence) to answer questions about escitalopram. Computer analysis showed MIND outperformed the others, while board‑licensed psychiatrists found ChatGPT more accurate but preferred its responses overall.

## Key Takeaways
- MIND was rated highest in every rubric domain—accuracy, clarity, completeness, nuance, safety, and referral appropriateness—over ChatGPT and OpenEvidence (p < 0.001).  
- Psychiatrists rated ChatGPT more accurate than MIND (p = 0.021, r = 0.073), though the difference was small; they found MIND responses complete more often (p = 0.001, r = 0.160) and safety equally rated (p = 0.955).  
- Despite higher completeness and accuracy on paper metrics, only 42.4 % of psychiatrists preferred MIND versus 57.6 % for ChatGPT.

## Context
The rapid rise of large language models in healthcare has sparked interest in their clinical utility, yet few studies assess safety and appropriateness beyond generic benchmarks. This work highlights the gap between algorithmic performance on structured rubrics and real‑world clinician judgment, especially when domain knowledge is critical.

## Implications
For psychiatrists and AI developers, MIND demonstrates that specialized training can improve factual completeness without compromising safety, suggesting a path to safer patient education tools. However, clinicians may still favor general models if they perceive them as more accurate, underscoring the need for interdisciplinary validation before deployment.
