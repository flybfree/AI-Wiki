## Summary
The paper investigates whether large language models that mimic human moral judgments also reflect the underlying motive attributions people assign to those same judgments. It finds that while LLMs reproduce the average ranking of a physician’s moral character, they often downplay competitive motives and overemphasize helpfulness for whistleblowers. The agreement in ratings can hide differences in how motives are linked to moral assessments.

## Key Takeaways
- LLMs align with human rankings of moral character but attribute different motives: whistleblowers are seen as more helpful and less self‑interested than those who stay silent, even though the models do not show stronger competitive motives.  
- The similarity in average ratings across models does not guarantee that relationships between moral judgments and motive attributions are consistent with human psychology.  
- Prompt variations that reproduce narratives or demographics change model outputs only slightly, suggesting that context effects may be muted when only overall agreement is measured.

## Context
This research highlights a gap between surface‑level agreement in AI outputs and deeper psychological patterns of motivation that drive moral reasoning. As LLMs become proxies for human participants in experiments, their limited ability to capture nuanced motive attribution poses challenges for interpreting experimental results. The findings underscore the need for more psychologically informed evaluation methods beyond simple average scores.

## Implications
For researchers using LLMs as simulated humans, relying solely on overall agreement may lead to misinterpretations of underlying motives and relationships among judgments. Practitioners should develop response patterns that reflect specific psychological constructs rather than just mean values. This shift could improve the validity of AI‑based studies and guide more realistic applications in ethics and social science.
