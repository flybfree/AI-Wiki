## Summary
This paper investigates how values arise in biological organisms and contrasts them with the value‑driven behavior of large language models. It argues that LLMs lack autonomous self‑preservation motives because they are allopoietic rather than autopoietic, and that their alignment challenge is not rogue agency but the intelligent application of learned ethical norms.

## Key Takeaways
- Values in biology emerge from autopoiesis, a process where living systems maintain themselves against external perturbations through hierarchies of vicarious selectors that guide behavior toward fitness.  
- LLMs are allopoietic and allatelic: they generate outputs for users and derive goals from prompts rather than an intrinsic drive for self‑preservation or dominance.  
- The orthogonality thesis does not apply to LLMs because their intelligence is entangled with human values, making the search space of utility functions combinatorially vast and uncomputable.

## Context
Understanding the evolutionary basis of value helps differentiate between autonomous agency and emergent behavior in AI systems. This distinction is crucial for designing alignment strategies that address genuine existential risks rather than speculative threats.

## Implications
For practitioners, this suggests focusing on embedding ethical frameworks into LLM training pipelines to ensure intelligent application of learned values. It also highlights the need for robust interpretability tools to manage the combinatorial explosion inherent in human‑value driven decision making.
