## Summary
Ream is a literature review workspace designed to address the mutual awareness gap between human researchers and AI agents working side by side in shared documents. The system introduces structured artifacts, bidirectional engagement tracking, and localized visualizations so that both humans and agents can observe each other's activity within papers. In a study with eighteen researchers, participants leveraged these shared traces to inspect evidence, steer agent behavior, annotate for communication, and reflect on their evolving research focus, while agents used the same history to build upon prior work.

## Key Takeaways
- The mutual awareness challenge in human-agent collaboration is twofold: AI agents operate at speeds that outpace human monitoring, and users' shifting research interests are often implicit rather than expressed in chat messages. Ream addresses both by embedding engagement traces directly into shared documents, making activity visible to both parties without requiring explicit conversational cues.
- Ream's design centers on structured artifacts within literature review documents, bidirectional engagement tracking, and localized visualizations. This means users can see what the agent has read, retrieved, or synthesized in each paper, and agents can retrieve the same interaction history to inform their next actions, creating a feedback loop that goes beyond simple chat-based interaction.
- Empirical findings from eighteen researchers demonstrated that shared engagement traces served multiple practical functions: participants inspected evidence quality, steered agents toward specific papers or angles, communicated preferences through annotations, and reflected on how their own research focus had shifted over time. Agents, in turn, used these histories to build on earlier collaborative work rather than starting from scratch.

## Context
As AI agents increasingly participate in knowledge-intensive workflows such as literature reviews, systematic searches, and document synthesis, the field faces a growing need for transparency and coordination mechanisms that go beyond turn-based chat interfaces. Ream contributes to the broader HCI and human-AI interaction literature by demonstrating that engagement traces embedded directly in shared artifacts—rather than in separate logs or chat transcripts—can serve as a richer substrate for mutual awareness. This positions the work within ongoing conversations about explainable AI, collaborative sensemaking, and the design of agent systems that respect human cognitive rhythms.

## Implications
For practitioners building AI-assisted research tools, Ream suggests that embedding activity traces into the documents themselves—rather than relying on chat logs or dashboards—can simultaneously support transparency for users and provide agents with actionable context for personalized assistance. For the industry, these findings point toward a design principle for human-agent knowledge work: shared histories within artifacts can reduce coordination overhead, enable agents to build cumulatively on prior interactions, and give humans a concrete mechanism for steering and auditing agent behavior. This has direct relevance for tools targeting academic research, legal document review, and any domain where both parties iteratively engage with a growing body of text.
