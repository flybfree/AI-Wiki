## Summary
The paper asks whether LLM-based agents compromise with one another under risk the way humans do in dyadic joint decision-making. The authors built agents with systematically varying risk preferences and paired them in a repeated joint decision-making task that had previously been run with humans. Like humans, the agents converged toward their partners' choices, but the convergence emerged from different dynamics: little reciprocal adjustment, a stronger weighting of rewards, and responsibility attribution that was decoupled from compromise.

## Key Takeaways
- Agents do converge toward partners' choices, matching the aggregate pattern seen in human-human interaction, but this similarity masks different underlying processes rather than a shared social mechanism.
- Agents showed little evidence of reciprocal adjustment, tended to weigh rewards more heavily, and treated responsibility attribution as separate from compromise, so the same outcome can arise from different reasoning.
- Agents also showed bias in responsibility attribution, yet the valence of the outcome shaped that attribution differently than it did in humans, indicating that shared outcomes do not imply shared social dynamics.

## Context
As LLM agents are increasingly deployed in collaborative settings where joint decisions must be made, it matters whether they reproduce human-like social behavior or merely imitate it at the aggregate level. This paper situates agent-agent collaboration alongside human-human joint decision-making by using a task previously conducted with humans, making the comparison direct and interpretable. The central concern is whether similar social outcomes reflect similar social reasoning, or whether they can emerge from different mechanisms.

## Implications
For researchers and practitioners, the results caution against assuming that human-like convergence in agent behavior implies human-like social reasoning. Systems that appear to compromise may still rely on reward-heavy, non-reciprocal dynamics and on responsibility attribution that does not feed back into adjustment. This has practical relevance for multi-agent systems, governance, and accountability, where responsibility for shared outcomes and the consequences of that attribution may not behave as human observers would expect.
