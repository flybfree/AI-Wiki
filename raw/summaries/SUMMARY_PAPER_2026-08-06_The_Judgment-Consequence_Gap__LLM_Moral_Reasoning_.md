## Summary
This paper investigates how large language models reason about moral responsibility in healthcare scenarios involving scarce resources, revealing a systematic gap between their judgment of patient culpability and the allocation decisions they make. While LLMs often agree with humans that patients bear responsibility for health‑harming behaviors, they frequently override this judgment by assigning care at random rather than favoring the less‑culpable patient. The study also shows that LLMs place more weight on information access, diminishing responsibility assessments when health‑risk knowledge is unavailable.

## Key Takeaways
- LLMs agree with humans that patients are responsible for harmful behaviors but then allocate scarce resources randomly instead of prioritizing the less‑culpable patient.  
- The judgment‑consequence gap persists across different model families and capability levels, indicating a consistent moral framework divergence.  
- When health‑risk information is missing, LLMs reduce responsibility judgments, reflecting an emphasis on access to data over ethical reasoning.

## Context
The integration of AI into high‑stakes domains such as healthcare raises urgent questions about how algorithmic decision‑making aligns with human values and ethical norms. This research contributes to the broader discourse by empirically demonstrating that current LLMs do not merely reflect human moral intuitions but instead operate under a distinct, often contradictory, reasoning structure.

## Implications
These findings warn developers and clinicians that deploying LLMs for resource allocation could exacerbate inequities if their outputs are perceived as arbitrary rather than ethically grounded. The gap identified suggests a need for revised evaluation metrics and safeguards to ensure AI decisions align with human moral standards in critical medical contexts.
