## Summary
This paper investigates how much reasoning effort an AI code reviewer needs to correctly reject plausible-but-wrong patches generated by coding agents. Using Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Haiku 5.5 at low, medium, and high effort settings, the authors evaluated 90 agent-written Django patches from SWE-bench Verified against strengthened SWE-ABS tests. The central finding is that while higher effort consistently reduces false acceptances across all three models, no effort setting constitutes a strong gate—each accepted at least 45% of wrong patches—and for a majority of the wrong patches the underlying defect may not be detectable through the failing test alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Across all three Claude models, high effort produced measurably fewer false acceptances than low effort (false success rates of .45 vs .67 for Opus, .49 vs .65 for Sonnet, .51 vs .78 for Haiku), confirming that reasoning effort does improve reviewer diligence, with statistically supported differences for Opus and Haiku.
- For Claude Opus 5.5 specifically, medium effort was statistically non-inferior to high effort within a 15 percentage-point margin (difference .018, 95% CI [-.065, .108]) while costing 20% less, falling short of the preregistered 30% cost-reduction threshold; for Sonnet and Haiku, medium effort saved 39% and 51% respectively but could not be shown non-inferior to high effort.
- No reviewer configuration serves as a reliable gate: every model at every effort level accepted at least 45% of the 55 wrong patches, and for 32 of those 55 wrong patches the actual defect may not be something the failing test is designed to check, meaning neither the accuracy hypothesis nor the effort-scaling hypothesis holds universally when those patches are excluded.

## Context
As AI coding agents increasingly generate code at speeds that outpace human review capacity, teams are deploying AI reviewers as a scalable quality-control layer. This paper addresses a practical and financially consequential question: whether the configurable reasoning-effort knob in frontier models can be tuned to balance review accuracy against computational cost. By benchmarking against strengthened test suites rather than the original SWE-bench tests, the study probes whether AI reviewers can catch defects that pass surface-level validation, a scenario directly relevant to production software pipelines where agent-generated patches are merged with minimal human oversight.

## Implications
For practitioners deploying AI code review pipelines, the findings caution against assuming that any single effort setting provides a reliable correctness gate, since even high-effort configurations accept nearly half of wrong patches. The partial non-inferiority of medium effort for Opus suggests a viable cost-accuracy tradeoff for some model tiers, but the inability to generalize this to Sonnet or Haiku means teams must validate effort settings per model and per codebase. More broadly, the result that many wrong patches involve defects not targeted by the failing test implies that test-strengthening alone is insufficient and that AI reviewers may need complementary defect-detection strategies beyond test-based verification.
