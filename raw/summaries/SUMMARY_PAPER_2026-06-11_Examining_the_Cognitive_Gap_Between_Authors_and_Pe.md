## Summary
The study investigates the cognitive gap between authors and peer reviewers regarding the novelty of academic papers, using a dataset of 15,328 papers in Nature Communications from 2016 to 2021. It finds that promotional language by authors influences reviewer judgments only for moderately innovative works, while highly or lowly novel papers are unaffected.

## Key Takeaways
- Reviewers adopt a more comprehensive evaluation perspective than authors, focusing on result-oriented innovation rather than the breadth of novelty claims.  
- The effect of author promotion is strongest when a paper’s actual novelty lies in the middle range; high‑novelty or low‑novelty papers see little impact from promotional language.  
- Promotional intensity correlates with reviewer disagreement specifically for moderate‑innovation papers, indicating that reviewers are more sensitive to ambiguous claims.

## Context
This research matters within AI and scholarly communication because it highlights how self‑presentation can skew the assessment of intellectual contribution, a concern amplified by automated review systems that rely on textual cues. Understanding this gap helps improve fairness in peer‑review processes where novelty is a core quality metric.

## Implications
For researchers, the findings suggest tailoring promotional language to match the paper’s genuine level of innovation to avoid unnecessary reviewer friction. For institutions and AI‑driven review platforms, incorporating nuanced models that distinguish between high, moderate, and low novelty can lead to more equitable evaluations.
