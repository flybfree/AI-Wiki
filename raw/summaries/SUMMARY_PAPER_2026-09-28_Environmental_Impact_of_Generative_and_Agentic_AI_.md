## Summary
This paper analyzes the environmental footprint of generative and agentic AI systems across their full lifecycle, utilizing order-of-magnitude estimations to assess energy consumption, carbon emissions, water usage, and e-waste. The study reveals that inference phases can rival or exceed training energy over a model's lifetime, while agentic workflows significantly amplify energy demands through iterative tool calls. Furthermore, the authors identify critical gaps in current measurement practices regarding indirect water use and embodied carbon, advocating for standardized frameworks like SAFIA and mandatory disclosure policies to address these escalating sustainability challenges.

## Key Takeaways
- The authors introduce a comprehensive lifecycle taxonomy and the Sustainability Assessment Framework for AI Systems (SAFIA), which includes nine indicators to evaluate impacts across five dimensions and three emission scopes, enabling systematic comparisons between traditional, generative, and agentic AI models from hardware fabrication through end-of-life disposal.
- Analysis indicates that the energy required for inference can match or surpass training energy over a deployment lifetime, with agentic workflows potentially multiplying energy consumption by one to several orders of magnitude compared to single-pass inference due to repeated model and tool interactions.
- Indirect water usage and embodied carbon are found to be systematically underreported in current assessments, prompting recommendations for agent-aware energy measurement tools, lifecycle-based accounting standards, and mandatory disclosure requirements for large-scale AI training and deployment to ensure regulatory and industry practices keep pace with technological advancements.

## Context
As generative and agentic AI systems become increasingly integrated into critical infrastructure and consumer applications, their escalating computational demands pose significant risks to global sustainability goals and resource availability. This research addresses a growing knowledge gap by shifting focus from isolated training metrics to holistic lifecycle assessments, providing essential data for stakeholders concerned with the ecological costs of rapid AI scaling and the unique energy profiles introduced by autonomous agent behaviors.

## Implications
The findings necessitate immediate revisions to AI development practices and regulatory frameworks, urging cloud providers, hardware manufacturers, and developers to adopt lifecycle-based carbon and water accounting alongside mandatory disclosure protocols for large-scale models. Practitioners must also prioritize efficiency optimizations in agentic workflows and inference pipelines, while policymakers should implement standards that mandate agent-aware measurement tools to mitigate the unchecked environmental expansion of autonomous AI systems.
