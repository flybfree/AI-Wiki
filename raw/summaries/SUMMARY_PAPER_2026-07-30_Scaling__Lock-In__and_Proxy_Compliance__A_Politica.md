## Summary
The paper proposes a sequential political‑economy model that explains how AI vendors and deployers negotiate accountability at scale. It shows that vendors often settle for observable procurement floor compliance while leaving substantive mitigation below the social first best, leading to a proxy‑compliance equilibrium where harm persists despite formal checks.

## Key Takeaways
- Vendors may stop providing full auditability once they reach an observable procurement floor, resulting in mitigations that are insufficient to achieve the optimal outcome. 
- Deployers face switching costs that limit their ability to enforce changes after adoption, so enforcement relies on verifiable evidence rather than direct intervention. 
- Independent audit rights increase enforcement exposure directly, while portability and incident reporting create channels for regulators to monitor outcomes.

## Context
AI accountability is a central concern in the rapid deployment of large language models and other automated systems that affect society. This work situates these concerns within political‑economy theory, offering a framework that links vendor incentives with deployer constraints to predict real‑world compliance gaps.

## Implications
For regulators and industry practitioners, the paper suggests that standard documentation and evaluations alone cannot guarantee safe AI outcomes; they must be complemented by mechanisms that expose vendor behavior through independent audits. Understanding proxy‑compliance can guide policy design that aligns liability and monitoring incentives with actual harm reduction.
