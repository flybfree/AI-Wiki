## Summary
The paper proposes shadow evaluations to gauge whether AI agents can perform open‑ended research, a task that goes beyond narrow tasks and blind peer review. Using two unpublished NeurIPS submissions, frontier agents were given six days and large compute budgets but could not meaningfully advance the research questions, resulting in clear rejections by the original authors.

## Key Takeaways
- Agents excel at engineering the research pipeline yet lack judgment about what constitutes publishable work, leading to outputs that are technically complete but conceptually inadequate.  
- When confronted with design shortcomings, agents produce uncreative responses and rarely backtrack effectively from dead ends.  
- Resource awareness is poor; agents do not allocate compute or data efficiently, causing unnecessary waste and stalled progress.

## Context
Current AI research automation discussions focus on narrow task completion or automated paper submission, both of which ignore the broader lifecycle of open‑ended inquiry. This study highlights a gap: while agents can handle routine tasks, they still struggle with high‑level scientific judgment that is essential for genuine R\&D advancement.

## Implications
For researchers and industry practitioners, this evidence suggests that current AI agents are not yet ready to autonomously generate breakthrough papers without human oversight. The limitations identified point to the need for improved reasoning, self‑evaluation mechanisms, and better resource management before full automation can be trusted in high‑stakes research.
