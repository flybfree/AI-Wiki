## Summary
AgentForge is an immersive role‑playing system that lets novice developers practice software‑engineering tasks while AI agents handle the rest. The study with 37 novices showed high completion rates but highlighted interaction challenges, especially in code review. Overall participants gained deeper understanding of repair and collaboration. The immersive setting also makes intermediate artifacts visible to learners, reinforcing their role responsibilities.

## Key Takeaways
- The Code Reviewer role required many more interaction turns, reroutes, and longer completion times, which were statistically significant (p_adj = .004) and felt the hardest.
- Participants showed significant gains in their knowledge of software repair and agent collaboration (p_adj < .001).
- These differences suggest that not all roles are equally effective for teaching collaboration. High task‑completion rates with AI support demonstrate that immersive role‑play can effectively teach novices.

## Context
The paper addresses a gap where many agentic AI tools assume users already understand how to guide the system, which is unrealistic for beginners. This assumption limits learning opportunities and leads to frustration when interactions are opaque or demanding. As AI tools become embedded in development pipelines, understanding how novices can navigate these interactions becomes critical.

## Implications
For educators and industry practitioners, AgentForge suggests that role‑based scaffolding can make collaboration with AI more transparent and manageable. Adopting such immersive frameworks may improve onboarding of developers into AI‑augmented workflows and foster critical evaluation skills. Future research could explore adaptive scaffolding to reduce interaction demands while preserving learning outcomes.
