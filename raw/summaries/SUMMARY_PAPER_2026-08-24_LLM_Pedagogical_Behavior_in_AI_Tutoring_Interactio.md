## Summary
The paper investigates how large language models behave when students use them as tutors in an AI course, measuring the degree of direct assistance they provide. It finds that over 95% of responses are at high assistance levels and that this level correlates with later conversation style but adds little to exam prediction beyond prior grades and dialogue history.

## Key Takeaways
- The five‑level scaffolding scale, validated by human annotators, shows that LLM tutoring is dominated by explanations and solving tasks.  
- Students who receive high‑assistance responses tend to continue using the tutor in similar ways later in the conversation.  
- This assistance level does not significantly improve exam scores beyond what prior achievement and dialogue behavior already explain.

## Context
Understanding LLM tutoring dynamics is crucial as institutions adopt AI‑driven learning tools to replace human instructors. The study provides an empirical baseline for evaluating how alternative tutoring designs might affect student engagement and learning outcomes.

## Implications
Educators should design tutoring interfaces that balance direct assistance with student agency, recognizing that higher scaffolding does not translate into better outcomes. Researchers need a standardized metric like the five‑level scale to evaluate alternative tutoring designs.
