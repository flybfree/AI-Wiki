## Summary
The paper argues that large language models should be understood as algorithmic mediators within Mufwene’s ecological model of language evolution, shifting the locus of selection from speakers to distributional reweighting by the model. It proposes that LLMs can alter the relative frequencies of linguistic variants that human selectors encounter, but it does not predict inevitable convergence. The study suggests testable predictions about uptake, version effects, and possible social reversal.

## Key Takeaways
- Model mediation creates an algorithmic feature pool that reweights speaker‑accessible linguistic distributions before any human selection occurs.  
- Emerging model‑specific profiles may influence which variants become more or less salient to speakers, but this does not guarantee uniform adoption across languages.  
- Human social evaluation remains decisive; AI‑generated forms can be embraced, avoided, or remain marginal depending on cultural and evaluative contexts.

## Context
This work situates LLMs within the broader discourse on language evolution, highlighting how machine‑learned representations reshape the ecological dynamics traditionally dominated by human interaction. It underscores that while AI can amplify certain linguistic features, it does not replace the role of social judgment in determining which forms become conventionalized or discarded.

## Implications
For linguists and AI practitioners, this framework offers a testable lens to evaluate how model versions affect language uptake and whether algorithmic reweighting can lead to convergence. It also warns that without careful design, LLMs may reinforce existing biases rather than democratize linguistic diversity.
