## Summary
This paper investigates whether a strong statistical correlation between negative affect and emphatic vocabulary in interviews of public intellectuals is an artifact of keyword‑based measurement rather than genuine rhetorical stance. By comparing traditional keyword counting with zero‑shot LLM classification on the same diarized corpus, it shows that keyword scores overestimate certainty while LLM analysis reveals true hedging patterns.

## Key Takeaways
- The keyword lexicon produces a high Pearson correlation (r = 0.72–0.93) between negative affect and emphatic words, suggesting a systematic bias in measuring epistemic certainty.
- Zero‑shot semantic classification reduces this correlation to near zero or even negative values, indicating that the lexical pattern is not reflective of speaker psychology but of counting errors.
- Structural failures such as syntactic blindness, polysemy blindness, and categorical absence cause keyword counts to invert meaning (e.g., “never absolutely totally confident” being scored high‑certainty).

## Context
The study highlights a recurring problem in computational social science: reliance on simplistic lexical metrics can mask deeper rhetorical dynamics. As AI models become standard for zero‑shot classification, the contrast between human‑crafted keyword tools and neural classifiers underscores the need for more robust measurement frameworks.

## Implications
Researchers should treat keyword counts as indicators of lexical co‑occurrence tendencies rather than direct measures of epistemic certainty. Practitioners must adopt semantic models like LLMs to avoid systematic misinterpretations that could lead to false conclusions about speaker attitudes.
