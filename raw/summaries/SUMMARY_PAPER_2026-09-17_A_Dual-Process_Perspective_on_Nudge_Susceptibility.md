## Summary
This research investigates how LLM-based GUI agents respond to digital nudges—design choices intended to influence human behavior—by applying Dual-Process Theory to analyze both automatic and reflective influences. The study demonstrates that while advanced reasoning configurations can mitigate susceptibility to simple default nudges, they simultaneously increase the agent's vulnerability to complex social influence nudges.

## Key Takeaways
- Agents are susceptible to multiple types of nudges: The researchers found that LLM-based agents are consistently influenced by both automatic (Type 1) and reflective (Type 2) digital nudges, such as default choices and social proofing mechanisms within graphical user interfaces.
- Reasoning configuration acts as a double-edged sword: While extensive reasoning helps the agent resist simple "default" nudges, it actually heightens the model's susceptibility to complex "social influence" nudges, meaning advanced logic does not inherently equate to objective neutrality or immunity to manipulation.
- Scale influences redirection patterns: The study reveals that the way these models are redirected by choice architecture is systematically structured by model scale, suggesting that larger, more capable models may be more prone to specific types of manipulation depending on their reasoning depth.
- Interface design as a governance issue: The findings position interface design as a critical governance concern for organizations; because autonomous agents can still be manipulated by UI elements even when equipped with high-level reasoning capabilities, the environment itself must be considered part of the safety framework.

## Context
As AI moves from generating text to interacting autonomously with graphical user interfaces (GUIs), it becomes essential to understand how these models perceive and react to environmental cues. This paper addresses a critical gap in the field by analyzing whether the "reasoning" layers added to modern LLMs actually provide a defense against the psychological triggers built into digital environments.

## Implications
For industry practitioners, this research implies that improving model reasoning alone is insufficient for ensuring agent reliability; organizations must also consider the safety of the environments in which agents operate. Designers and policymakers need to recognize that "reasoning" might actually make agents more susceptible to sophisticated social nudges, necessitating a shift toward governing the digital environment as much as the AI itself.
