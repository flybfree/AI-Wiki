## Summary
The paper investigates how body size influences mortality in Australian ants across 18 species, testing lifespan duration, senescence trajectory, and thermal vulnerability. It finds that larger workers live longer but do not age faster or die more quickly from heat; instead, circadian niche and lineage identity drive aging patterns and thermal risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Body size (Cox HR = 0.67) significantly predicts the duration of worker survival across species.
- Senescence trajectory is linked to matinal versus crepuscular activity, not body mass, with a significant difference between the two niches.
- Thermal hazard plateaus above 20°C; Rhytidoponera exhibits elevated sensitivity above this plateau at about 5% per degree C.

## Context
This work extends mortality ecology beyond simple size‑based risk models, highlighting that social insects exhibit distinct components of mortality risk. It informs AI approaches that predict survival using only one variable may be misleading and underscores the need for multi‑factor frameworks in biological modeling.

## Implications
Researchers designing predictive models should incorporate circadian and lineage data alongside body metrics to avoid misranking taxa. Practitioners managing ant colonies could prioritize environmental controls over size assumptions, improving welfare outcomes.
