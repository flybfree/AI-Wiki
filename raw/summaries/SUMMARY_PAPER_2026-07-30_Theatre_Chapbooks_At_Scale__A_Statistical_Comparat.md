## Summary
The paper introduces a statistical method that measures the similarity of typefaces in printed historical books by using character prototypes derived from clustering and aligning automatically extracted images. The authors apply this technique to 17th‑century Spanish theatre chapbooks, discovering new printer attributions and revising previous ones. The statistical framework also provides a way to interpret the significance of computed typeface distances through an a contrario analysis.

## Key Takeaways
- The method defines a quantitative distance between any two typefaces based on aligned character prototypes.
- Human validation confirmed that the automatic comparisons can uncover previously unknown printer identities.
- The approach scales to large bibliographic collections beyond manual inspection limits.

## Context
This work demonstrates how clustering and image alignment can be used to generate comparable visual features for typographic analysis, a task traditionally limited by subjective expert judgment. In AI research it shows an application of unsupervised learning to cultural heritage data, bridging machine perception with philology. Such automated typographic clustering could inform future AI models that need to handle heterogeneous visual data without labeled examples.

## Implications
Practitioners in digital bibliography can automate attribution tasks that previously required extensive manual review. The scalability of the method suggests new possibilities for large‑scale typographic studies across historical corpora.
