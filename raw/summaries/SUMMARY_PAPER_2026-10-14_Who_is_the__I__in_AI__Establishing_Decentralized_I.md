## Summary
This paper addresses the critical gap in identity and accountability frameworks for autonomous AI agents operating in decentralized ecosystems. The authors propose a novel ABCD Framework that establishes decentralized AI identity through four orthogonal dimensions—Algorithmic Fingerprint, Behavioural Fingerprint, Cryptographic Identity, and Delegation—creating a formal model that distinguishes human users from AI agents while maintaining a unified identity calculus grounded in Self-sovereign identity principles.

## Key Takeaways
- The ABCD Framework provides a multi-layered approach to agent identification: the Algorithmic Fingerprint binds an agent's identity to its static model architecture and provenance, the Behavioural Fingerprint verifies dynamic performance metrics over time, the Cryptographic Identity anchors the agent through Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs), and the Delegation layer cryptographically binds agent actions to human accountability, collectively solving the "black box identity problem" in autonomous economic activities.
- The framework explicitly distinguishes between human users and AI agents by enabling distinct verification logic for each class while maintaining a unified identity calculus, acknowledging that human identity is traditionally anchored in biological attributes and government credentials whereas AI agents lack any standardized definition of selfhood.
- By orthogonalizing the four dimensions, the system can enforce granular trust policies ranging from simple authentication to complex liability assignment, creating a spectrum of trust enforcement mechanisms applicable to different operational contexts in decentralized AI ecosystems.

## Context
As autonomous AI agents proliferate across decentralized economic ecosystems, existing identity paradigms designed for human users become inadequate for establishing trust and accountability. The broader AI field currently lacks standardized mechanisms for defining agent selfhood, creating critical gaps in how systems verify, attribute, and hold accountable autonomous agents performing economic activities. This paper sits at the intersection of decentralized identity infrastructure, AI governance, and accountability engineering, responding to an urgent need as multi-agent systems become increasingly autonomous and economically active.

## Implications
For practitioners building decentralized AI systems, this framework offers a concrete architectural blueprint for implementing trust policies that scale from basic authentication to full liability assignment, enabling safer deployment of autonomous agents in economic transactions. For the industry at large, establishing standardized identity and accountability mechanisms for AI agents is a prerequisite for regulatory compliance, insurance frameworks, and the development of trustworthy multi-agent economies. The paper signals that the field must move beyond treating AI agents as anonymous tools toward treating them as identifiable entities with traceable provenance and delegated human responsibility.
