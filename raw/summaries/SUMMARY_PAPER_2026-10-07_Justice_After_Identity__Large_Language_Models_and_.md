## Summary
This paper by W. Russell Neuman explores whether large language models, which lack a singular social identity yet encode diverse human moral traditions in their parameters, can approximate John Rawls' "original position" as a computational "view from everywhere" for judging justice and fairness. The author presents comparative data examining human judgments, base model outputs, and frontier/fine-tuned model responses to classic moral dilemmas under varying identity constraints, ultimately speculating on whether humans would accept AI-mediated ethical guidance in polarized social contexts.

## Key Takeaways
- Rawls' "original position" thought experiment asks people to select principles of justice without knowing their future social identities, but critics have long argued that humans cannot meaningfully suspend lived experience, cultural inheritance, and self-interest shaped by social position. This limitation motivates the search for alternative frameworks for collective moral reasoning.
- LLMs present a novel possibility: they possess no singular class, race, gender, nationality, or biography, yet their parameters encode linguistic representations spanning a vast range of human identities and moral traditions. This structural feature could allow them to computationally incorporate identity diversity rather than excluding it, potentially approximating an integrative "view from everywhere."
- The paper explicitly rejects the notion that humanity will "hand over the keys" to fully agentic AI systems controlling distributive and procedural processes. Instead, it positions AI as a collaborative interlocutor interacting with individual and collective human judgment, particularly as societies face increasingly polarized disagreements about fairness and justice.

## Context
This work sits at the intersection of political philosophy, computational ethics, and AI alignment research, addressing a persistent challenge in the field: how to encode fairness in systems when human moral reasoning is inherently perspectival. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in governance, policy recommendation, and public discourse, understanding whether their outputs can serve as integrative moral arbiters—rather than reflecting the biases of their training data or fine-tuning—becomes a critical question for AI safety and democratic legitimacy.

## Implications
For practitioners building AI-assisted decision systems in law, public policy, and institutional governance, this research suggests a cautious but potentially productive role for LLMs as deliberative partners rather than autonomous arbiters of justice. Industry leaders and policymakers should consider how fine-tuning choices and identity-aware prompting may either narrow or broaden the moral perspectives an AI system can represent. The finding that humans may resist accepting AI-generated ethical advice also underscores the need for transparency, contestability, and human-in-the-loop design in any deployment of AI for fairness-critical applications.
