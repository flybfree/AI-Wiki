## Summary
The paper introduces a pragmatics‑inspired taxonomy to evaluate how large language models handle refusals in harmful interactions. By applying the framework to 16 modern LLMs across 14 harm categories, it finds that model refusals are generally explicit and carry strong moral judgments, with repair typically achieved by offering safer alternatives rather than through interpersonal facework.

## Key Takeaways
- The taxonomy shows that LLM refusals function as face‑threatening acts that can challenge the requester’s self‑image, highlighting their social impact.  
- Across diverse models and harm contexts, refusals are consistently explicit and morally evaluative, indicating a shared alignment with normative expectations.  
- Interactional repair is usually mediated by providing safer alternatives, which suggests a pragmatic strategy focused on mitigation rather than direct interpersonal facework.

## Context
In AI safety research, evaluating refusal strategies often stops at whether a model complies or not, overlooking the communicative and relational consequences of that compliance. This work bridges that gap by grounding evaluation in pragmatic theory, offering a nuanced view of how refusals affect user experience beyond mere safety alignment.

## Implications
For practitioners, this taxonomy can guide the design of LLM responses to be socially responsible and context‑aware, reducing potential shaming or provocation. It also prompts industry standards to consider interactional outcomes as part of model evaluation, fostering more humane AI interactions.
