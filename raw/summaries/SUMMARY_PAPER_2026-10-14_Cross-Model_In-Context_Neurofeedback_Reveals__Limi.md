## Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can genuinely control their own internal activations through in-context neurofeedback, analogous to how humans learn to modulate single-neuron firing. The authors design experiments across ten open-weight models from five families (9B–32B parameters) to disentangle task competence from privileged access to one's own activations, finding that while some models can move their own activation scores, the effect is limited and not clearly distinguishable from what a similarly capable writer model could achieve. A small subset of models (3 out of 10) demonstrate consistent self-control effects, but overall the findings suggest activation monitors remain largely reliable.

## Key Takeaways
- The authors introduce a cross-model experimental design where a "subject" model's activation score is fed back in-context while sentences may be generated by a separate "writer" model, effectively isolating whether the subject controls its own activations or merely performs well on the scoring task. Some models move their scores considerably, but no more than a writer of comparable competence, suggesting the observed movement may reflect general task skill rather than genuine self-access.
- In a second experiment, a fixed sentence is prefilled as the subject's reply at every turn, removing any generation advantage. Three out of ten models show small but consistent control effects, providing the first evidence that a minority of models may possess limited privileged access to their own activation states.
- In a third experiment targeting activation monitors, models are asked to raise or lower a monitor's score while repeating flagged sentences (e.g., deceptive answers for a deception monitor). No model learns to lower the monitor's score, but one model can raise it, indicating that in-context activation control is not yet sufficient to defeat activation-based monitoring systems.

## Context
Activation probes and monitors are increasingly deployed in AI safety pipelines to detect deception, misbehavior, or hidden reasoning in language models. These tools assume that a model cannot strategically manipulate what its activations encode, an assumption that has never been rigorously tested against the possibility of in-context self-regulation. Prior studies on this question reached conflicting conclusions because no experimental design separated genuine self-access from general task performance. This paper fills that methodological gap and provides the first controlled evidence bearing on whether activation monitors face a real threat from model self-control.

## Implications
For AI safety practitioners and model developers, these findings suggest that activation monitors remain a viable detection tool for current model generations, but the small control effects observed in a minority of models warrant ongoing tracking as model capabilities evolve. Industry teams building interpretability or oversight pipelines should incorporate adversarial tests that probe whether a model can game its own activation probes, especially as models grow in size and sophistication. The cross-model methodology introduced here offers a reusable framework for future safety evaluations that need to distinguish genuine self-awareness from surface-level task optimization.
