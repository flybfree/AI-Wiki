## Summary
This paper investigates the proliferation of the antithetical construction "rather than" in NLP research papers, comparing its frequency across ACL papers from 2019, ACL-style arXiv papers from 2026, and papers generated by GPT models. The authors find that the construction's rate has increased sevenfold from 2019 to 2026 and is even higher in AI-generated text, and they argue this trend is a side effect of post-training on pairwise preference data that rewards disavowal in isolated responses without accounting for its cumulative cost across a full document.

## Key Takeaways
- The "rather than" construction appears seven times more frequently in 2026 ACL-style arXiv papers than in 2019 ACL papers, and its rate is higher still in papers written by GPT models from the same titles and abstracts, suggesting a direct link between LLM-assisted writing and the rise of antithesis.
- Two blind annotators found almost no 2019 uses to be annoying, but roughly one in ten 2026 uses were rated as annoying; annotators rarely agreed on which specific uses were annoying, yet about half of all 2026 papers contained at least one use that annoyed each annotator, indicating the problem is widespread but inconsistently perceived.
- Annoying uses of "rather than" present the rejected alternative less favorably than legitimate uses, and preference-data raters and open reward models systematically favor the construction, while an instruction to be honest actually promotes its use, pointing to a structural incentive embedded in post-training pipelines.

## Context
As LLMs become routine tools for scientific writing, the stylistic fingerprints of their training objectives increasingly shape published research. This paper matters because it identifies a concrete, measurable degradation in academic prose quality that is not random but traceable to specific training mechanisms—namely, pairwise preference optimization that credits a single disavowal in one response without penalizing its repetition across a multi-paragraph text. The study bridges NLP methodology critique with the growing literature on LLM alignment side effects.

## Implications
For researchers and reviewers, the findings suggest that the antithetical padding now common in NLP papers is not a matter of authorial style but an artifact of model post-training, meaning peer review and editorial guidelines may need to explicitly address it. For practitioners building and fine-tuning language models, the work highlights a blind spot in preference-based training: reward models and human raters reward individual disavowals, creating a systemic incentive for models to overuse "rather than" constructions, which could be mitigated by document-level evaluation or training objectives that penalize repetitive rhetorical patterns.
