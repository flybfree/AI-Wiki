## Summary
The paper investigates how collective aggregation can arise from individual foraging behavior when agents optimize only for finding replenishable targets. It demonstrates that as visual range expands, agents transition from environment‑tuned searches to a scale‑agnostic collective strategy without any direct reward for grouping. The crossover is reproduced by a minimal analytical first‑passage model.

## Key Takeaways
- Agents achieve spatial aggregation purely through indirect reinforcement learning driven by resource availability rather than explicit social incentives.
- The transition from individual to collective search is triggered by increasing visual range, showing a sharp crossover that coincides with the onset of clustering.
- A simple first‑passage analytical framework explains this emergent behavior as a switch between two optimal foraging strategies.

## Context
This work contributes to AI research on decentralized coordination by illustrating how resource‑centric objectives can generate group dynamics without central control. It highlights the importance of indirect feedback loops in shaping collective outcomes, a theme relevant to multi‑agent systems and reinforcement learning.

## Implications
For practitioners designing autonomous agents, this suggests that emergent cooperation may be achieved through simple reward structures focused on local resources. The findings could inform the development of scalable swarm algorithms where agents self‑organize around shared goals without explicit group rewards.
