## Summary
The paper analyzes 94 AI value alignment research papers to reveal the implicit philosophical assumptions about human values that guide model training and evaluation. It finds that most work avoids defining values explicitly, instead relying on preference models that reduce complex cultural concepts to binary choices. The authors argue this creates a risk of closing alternative ways to contest or enact values in foundation models.

## Key Takeaways
- Researchers often treat human values as mere preferences, which can oversimplify culturally situated moral and aesthetic judgments.
- By replacing human annotators with synthetic data and autorater methods, the field may unintentionally lock out diverse value‑contesting perspectives.
- Making philosophical commitments about values explicit could open new avenues for contesting AI behavior beyond simple preference optimization.

## Context
AI safety research has increasingly focused on aligning large language models and multimodal foundation systems with human goals. Yet the literature rarely clarifies what “human values” entail, leading to vague or inconsistent alignment strategies that may not reflect real‑world ethical diversity.

## Implications
If value concepts remain implicit, future AI systems might systematically ignore marginalized viewpoints. Explicitly articulating these commitments could improve transparency and allow stakeholders to challenge model behavior in ways that go beyond preference‑based optimization.
