## Summary
This paper investigates how large language models generate reasoning in legal case forecasting using European Court of Human Rights cases, focusing on the performance of GPT‑5.4 under different prompting strategies. The study finds that while responses are structurally complete, their substantive analysis is shallow and human evaluators rate them far below ideal standards.

## Key Takeaways
- The model produces answers that are logically organized but lack depth, indicating superficial legal reasoning.
- Human and LLM‑based judges score the outputs inconsistently, showing limited alignment with expert annotations.
- Expertly crafted prompts yield richer reasoning yet do not improve prediction accuracy compared to simpler setups.

## Context
Legal case forecasting relies on models that must interpret complex statutes and precedents. Automated reasoning is increasingly expected in AI systems, but existing evaluations often conflate output completeness with legal insight, overlooking the nuanced judgment required in jurisprudence.

## Implications
Relying solely on automated LLM evaluation can mislead practitioners into believing their models perform legally meaningful tasks when they do not. Practitioners should incorporate human expertise to assess reasoning quality and avoid using task accuracy as a proxy for legal competence.
