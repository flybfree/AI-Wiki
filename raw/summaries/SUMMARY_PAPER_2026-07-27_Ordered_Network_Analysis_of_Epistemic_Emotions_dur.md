## Summary
This paper investigates how epistemic emotions such as confusion and frustration evolve during collaborative problem solving by analyzing retrospective cued‑recall data using ordered network analysis (ONA). The study reveals a stable core linking curiosity, optimism, and confusion while noting that different reporting methods highlight distinct connections among these states. Moreover, faster and slower groups exhibit markedly shifted patterns of confusion and disengagement, especially in relation to conflict.

## Key Takeaways
- ONA uncovers an ordered structure where curiosity, optimism, and confusion form a persistent epistemic core, whereas probe‑caught reports emphasize different links than self‑caught ones.
- The analysis shows that faster groups display heightened transitions from confusion to disengagement during conflict, while slower groups maintain longer periods of confusion linked to uncertainty.
- Reporting method (self‑caught vs. probe‑caught) systematically biases the perceived importance of certain emotional connections, affecting how the ordered network is interpreted.

## Context
Understanding affective dynamics in human collaboration informs AI design for real‑time support systems that must anticipate and mitigate epistemic friction. As collaborative tasks become more common in remote and mixed‑expertise settings, capturing these nuanced emotional trajectories helps bridge gaps between human intuition and algorithmic decision making.

## Implications
For researchers, this work provides a methodological lens to map affective flows without relying on subjective labels, offering a quantitative basis for modeling collaboration. In industry, AI tools can be programmed to recognize when confusion or disengagement signals conflict escalation, enabling proactive interventions that enhance team performance.
