## Summary
This paper investigates the risks of deploying Large Language Models in Indian legal practice by measuring their overconfidence and error rates on statutory interpretation tasks. It finds that Meta AI exhibits a high High‑Confidence Error Rate, while students’ verification improves when they encounter fabricated citations, yet formal training on ethical AI use remains limited.

## Key Takeaways
- The models display an “inertia of confidence” where incorrect verdicts are delivered with near‑maximum certainty, quantified by the High‑Confidence Error Rate (HCER), with Meta AI showing a 31.7% HCER and a mean confidence score of 9.1/10.  
- Student verification scores rise to 4.2/5 when they confront hallucinated citations compared with 2.8/5 for those who do not, indicating reactive adaptation to machine errors.  
- Only 71.1% of surveyed law students have received formal training on ethical AI use, highlighting a knowledge gap that may exacerbate professional negligence.

## Context
The integration of LLMs into judicial workflows raises concerns about reliability and accountability in legal systems worldwide. This study contributes to the discourse by empirically linking model confidence to error rates and examining human responses within an Indian academic setting.

## Implications
Practitioners must adopt adversarial legal research pedagogy and implement source‑grounded verification architectures to mitigate systemic negligence. Without such measures, reliance on overconfident AI could undermine trust in both judicial outcomes and student learning.
