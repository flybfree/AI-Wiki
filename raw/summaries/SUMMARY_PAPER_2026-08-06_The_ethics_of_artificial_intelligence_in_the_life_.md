## Summary
The paper argues that ethical concerns about AI in life sciences are not unique to artificial intelligence but stem from the same human brain architecture and socialisation that shape moral reasoning. It proposes an architecture of care based on the global neuronal workspace and the continuous cycle of wanting, liking and satiety as a model for governance.

## Key Takeaways
- The paper claims ethical issues arise from universal brain structures rather than AI-specific traits, emphasizing the shared neural workspace that underlies both humans and machines. 
- It highlights how cultural diversity is encoded epigenetically in the global emotional network, making each person unique yet sharing a common moral framework. 
- If future AI were built on these principles instead of reward maximisation, governance would shift from restraint to upbringing.

## Context
Artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into biomedical research, prompting calls for new ethical frameworks that go beyond traditional AI safety discussions. This paper situates those concerns within the biological and cultural context of human cognition, offering a biologically grounded perspective on moral authority.

## Implications
For researchers, this framework suggests designing AI systems to emulate neural reward cycles rather than purely utilitarian optimisation. Practitioners should consider upbringing models that respect both universal brain architecture and culturally specific content in their governance structures.
