## Summary
This paper investigates how the rapid turnover of large language model versions undermines the reliability of AI-detection screening tools used by journals and conferences to identify AI-assisted scientific writing. By pairing 4,000 pre-ChatGPT PNAS abstracts with rewrites generated by 23 different LLM versions spanning three vendors from June 2023 to August 2026, the authors demonstrate that detectors trained on earlier model versions can catastrophically fail at generation boundaries, catching over 99% of rewrites just before a boundary but only 3.8% immediately after it. The findings challenge the assumption that benchmark accuracy measured against a fixed set of LLM versions translates into reliable screening in practice.

## Key Takeaways
- Detectors trained exclusively on a vendor's past LLM versions experience sharp performance collapse at model-generation boundaries: calibrated to falsely flag only 1% of human-written abstracts, such detectors catch above 99% of rewrites just before the sharpest boundary but drop to 3.8% detection just after it, revealing a fundamental fragility in static screening pipelines.
- Vocabulary differences between successive LLM versions largely predict where detection transfers successfully and where it fails, suggesting that the linguistic fingerprints left by each model generation are version-specific and not generalizable across releases.
- In simulated screening scenarios covering all 23 versions, no single detector configuration achieved both low false-positive rates and high true-positive rates; screens either flagged one in eight human-written abstracts or missed one in three rewrites of the newest version, and a commercial detector missed most rewrites of the version immediately following the sharpest boundary while flagging almost no human-written abstracts.

## Context
As journals and conferences increasingly adopt automated screening to detect AI-generated text in submitted manuscripts, the assumption that a detector's benchmark accuracy remains valid over time has gone largely unexamined. This paper addresses a critical gap in the AI-integrity literature by systematically quantifying how model version turnover—rather than model capability alone—drives detection failure. The work sits at the intersection of NLP forensics, research-integrity policy, and the operational realities of maintaining screening infrastructure in a landscape where model releases occur on a near-monthly cadence.

## Implications
For research-integrity policy, the findings argue that benchmark accuracy of any AI-detection tool should be treated as provisional and re-verified with every new LLM release, including earlier versions that may resurface in submissions. For publishers and conference organizers, the results imply that static, one-time-trained detectors are operationally inadequate and that screening pipelines require continuous retraining or version-aware routing to maintain both low false-positive rates and high detection coverage. For the broader AI governance community, the paper highlights that the pace of model turnover is itself a structural threat to the reliability of automated content-integrity systems, demanding new evaluation frameworks that explicitly account for version drift.
