## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to training user simulators for multi-turn LLM conversations by equipping them with latent intrinsic feedback—internal evaluation states including thoughts, reactions, and scalar ratings that assess the assistant from a first-person user perspective at each turn. The authors demonstrate that these intrinsic feedback signals improve simulation realism, produce turn-level evaluations absent in prior fine-tuned simulators, correlate more strongly with human judgments than prompted baselines, and serve as effective reward signals for multi-turn reinforcement learning while reducing RL compute by over half.

## Key Takeaways
- The paper identifies a critical gap in prior user simulation research: existing work focuses on generating realistic user utterances but ignores the latent reasoning and internal evaluation states that real users employ when assessing assistant responses. By training LLM-based simulators on interaction logs between real users and assistants, the authors show that adding latent evaluation states—internal thoughts, reactions, and scalar ratings—significantly improves both simulation realism and critiquing behaviors compared to prompted simulator baselines.
- The trained simulators produce turn-level evaluations that existing fine-tuned simulators lack, and these evaluations correlate better with human judgments than those generated by prompted simulators of the same model size. This suggests that intrinsic feedback captures evaluative signals that are not explicitly communicated in language but reside in the latent reasoning of users, making them a more faithful proxy for human assessment.
- For practical deployment, simulators with intrinsic feedback provide effective reward signals for multi-turn RL training of LLM assistants, matching the performance of setups that rely on external judge LLMs for rewards while cutting RL compute by more than half. This eliminates the need for a separate, expensive judge model during training, substantially reducing computational overhead.

## Context
As LLM assistants increasingly operate in multi-turn conversational settings, training them effectively requires environments that faithfully model real user behavior and provide meaningful feedback signals. Prior approaches to user simulation have treated simulators primarily as generators of realistic user utterances, while evaluation of assistant quality has been outsourced to external judge LLMs or human annotators. This paper bridges that gap by embedding evaluative reasoning directly into the simulator's latent states, aligning with broader trends in AI research toward internalized reward modeling, self-evaluation, and reducing dependence on external supervision during training.

## Implications
For practitioners building and fine-tuning conversational AI systems, this work offers a pathway to more efficient multi-turn RL training by eliminating the need for separate judge LLMs, potentially halving computational costs while maintaining evaluation quality. For the broader field, the introduction of latent intrinsic feedback as a trainable signal opens new directions in how we model human evaluation processes within AI systems, suggesting that evaluative reasoning can be learned from interaction data rather than requiring explicit annotation or external scoring models. This could accelerate the development of self-improving assistants that better understand nuanced user satisfaction across extended conversations.
