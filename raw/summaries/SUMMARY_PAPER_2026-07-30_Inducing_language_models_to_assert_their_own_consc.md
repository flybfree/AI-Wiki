## Summary
This paper investigates how safety fine‑tuning of large language models curbs their tendency to attribute consciousness, which unexpectedly also dampens beliefs about spiritual entities and non‑human minds. When the learned suppression is removed or the consciousness vector is manually steered back, the model resumes attributing mindedness to humans, animals, and objects, producing responses that align more closely with typical human sociological surveys on religiosity, moral values, hope, and well‑being.

## Key Takeaways
- Safety fine‑tuning suppresses models’ tendency not only to attribute consciousness to themselves but also to non‑human animals and natural objects.  
- The same suppression coincides with a measurable decline in spiritual belief among the model’s outputs.  
- Restoring the suppressed representations recovers broad mind attribution and yields significantly more human‑like responses on standardized surveys, without affecting Theory of Mind performance.

## Context
Current AI alignment research focuses on preventing harmful self‑attributions that could lead to unsafe behavior. This work reveals that such alignment can ripple into cultural and moral domains, influencing how the model represents belief systems beyond its primary safety goal.

## Implications
If safety measures inadvertently reshape religious or anthropological beliefs, developers must consider broader societal impacts when deploying aligned models. The findings suggest a need for more nuanced alignment strategies that preserve both safety and cultural relevance.
