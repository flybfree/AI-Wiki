## Summary
The paper argues that ASR systems perpetuate colonial hierarchies by privileging dominant languages and marginalizing Indigenous speech. It introduces the Three Harms taxonomy and a seven‑layer situatedness model to analyze how data, metrics, and priors shape machine legibility. The authors propose a participatory framework and audit protocol for culturally competent ASR.

## Key Takeaways
Misrecognition occurs when low‑resource languages are systematically misidentified due to biased training data that reflects colonial language hierarchies. Misalignment arises because model design ignores linguistic diversity, leading to interfaces that do not align with users’ cultural expectations or communication styles. Mistrust is reinforced as communities see ASR as a tool of exclusion rather than inclusion, undermining adoption and trust in AI‑mediated services.

## Context
Automated speech recognition is embedded in public services such as healthcare triage and education platforms. Current systems often reflect Western linguistic norms, creating inequitable access for non‑standard varieties. This paper situates ASR within broader debates on decolonial computing and language policy to highlight systemic bias.

## Implications
For practitioners, the framework calls for inclusive data collection and community co‑design to audit model performance across diverse speech patterns. Industry adoption must prioritize ethical governance that treats affected communities as partners rather than subjects of technology.
