## Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) align with human judgments of Japanese writing when non‑native speakers compose emails in Japanese. The study finds that LLMs reproduce a bias present among native raters: they rate L2 texts lower than L1 texts across fluency, status, and solidarity dimensions, though the magnitude differs from human perceptions.

## Key Takeaways
- Human raters consistently score L2 Japanese emails as less fluent than L1 ones, with the fluency gap about twice that of the gaps for status and solidarity.  
- LLMs mirror this direction but understate the solidarity gap, which is considered the most socially grounded dimension of language attitudes.  
- The models also differentiate evaluations by learner’s first‑language background, a pattern absent in human raters.

## Context
The research highlights that AI systems used for high‑stakes evaluation may embed cultural and linguistic biases that affect non‑native speakers differently from native speakers. This issue is central to the broader effort to ensure fairness and reliability of automated assessment tools across multilingual contexts.

## Implications
For practitioners deploying LLMs in hiring or academic settings, this study suggests a need for regular audits using established language attitudes frameworks to detect subtle biases. Addressing these biases can improve the equitable treatment of non‑native applicants and maintain trust in AI‑driven decision processes.
