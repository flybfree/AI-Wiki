## Summary
This paper presents a scenario-based survey of 289 U.S. adults with prior AI experience, examining how people want to delegate tasks to AI agents across 45 use cases spanning nine life domains. The study finds that automation preferences vary substantially by domain and that delegation is best understood not as a binary choice but as a configuration of execution, collaboration, leadership, and authority, with participants overwhelmingly favoring "bounded autonomy" where AI handles substantial execution while humans retain oversight and final decision-making power.

## Key Takeaways
- Automation preferences are highly domain-dependent: respondents evaluated scenarios across nine distinct life domains and produced markedly different levels of willingness to delegate, indicating that no single delegation policy can apply universally across everyday life and work contexts.
- Trust in the AI agent, a desire for AI leadership, prior task-specific AI use, and anticipated financial savings all predicted greater willingness to automate tasks, whereas concerns that automation would diminish personal fulfillment predicted lower automation preference, revealing a tension between efficiency gains and the psychological value humans place on doing things themselves.
- Open-ended responses revealed a strong preference for bounded autonomy: participants frequently endorsed substantial AI execution of tasks while insisting on retaining human involvement, oversight, or final authority, suggesting that the public does not seek full handoff but rather a carefully calibrated division of labor between human and agent.

## Context
As agentic AI systems move from chat-based assistants to autonomous multi-step task executors, the field lacks empirical grounding for how ordinary users actually want to partition responsibility between themselves and AI. Most prior work on AI delegation has focused on technical capability or safety alignment rather than on lived human preferences across diverse everyday scenarios. This study fills that gap by collecting repeated measures of trust, collaboration desire, leadership preference, privacy concern, and willingness to pay across a broad taxonomy of use cases, providing a behavioral foundation that complements technical and policy discussions about agent autonomy.

## Implications
For AI product designers and deployment teams, these findings argue against one-size-fits-all autonomy settings and instead call for configurable delegation frameworks that let users calibrate execution, collaboration, leadership, and authority on a per-task basis. Policymakers and organizations integrating agentic AI into workplaces should treat personal fulfillment and perceived loss of agency as legitimate adoption barriers, not merely irrational resistance, and should design systems that preserve human oversight as a feature rather than a fallback. The study also highlights that financial savings and prior positive AI experiences are powerful levers for increasing delegation acceptance, which practitioners can leverage in onboarding and trust-building strategies.
