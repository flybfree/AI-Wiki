## Summary
This paper investigates whether frontier vision‑language models exhibit a unified Theory‑of‑Mind profile across different psychology tasks or if their reasoning abilities fragment, producing profiles that differ from human reference groups. The study finds that most models make egocentric errors on the Keysar Director Task without chain‑of‑thought reasoning, while their intention attribution on the Frith‑Happé triangles aligns more closely with high‑functioning autistic adults than typical development adults.

## Key Takeaways
- Without chain‑of‑thought prompting, frontier VLMs replicate childlike egocentric responses on the Director Task at roughly 78 % error rate, indicating a lack of adult‑level perspective taking.  
- The same models under‑attribute intention in the triangle task, placing their ToM scores nearer to high‑functioning autistic adults than typical development adults.  
- No model simultaneously matches human adult profiles on both tasks; the most adult‑like Director performer is also closest to the HF‑ASD mean on triangles.

## Context
Frontier vision‑language models are evaluated for cognitive abilities that mirror human social cognition, a key area of AI research seeking embodied understanding. This work highlights how task design and prompting can dramatically shift model performance, revealing gaps between synthetic reasoning and genuine mental state inference.

## Implications
These findings suggest that current frontier VLMs lack a coherent Theory‑of‑Mind framework, limiting their reliability in applications requiring social reasoning such as assistive robotics or educational tutoring. Researchers should prioritize chain‑of‑thought prompting and task alignment to bridge the gap between synthetic performance and human‑like mental state understanding.
