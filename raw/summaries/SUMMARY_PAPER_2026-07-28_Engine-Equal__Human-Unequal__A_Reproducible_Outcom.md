## Summary
The paper investigates why chess positions that engines deem equal actually lead to outcome skews in human play, and it shows these biases are stable across different player groups, time controls, rating bands, and even eight months later. It finds a strong linear relationship between position skew and the side that is disadvantaged, with slopes as high as 0.94 for popular positions.

## Key Takeaways
- The observed outcome skews persist in human games despite engines judging positions equal, indicating a gap between machine assessment and actual play.
- Skew measures are stable across disjoint player accounts, time controls, rating bands, and even after eight months, showing reproducibility of the bias.
- The linear prediction slope between skew measurements is high (0.69 to 0.94) suggesting that position characteristics strongly influence which side wins.

## Context
This work highlights a persistent discrepancy between AI evaluation and human decision making in chess, challenging assumptions about engine reliability as a predictor of real‑world outcomes. It underscores the need for more nuanced models when interpreting machine judgments in competitive settings.

## Implications
For researchers and practitioners, the findings suggest that future studies must consider both engine confidence and human factors to understand performance. The causal link remains unproven, but the paper calls for a pre‑registered randomised study to test causality.
