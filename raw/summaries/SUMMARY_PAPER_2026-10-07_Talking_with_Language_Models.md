## Summary
This paper introduces the "artifactual stance," a philosophical framework that reconceives human-AI interaction not as genuine conversation but as artifact-mediated exchanges of candidate texts. The authors argue that large language models are sophisticated text generators optimized for utility rather than speakers bearing meaning or communicative force, and that abandoning the conversational framing dissolves numerous philosophical puzzles surrounding AI identity, responsibility, and dialogue.

## Key Takeaways
- LLM outputs are candidate texts optimized for utility, not utterances bearing meaning or illocutionary force. The authors argue that the design of LLM interfaces invites users to treat them as intelligent interlocutors who remember, act, and make commitments, but this appearance is deceptive. Between sessions nothing runs, between turns no one remembers, and what persists is merely a configuration and a transcript. The apparent dialogue is actually a user's solo performance, interpretive labor disguised by interface and artifact design.
- The "artifactual stance" dissolves recent philosophical puzzles about AI by rejecting their false presuppositions. Questions about what "I" and "you" refer to in AI exchanges, whether systems can lie or be held to promises, and the identity of supposed interlocutors all rest on the assumption that there is a speaker behind the screen. Since there is no speaker, there is no one to refer to and no one to hold responsible, rendering these questions moot.
- The philosophical questions that genuinely matter shift from the nature of AI interlocutors to the normative underpinnings of design, adoption, authorization, and human practices of use. Rather than asking whether an LLM can be a genuine conversational partner, we should interrogate the ethical and design decisions that shape how these artifacts are built, deployed, and integrated into human workflows.

## Context
This paper enters a crowded philosophical debate about whether LLMs constitute genuine agents, speakers, or conversational partners, a debate that has intensified as models become more fluent and anthropomorphized in their interfaces. By positioning LLMs squarely as artifacts rather than interlocutors, the authors challenge a growing consensus in philosophy of language and AI ethics that treats model outputs as speech acts requiring truthfulness, accountability, and referential commitment. The work matters because it reframes the terms of engagement for both academic philosophy and public discourse about AI capabilities.

## Implications
For practitioners and industry, the artifactual stance suggests that design choices around anthropomorphic interfaces, persistent memory features, and conversational framing carry normative weight that should be scrutinized rather than taken for granted. For researchers and policymakers, the paper redirects regulatory and ethical attention away from questions of AI personhood or deception toward the more tractable questions of who authorizes these systems, how they are adopted, and what human practices of use they enable or distort. This reorientation could influence how organizations evaluate AI tools, how liability frameworks are constructed, and how users are educated about the nature of their interactions with language models.
