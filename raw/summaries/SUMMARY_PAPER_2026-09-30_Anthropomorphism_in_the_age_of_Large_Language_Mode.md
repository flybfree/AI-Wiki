## Summary
This paper synthesizes recent literature on the tendency to attribute human-like characteristics to Large Language Models and other AI systems, examining both the psychological drivers behind this phenomenon and its broader consequences. The authors develop a comprehensive taxonomy categorizing twenty-one distinct risks into epistemic, affective, human agency, normative, and societal domains. Ultimately, they argue that mitigating these risks requires aligning public communication and system design with the actual technical architecture and capabilities of AI rather than relying on anthropomorphic metaphors.

## Key Takeaways
- The authors explore why humans naturally project human traits onto AI systems and demonstrate how linguistic framing significantly amplifies or dampens these perceptions, making language a critical factor in shaping public understanding and societal expectations.
- A conceptual taxonomy is introduced that organizes twenty-one specific concerns into five analytical categories: epistemic (misunderstanding technical capabilities), affective (emotional manipulation or unhealthy attachment), human agency (erosion of personal autonomy), normative (shifting moral expectations), and societal/institutional risks (systemic bias or accountability gaps).
- Mitigation strategies are mapped across four intervention domains—design, communication, education, and governance—with the central thesis that scientific descriptions must be grounded in AI’s actual organizational structure to prevent misleading anthropomorphic narratives from dictating policy and social roles.

## Context
As conversational AI systems become increasingly integrated into daily life, the line between functional tools and perceived social agents continues to blur across consumer, educational, and professional settings. This paper addresses a growing gap in AI ethics literature by systematically cataloging how human-like projections can distort technical understanding and influence societal expectations. By bridging cognitive science, linguistics, and AI safety research, it provides a structured framework for analyzing a phenomenon that has largely been discussed anecdotally or in isolation across disciplines.

## Implications
Practitioners and developers must prioritize transparent system design and precise communication protocols to prevent unintended psychological dependencies among users while maintaining functional utility. Policymakers can leverage the proposed risk taxonomy to draft targeted regulations that address accountability, user protection, and institutional oversight without stifling technological innovation. Ultimately, fostering a scientifically grounded public discourse around AI capabilities will be essential for aligning deployment practices with ethical standards and long-term societal well-being.
