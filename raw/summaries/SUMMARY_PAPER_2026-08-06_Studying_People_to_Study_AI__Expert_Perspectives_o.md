## Summary
The paper surveys AI safety and ethics researchers to understand why human research is undervalued despite its evidential value for AI risk assessment. It finds that while the importance of human studies is recognized, adoption is limited by concerns about validity, resource constraints, methodological preferences, and infrastructure gaps. The authors recommend improving epistemic fit between human research and AI safety work.

## Key Takeaways
- Human research is seen as valuable but its acceptance is hindered by perceived lack of scientific rigor and difficulty proving relevance to technical AI safety problems.
- Researchers cite tangible barriers such as limited funding, time allocation, and the need for specialized tools that are not widely available across disciplines.
- There is a clear epistemic tension between technical researchers who favor computational benchmarks and those who prioritize human‑centered studies.

## Context
AI safety research increasingly relies on simulations and benchmarking to predict risks, yet these methods cannot capture real‑world user behavior or societal impacts. Human studies provide the only way to observe how people actually interact with AI systems in diverse contexts.

## Implications
Practitioners must recognize that human data is essential for robust safety assessments and should allocate resources accordingly. Bridging methodological gaps will lead to more trustworthy AI governance frameworks and prevent superficial “human‑washing” of technical solutions.
