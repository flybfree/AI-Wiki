## Summary
This paper investigates how civil society organizations in Germany monitor far‑right activity online and why they struggle to adopt technical tools. The authors find that monitoring is largely manual, with a strong demand for better search capabilities, while participants are cautious about AI‑driven classification due to trust and legal concerns.

## Key Takeaways
- Monitoring remains largely manual because existing tools lack tailored features such as enhanced search functionality.
- Practitioners express openness to AI support for media processing but remain skeptical of automated content classification because it threatens professional credibility and legal usability.
- The study introduces the “manual labor trap” concept, explaining why CSOs stay in low‑capacity arrangements despite clear technical needs.

## Context
The rapid rise of far‑right ideologies on digital platforms has created a gap between emerging AI technologies and the limited resources available to grassroots monitors. This research highlights that while many stakeholders focus on platform moderation, civil society actors are often excluded from co‑designing solutions that suit their operational realities.

## Implications
For policymakers and industry partners, the findings suggest a need for open‑source, flexible tools that respect legal frameworks and professional standards. Empirical evidence of the “manual labor trap” underscores the urgency to design interventions that reduce reliance on exhaustive manual work while preserving trust in monitoring outcomes.
