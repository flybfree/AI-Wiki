## Summary
The paper investigates how a transformer’s answer can be generated by keeping its internal representations off‑axis from the read‑out axis, rather than forcing them onto it early. It demonstrates that a 12‑layer model can compute concepts in two phases: first accumulating content in a subspace orthogonal to the output, then adding the result at the end. This approach preserves concept quality while reducing effective dimensions.

## Key Takeaways
- The transformer’s intermediate states occupy a subspace roughly 75–96 degrees off the read‑out axis, and this off‑axis geometry is functional rather than obstructive.  
- Moving attention values onto the read‑out causes up to 84 times more damage than random rotation, primarily through cross‑token mixing that isolates composition from vocabulary.  
- Prescribing a fixed rotation at the phase boundary yields baseline quality across all seeds, whereas forcing the model to null its projection leads to collapse.

## Context
This work challenges the prevailing view that off‑axis representations hinder interpretability in transformers. By showing that such geometry can be harnessed for concept computation, it aligns with recent efforts to understand internal states without altering training objectives.

## Implications
For practitioners, this suggests that architectural flexibility—allowing off‑axis subspaces—can maintain performance while reducing model complexity. It also hints at new strategies for early exit and sparse rotation that could improve efficiency in large language models.
