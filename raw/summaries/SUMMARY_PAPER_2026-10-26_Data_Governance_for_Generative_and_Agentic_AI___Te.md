## Summary
This book presents a comprehensive, unified governance framework for generative and agentic AI systems, spanning from large language models and retrieval-augmented generation pipelines to autonomous multi-agent systems. Through eleven chapters and eight appendices, it integrates three disciplinary lenses—technology, law, and economics—across the entire data lifecycle, from data quality and provenance to the design of a governance control plane envisioned for 2030. The work is anchored in Indian law and institutions while maintaining a comparative, cross-jurisdictional perspective covering six regulatory jurisdictions.

## Key Takeaways
- The book constructs a full-lifecycle governance framework that addresses data quality, lineage, provenance, intellectual property, and digital trust alongside cybersecurity and zero-trust architecture, recognizing that generative and agentic AI systems require governance that spans technology, law, and economics simultaneously rather than treating these as siloed concerns.
- It provides detailed treatment of governing generative AI pipelines and agentic AI multi-agent systems, extending governance beyond single-model deployment to complex autonomous systems that retrieve, generate, infer, remember, and act on information, including their implications for elections and the public sphere.
- The work maps AI regulation across six jurisdictions and proposes a forward-looking governance control plane for 2030, grounding its analysis in Indian law and institutions while maintaining comparative cross-jurisdictional rigor, offering a model for how emerging economies can shape AI governance standards.

## Context
As generative AI and agentic systems move from experimental prototypes into production deployments across public and private sectors, existing regulatory frameworks remain fragmented and jurisdictionally inconsistent. This book addresses a critical gap in the literature by unifying technical, legal, and economic perspectives into a single governance architecture, particularly for systems that combine retrieval, generation, reasoning, and autonomous action. Its emphasis on Indian law and institutions contributes a perspective that is underrepresented in mainstream AI governance scholarship, which has historically centered on EU and US regulatory frameworks.

## Implications
For policymakers, regulators, and institutional leaders, this work offers a structured blueprint for designing governance control planes that can keep pace with the rapid evolution of agentic AI systems, including their impact on democratic processes such as elections. For industry practitioners and AI developers, the integrated treatment of data provenance, zero-trust security, and multi-agent governance provides actionable guidance for building compliant and trustworthy AI pipelines across multiple jurisdictions. The proposed 2030 governance control plane signals a shift toward proactive, architecture-level governance rather than reactive compliance, which will be essential as autonomous AI systems become embedded in critical infrastructure and public decision-making.
