## Summary
The study investigates whether conversational interactions with a large language model can mitigate belief in newly emerging conspiracy theories after high‑profile events such as the July 2024 assassination attempt on Donald Trump and the September 2025 attack on Charlie Kirk. It reports that participants who engaged in multi‑turn dialogues with an LLM prompted to counter conspiratorial thinking showed significantly lower levels of conspiracy belief compared to control groups, and that this reduction persisted for up to two months as other conspiracies unfolded.

## Key Takeaways
- Participants who conversed with the LLM displayed a statistically significant drop in their conviction about the immediate crisis conspiracy.  
- The effect was observed across two independent experiments, one with 472 participants and another with 1035, indicating robustness.  
- Later surveys revealed that the reduced belief transferred to subsequent conspiracies, suggesting lasting cognitive impact.

## Context
This work contributes to AI‑driven mental health and misinformation mitigation research by demonstrating a scalable conversational intervention that leverages natural language generation to counteract rapidly spreading false narratives.

## Implications
For practitioners, the findings suggest that deploying LLMs in crisis communication could be an effective tool for shaping public perception before misinformation solidifies. It also underscores the need for ethical safeguards to ensure dialogues remain truthful and do not inadvertently amplify harmful narratives.
