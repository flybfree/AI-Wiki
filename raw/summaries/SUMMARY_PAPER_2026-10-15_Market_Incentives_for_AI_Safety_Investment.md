## Summary
This paper by Nikita Tsoy develops a game-theoretic model of an AI supply chain consisting of an upstream large language model provider and multiple competing downstream firms to analyze how profit-maximization incentives shape safety investment decisions. The central finding is that downstream competition tends to push firms toward investing more in safety than what would maximize industry-wide profit, while the upstream provider systematically underinvests because it cannot capture the full surplus generated by its safety improvements. The paper further examines how various regulatory interventions alter these incentive structures and the resulting levels of model safety.

## Key Takeaways
- Downstream competition creates a divergence between individual firm incentives and industry-optimal safety levels: competing downstream firms invest more in safety than the level that would maximize total industry profit, suggesting that market competition can partially correct underinvestment in safety through reputational and differentiation pressures.
- The upstream LLM provider faces a structural underinvestment problem because safety improvements generate positive externalities across the downstream ecosystem, yet the provider is not fully compensated for the surplus those improvements create, leading to a market failure where the provider's private incentive to invest in safety falls below both the market-welfare-optimal and industry-profit-optimal benchmarks.
- Regulatory interventions can reshape the incentive landscape for both upstream and downstream participants, altering their profit structures and the equilibrium level of model safety, which means that the design of AI safety regulation must account for the supply-chain structure and the differential incentives faced by providers versus deployers.

## Context
As AI systems become embedded across industries through supply chains of model providers and downstream application developers, the question of who bears the cost of safety investment and who captures its benefits has become central to AI governance. This paper contributes to the growing intersection of industrial organization economics and AI safety research by formalizing the strategic interactions between upstream and downstream actors, moving beyond purely technical or normative discussions of safety toward a market-structure analysis. It matters because most existing AI safety frameworks assume a single developer-deployer relationship, whereas the real-world ecosystem involves complex multi-layer supply chains with misaligned incentives.

## Implications
For policymakers and regulators, the findings suggest that uniform safety mandates may be insufficient or misdirected without accounting for the structural asymmetry between upstream providers and downstream firms, and that interventions targeting the upstream provider's compensation for safety externalities could be more effective than those targeting downstream actors. For industry practitioners and investors, the analysis highlights that competitive dynamics in downstream markets can create unexpected safety investment pressures, meaning that strategic positioning in the AI supply chain carries safety-related costs and benefits that firms should anticipate in their business planning.
