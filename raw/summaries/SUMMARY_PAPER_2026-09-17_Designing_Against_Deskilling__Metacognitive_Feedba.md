## Summary
This research investigates methods to mitigate "deskilling"—the erosion of human skills caused by over-reliance on AI—while maintaining access to LLM assistants for productivity and problem-solving. The study specifically evaluates whether metacognitive feedback, which makes the implications of offloading explicit, can effectively reduce reliance on AI while improving long-term skill retention compared to effort-based rewards.

## Key Takeaways
- The researchers identified a critical tension in human-AI interaction: while LLM assistants provide immediate assistance and convenience, they often remove the "desirable difficulties" necessary for humans to practice and retain complex skills like fraction arithmetic.
- Two distinct interventions were tested in a large-scale online experiment: metacognitive feedback (which makes the consequences of offloading explicit) and an effort-based reward system designed to incentivize manual work over AI assistance.
- The results demonstrated that metacognitive feedback significantly reduced the frequency of offloading decisions (OR = 0.47) and led to a measurable improvement in unaided test performance (OR = 1.51). Conversely, the effort-based reward showed no significant impact on either metric, suggesting that awareness of consequences is more effective than external rewards for preventing deskilling.

## Context
As LLM tools become ubiquitous in education and professional environments, there is a growing concern regarding "cognitive atrophy," where users lose the ability to perform tasks independently because they rely on AI to do the heavy lifting. This paper matters because it shifts the focus from simply restricting AI access toward designing smarter interfaces that preserve human competence during the learning process.

## Implications
For developers and designers, these findings suggest that creating "transparent" interfaces—those that highlight the trade-offs between immediate assistance and long-term skill retention—is a more effective strategy than trying to gamify effort. Practitioners can use these insights to build educational tools that encourage active learning by making the cost of offloading visible to the user during complex tasks, thereby ensuring that AI acts as a scaffold rather than a replacement for human cognition.
