## Summary
The paper investigates AI journaling nudges using passive sensing and an LLM that labels each entry as expressing an intention to change a behavior or not, then measures follow‑through against 26 sensor features over three days. It finds that responsiveness depends most on whether the behavior involves other people: self‑directed behaviors improve up to 50–63% while interpersonal ones only 15–22%. Writing style mattered less; no single text feature separates improved from unimproved entries.

## Key Takeaways
- Responsiveness is higher for self‑directed behaviors, improving up to 50‑63%, compared with only 15‑22% improvement for behaviors that involve others.  
- No single text feature separates improved from unimproved entries; the signal emerges within specific behaviors, most clearly for longer, more personal intention entries.  
- The sample is small and exploratory, so these patterns should be treated as hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions.

## Context
AI journaling tools aim to personalize prompts based on sensed behavior, but their effectiveness remains uncertain. This study provides empirical evidence that nudges work better when they target actions individuals can control alone, highlighting a gap in current approaches.

## Implications
Practitioners should design AI journaling features focusing on self‑directed goals and longer, personal entries rather than interpersonal ones. The findings suggest future research validate these patterns with larger datasets to guide more effective nudging strategies.
