## Summary
the paper compares human annotations with those produced by large language models on evaluative language in TED talks. it finds that LLMs outperform trained linguists and achieve an F1 score of 0.77 after fine‑tuning.

## Key Takeaways
- Human-trained linguists show low agreement scores, indicating difficulty with complex appraisal categories such as affect, judgement, and appreciation.
- LLMs can classify these categories more accurately than human annotators when using the best prompt, reaching an F1 of 0.77 after fine‑tuning.
- The study demonstrates that automatic classification via prompts yields performance comparable to expert annotation despite the subjective nature of appraisal theory.

## Context
this work highlights a gap between human expertise and machine capability in handling nuanced linguistic phenomena, which is central to digital humanities where precise annotation drives analysis. it underscores the need for systematic evaluation of LLM outputs as alternatives to manual labeling.

## Implications
for researchers, the findings suggest that LLMs can serve as reliable annotators for complex appraisal tasks, reducing labor costs and enabling large‑scale studies. practitioners should consider integrating prompt‑based classification into their annotation pipelines to improve efficiency and consistency.
