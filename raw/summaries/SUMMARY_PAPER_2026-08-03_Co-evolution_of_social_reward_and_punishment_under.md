## Summary
This paper examines how peer and institutional incentives jointly shape the evolution of cooperation, social welfare, and enforcement efficiency in a four‑strategy Prisoners’ Dilemma model. It finds that peer punishment strongly promotes cooperation while peer reward benefits overall social welfare, and that institutional rewards to peer incentive strategies improve both outcomes whereas subsidising unconditional cooperators has little effect.

## Key Takeaways
- Peer punishment is the most effective mechanism for fostering cooperative behavior in the population dynamics.
- Institutional rewards directed at peer‑based incentives markedly raise cooperation levels and aggregate welfare without harming decentralised enforcement.
- Directly penalising defectors remains the only consistently effective institutional policy, whereas punishing incentive strategies undermines both cooperation and social welfare.

## Context
The study contributes to AI research on multi‑agent systems by providing a quantitative framework for evaluating how different reward structures affect long‑term cooperation in strategic games. It aligns with ongoing efforts to design agents that cooperate while maximizing collective utility, a core concern in cooperative AI and game theory applications.

## Implications
For practitioners developing autonomous agents or social platforms, the findings suggest that aligning incentives with peer punishment can boost cooperation but may sacrifice overall welfare if it eliminates decentralised enforcement. Institutions should therefore balance these trade‑offs to achieve both robust cooperation and high societal benefit.
