## Summary
The paper introduces a collective-centric evaluation framework for Preference Inference models used in participatory democracy platforms like Polis and Remesh. It demonstrates that even models with similar accuracy can reshape the broader preference landscape differently, showing that accuracy alone is insufficient for assessing AI impact on democratic outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- The study shows that PI models can artificially amplify or suppress existing support patterns, altering how consensus and conflict are perceived at scale.  
- Comparative experiments reveal that predictive accuracy does not guarantee preservation of the collective preference structure across languages and participants.  
- A large multilingual dataset spanning 90k participants, 1M votes, and 22 languages provides a benchmark for evaluating PI methods in diverse democratic settings.

## Context
This work addresses a growing need for AI systems that support large‑scale online deliberation without distorting collective preferences. In the broader AI community, user‑centric metrics often overlook systemic effects on group dynamics, making this evaluation approach novel and timely.

## Implications
For policymakers and platform designers, the findings warn against relying solely on prediction accuracy when deploying AI in democratic processes. Practitioners must adopt collective‑centric benchmarks to ensure that AI enhances rather than undermines the integrity of participatory outcomes.
