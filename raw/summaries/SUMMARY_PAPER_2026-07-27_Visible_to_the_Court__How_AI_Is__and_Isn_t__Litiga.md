## Summary
This paper conducts a systematic review of 559 U.S. federal court opinions where artificial intelligence is central to the parties’ disputes, categorizing the topics, technologies, and litigants involved. The analysis reveals seven recurring dispute areas, six AI technology categories, four common plaintiff or defendant types, and highlights that courts rely on existing legal doctrines rather than creating new AI‑specific rules.

## Key Takeaways
- Courts primarily apply pre‑existing statutes to resolve AI harms, leaving many AI‑related risks uncaught because they do not fit current legal definitions.  
- The taxonomy of litigated AI cases shows a narrow focus on specific technologies such as machine learning models and data analytics, while broader societal impacts are rarely addressed.  
- Federal court outcomes are shaped more by which harms can be framed under existing statutes than by the actual occurrence or severity of AI incidents.

## Context
The rapid deployment of AI across industries has created a complex regulatory vacuum in the United States, where federal courts have become one venue for scrutinizing its use. This study contributes to that conversation by empirically mapping how AI is treated within the judicial system, offering insight into gaps between documented risks and court‑recognizable harms.

## Implications
For policymakers, the findings suggest a need for clearer legal frameworks that can accommodate novel AI harms rather than forcing existing statutes to cover them. Practitioners should anticipate that litigation will continue to hinge on how plaintiffs or defendants align their claims with current legal doctrines, potentially limiting innovative solutions to those that fit within established categories.
