## Summary
The paper examines how autonomous AI agents driven by large language models transform offensive security practices. It identifies three dimensions of indeterminacy—non‑deterministic policy outputs, open‑ended impact, and a lowered skill floor—and argues that these create moral diffusion between users, developers, and third parties.

## Key Takeaways
- The non‑deterministic policy of LLM agents makes it impossible to attribute specific actions or explain outcomes, complicating incident analysis and safety reviews.
- Because the impact is open‑ended and supply chains are opaque, the technology can be used in ways that extend beyond intended defensive use, raising dual‑use concerns.
- The steep drop in required skill means a broader user base can deploy offensive capabilities, democratizing offense despite existing asymmetry between offense and defense.

## Context
Autonomous AI agents blur the line between tool and actor, challenging traditional cybersecurity frameworks built around deterministic tools. This shift forces a reevaluation of ethical responsibility when malicious intent is embedded within open‑source models.

## Implications
Practitioners must adopt new governance models that clarify attribution across stakeholders. The paper’s recommendations aim to balance innovation with accountability in an era where offensive AI is increasingly accessible.
