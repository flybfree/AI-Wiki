## Summary
This paper investigates how competitive pressure in an idealised AI race can drive unsafe development, using a behavioural experiment where participants repeatedly chose between safe and unsafe actions under varying private risk levels. The results show that the choice to adopt unsafe strategies is driven more by evolving strategic dynamics than by individual risk preferences, with early behaviour and opponent moves influencing later decisions.

## Key Takeaways
- Unsafe choices increase when a participant falls behind their opponent, suggesting competitive momentum shapes behaviour rather than personal risk tolerance.
- First‑round decisions predict subsequent actions, indicating that the race’s state creates a feedback loop of unsafe play.
- The model reproduces treatment effects with four strategies, highlighting how conditional unsafe behaviour can be favoured by competition.

## Context
The experiment mirrors real‑world debates where rapid AI advancement may sacrifice safety for speed. By isolating risk levels and competitive structure, it clarifies that the tension between speed and safety is not merely a matter of individual caution but also of strategic interaction within a race environment.

## Implications
Policymakers should consider reducing competitive pressure rather than solely focusing on individual risk aversion to foster safer AI development. Encouraging cooperation can mitigate the emergence of unsafe practices driven by fear of falling behind.
