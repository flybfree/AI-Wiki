## Summary
The paper introduces SILICA, an open instrument that tests whether large language model agents behave like humans, whether findings are robust to apparatus changes, and whether observed dynamics stem from genuine interaction or memorized results. Experiments across five environments with human anchors show limited agreement: only eight of eleven models match starting‑point contributions, none match end‑state outcomes, and cooperation collapses under simple ordering manipulations.

## Key Takeaways
- Agreement with human data is confined to first‑round public‑goods contributions for eight of eleven models, indicating a narrow equivalence margin.  
- No model matches human end‑state contributions or the corridor of cooperation, showing that long‑term dynamics are not reproduced.  
- A single ordering change reduces one model’s cooperation score by 58 points, revealing sensitivity to superficial rule presentation.

## Context
These findings highlight a gap between simulated social behavior and real human interaction in AI societies, underscoring how easily emergent patterns can be artifacts of experimental design rather than genuine agency. The results challenge the assumption that large language models can reliably model complex cooperative dynamics without explicit training.

## Implications
For researchers, SILICA suggests that current silicon societies support exploratory claims but lack robust validation against human benchmarks. Practitioners should treat such simulations as hypothesis generators, not definitive evidence of social intelligence, and consider additional verification steps before deploying AI agents in collaborative environments.
