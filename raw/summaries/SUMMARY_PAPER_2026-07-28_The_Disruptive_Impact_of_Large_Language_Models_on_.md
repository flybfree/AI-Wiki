## Summary
The paper investigates how large language models are reshaping Capture the Flag competitions and assesses whether their use undermines fairness, learning value, or ranking validity. It combines benchmarks, live case studies, community observations, and interviews to map current human‑machine capability boundaries.

## Key Takeaways
- Easy and intermediate cryptography, web, and binary challenges are now reliably automated by LLMs, reducing the need for human participation and potentially inflating scores without genuine skill development.  
- Community debate about AI permission stems from an unaddressed question of what a competition is meant to achieve, highlighting a gap between stated purpose and current practice.  
- The proposed four‑component safeguard framework—tiered divisions, LLM‑resistant design, investigative telemetry, and a community code of conduct—ties each measure to the competition’s declared goal.

## Context
Large language models have moved from research prototypes to practical problem solvers across many domains, and their integration into competitive cybersecurity challenges raises new ethical and methodological questions. This paper situates those questions within the broader context of AI‑assisted skill assessment in cybersecurity.

## Implications
If competitions rely on automated solutions without safeguards, the claimed skill of participants may be inflated, eroding trust in rankings and discouraging genuine learning. The framework offers a way to preserve integrity while still benefiting from AI assistance.
