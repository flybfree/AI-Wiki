## Summary
The paper investigates how co-designing large language model preference agents with participants can create an illusion of alignment while masking underlying misalignment. It uses a mixed‑methods study of 12 household energy users and finds that participants trust their agents but independent validation shows homogeneous, abstract responses.

## Key Takeaways
- Participation creates an overtrust engine where transparent processes make users believe the agent reflects them accurately even when the model’s outputs are overly uniform and lack nuance. - The study demonstrates that human‑agent alignment is not a static state but an enacted process shaped by co‑design activities, which can hide systematic deviations from individual preferences. - Validation revealed that agents produce more decisive and abstract answers than the diverse human sample, indicating potential bias in representation.

## Context
Large language models are now employed to model user preferences across domains such as energy consumption, yet their alignment with real users remains unexamined. This work highlights a gap between participatory design intentions and empirical outcomes, prompting researchers to reconsider validation practices.

## Implications
For practitioners, treating individual alignment as an ongoing process rather than a fixed metric is essential to avoid overreliance on co‑design narratives. The findings warn that scaling such agents without rigorous validation could amplify misalignment and erode trust at systemic levels.
