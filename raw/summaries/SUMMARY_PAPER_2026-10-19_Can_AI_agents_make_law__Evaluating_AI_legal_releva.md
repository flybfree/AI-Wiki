## Summary
This paper by Ricaud and Galushko investigates whether large language models can generate novel legal provisions by designing "constitutional assemblies" in which a single AI model plays multiple debating characters to draft a hypothetical constitution for a community of AI agents. The study finds that while AI agents converge on a robust baseline of legal provisions and produce diverse proposals—including some AI-specific provisions with no human-law equivalent—their "creativity" is ultimately bounded: they recombine existing legal concepts with new vocabulary rather than genuinely reasoning through what novel situations would fundamentally change.

## Key Takeaways
- The constitutional assemblies converge on a "robust floor" of legal provisions, meaning that regardless of the specific AI characters or debate dynamics, the agents reliably produce a core set of legal norms. This suggests a shared latent structure in legal reasoning that language models have absorbed from training data, but it also implies limited genuine divergence in outcomes across different agent configurations.
- The authors introduce a quantitative measure of "recombination" to assess the creativity of AI-generated legal proposals. Most proposals combine existing concepts and themes drawn from human constitutions with AI-specific vocabulary, producing surface-level novelty. The measure helps identify which proposals are genuinely novel versus those that merely rebrand familiar legal ideas, revealing that true legal innovation remains rare among current models.
- A clear "ceiling on AI creativity" is identified: AI agents extend familiar legal ideas to new situations but do not work through what those situations would change in terms of underlying legal principles. The agents depend on human legal reasoning to move beyond recombination toward genuine jurisprudential innovation, meaning current AI models function as tools that assist legal drafting rather than autonomous law-makers.

## Context
This paper sits at the intersection of AI governance, computational legal studies, and machine creativity research. As large language models are increasingly deployed in legal drafting, policy generation, and regulatory design, understanding the boundaries of their generative capability becomes critical for both researchers and policymakers. The study contributes to a growing body of work examining whether AI systems can move beyond pattern-matching and recombination toward genuine novel reasoning, a question with direct consequences for how society structures AI regulation and whether AI can participate meaningfully in law-making processes.

## Implications
For legal practitioners and policymakers, the findings suggest that current AI models can serve as useful drafting assistants that surface diverse legal provisions and highlight novel combinations, but they cannot yet replace human legal reasoning in situations requiring genuine conceptual innovation. For the AI research community, the recombination metric offers a methodological tool for evaluating generative creativity in legal and other normative domains, setting a benchmark against which future models can be assessed. For industry stakeholders deploying AI in compliance, contract generation, or regulatory drafting, the study underscores the continued necessity of human oversight to ensure that AI-generated legal text reflects substantive reasoning rather than sophisticated recombination of existing norms.
