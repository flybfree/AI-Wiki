## Summary
This paper challenges the assumption that statistical regularities in undeciphered scripts uniquely indicate language. Using a synthetic emblem system called SIGIL with explicit compositional meanings, it shows that many traditional measures of Indus script similarity reproduce across criteria and even generate high dictionary coverage for unrelated languages.

## Key Takeaways
- The same statistical signatures that label the Indus corpus also appear in SIGIL, indicating these measures detect organization without requiring language specificity.  
- Reproducing published outcomes with source‑defined decision rules demonstrates that many reported results are not uniquely unusual among plausible non‑languages.  
- High dictionary coverage on held‑out groups does not reliably identify the true encoded language, revealing limited discriminative power of these measures.

## Context
In AI research on decipherment and pattern recognition, distinguishing structured systems from random noise is a central challenge. This work provides empirical evidence that many statistical tools lack specificity to actual linguistic content, prompting reevaluation of their reliability in real‑world script analysis.

## Implications
For linguists and computational archaeologists, the findings caution against overreliance on statistical regularities as proof of language encoding. Practitioners should supplement such metrics with contextual and comparative evidence before concluding that a sign system encodes speech.
