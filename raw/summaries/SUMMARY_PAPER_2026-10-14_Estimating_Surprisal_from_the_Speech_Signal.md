## Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental limitation in surprisal theory research: the reliance on written text and transcriptions to estimate surprisal, which creates a "text bottleneck" that prevents evaluation of the theory in the spoken modality. The authors introduce an importance sampling estimator that approximates the probability of abstract linguistic units by marginalizing over acoustic realizations generated by a text-to-speech model, enabling surprisal estimation directly from speech signals. Using pupillometry data from a listening study, they find that importance-sampled surprisal better predicts pupil dilation than realization-specific surprisal for words, though surprisal overall remains a weak predictor of pupil size changes regardless of its source.

## Key Takeaways
- The paper removes the text bottleneck in surprisal estimation by training speech language models and estimating surprisal directly from acoustic signals, rather than relying on transcriptions processed by text-based LMs. This is a methodological breakthrough because it allows surprisal theory to be tested in its natural spoken modality for the first time.
- A central theoretical question is raised: surprisal should be defined over abstract linguistic units rather than realization-specific acoustic inputs, since a single word can have many distinct acoustic realizations each assigned different probabilities by a model. The importance sampling estimator addresses this by marginalizing over realizations generated by a text-to-speech model, yielding a more linguistically meaningful surprisal value.
- Empirical results from pupillometry show that importance-sampled surprisal consistently outperforms realization-specific surprisal in predicting pupil dilation at the word level, and that sub-lexical units also show predictive value. However, the authors honestly report that surprisal as a whole is not a strong predictor of pupil size changes, tempering claims about the theory's explanatory power even in this new text-free setting.

## Context
Surprisal theory, rooted in information-theoretic models of language processing, has been extensively validated using reading-time data and text-based language models. This paper represents a critical step toward extending those findings to spoken language comprehension, a domain where the vast majority of human language use actually occurs. By leveraging speech language models and text-to-speech generation within an importance sampling framework, the work bridges computational linguistics, psycholinguistics, and speech technology in a way that prior text-only approaches could not.

## Implications
For researchers in psycholinguistics and cognitive science, this work provides a new experimental paradigm for testing surprisal theory without transcription-mediated confounds, potentially revealing how real-time spoken comprehension differs from reading. For practitioners in speech technology and NLP, the importance sampling approach offers a reusable technique for evaluating model outputs against human behavioral data in speech settings. However, the finding that surprisal remains a weak predictor of pupil dilation regardless of estimation method suggests that the field must look beyond surprisal alone to model the full complexity of spoken language processing effort.
